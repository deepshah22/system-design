Authentication: OAuth 2.0 & JWT
The Wristband and the Valet Key

Yesterday's gate guard asked the sharpest question in security: "who are you?" Today we answer it at internet scale — across dozens of services and across companies — with the two technologies hiding behind nearly every "Sign in with Google" button and every modern API. The first is the JSON Web Token (JWT): a tamper-proof, self-contained festival wristband — a signed claim about who you are that any service can check on the spot, without phoning a central database. The signatures you learned yesterday are exactly what make it trustworthy. The second is OAuth 2.0: a valet key — a protocol that lets one app act on your behalf inside another without ever seeing your password, by handing it a limited, expiring, revocable token. We'll separate stateful sessions from stateless tokens and weigh their tradeoffs, crack open a JWT's three parts (and learn the dangerous truth that it's signed but not encrypted), walk the four roles and the authorization-code flow of OAuth, clear up the single most common confusion in the field (OAuth is authorization, not authentication — and how OpenID Connect fixes that), meet access and refresh tokens, confront the hard revocation problem, and learn where to store a token safely. By the end, that little login button will feel less like magic and more like a wristband and a valet key.

Picture this

Picture a huge music festival. At the entrance you prove who you are once — ID, ticket, payment — and in return they snap a wristband onto your arm. That wristband is a JWT. Notice what's clever about it. It's self-contained: printed right on it is who you are and what you're allowed — "GA", "VIP", "backstage until 11pm". So the guard at the beer tent, the guard at the VIP lounge, and the guard at the backstage door can each glance at your wrist and decide instantly — none of them has to radio the box office to look you up. It carries a tamper-evident seal (a hologram the festival stamped on — yesterday's digital signature): if you try to color in an extra stripe to upgrade yourself to VIP, the hologram doesn't match and every guard spots the fake. And it expires — the band is for today only; tomorrow it's worthless. That's the whole idea of a token: prove who you are once, carry a signed, expiring badge, and let anyone verify it on the spot without a central lookup.

Now the second idea. Your friend wants to pull the photos off your phone to make a slideshow — but you are not going to hand over your phone's unlock PIN. Instead you drop your car at the hotel and give the valet a special key: it starts the car and opens the driver's door, but not the glovebox or the trunk, and you can cancel it the moment you leave. That valet key is OAuth 2.0. When a photo-printing app wants your Google Photos, you don't give it your Google password (your master key). Google hands the app a valet key — a token scoped to "read photos only", that expires, and that you can revoke — and the app never once sees your password. Burn in the image: a JWT is a signed festival wristband anyone can check on sight; OAuth is a scoped, revocable valet key that lets an app act for you without your master password.

The Story

Password-Sharing Pat built a friendly app that made birthday collages from your photos. In the early days it had one deeply alarming feature: to find your pictures, it asked you to type your Google email and password directly into its login box. Users did it — and in that one act they handed Pat's app the keys to their entire Google account: email, contacts, documents, everything. If Pat's servers were ever breached, every one of those passwords leaked. If a user wanted to revoke access, their only option was to change their Google password everywhere. This was the bad old world before OAuth, and it broke the most basic rule from yesterday: never give your master key to a stranger to do one small job.

The fix was OAuth 2.0, and it felt like magic the first time. Now when you click "Connect Google Photos", Pat's app redirects you to Google itself. You type your password only into Google's page — never Pat's — and Google asks: "Birthday Collage wants to view your photos. Allow?" You click Allow, Google sends Pat's app back a short code, and the app trades that code for an access token: a valet key scoped to read photos only. Pat's app never learned your password, can never read your email, and you can march into your Google security settings and revoke that one app's access with a single click, without disturbing anything else. Delegated access, done safely.

Pat's second headache was on the inside. As the app grew into a dozen microservices (photos, billing, notifications, exports…), every single service needed to answer "who is this request from, and may they do this?" At first each service, on every request, looked up a session ID in a central database to find the user. That central session store became a bottleneck and a single point of failure — every request, from every service, hammering one table.

The fix was the JWT. After you log in once, Pat's auth service hands your browser a signed token that already contains your user id and roles. Every later request carries that token, and each microservice can verify the signature locally — using yesterday's public-key math — and read who you are straight off the token. No database lookup. No central bottleneck. The wristband carries its own proof. Pat learned the two lessons every engineer eventually learns: let apps act on your behalf with scoped, revocable tokens — never your password (OAuth); and carry identity in a signed, self-verifying token so services don't all depend on one lookup (JWT). But he also learned the catch that haunts stateless tokens — once you hand out a wristband, you can't easily un-print it — which is where today's hard part lives.

What should stay in your head
Authentication ("who are you?") at scale rests on two ideas. (1) A JSON Web Token (JWT) is a self-contained, signed badge of identity: three parts — header.payload.signature, base64url-encoded and joined by dots. The payload holds claims (who you are, what roles, when it expires); the signature is yesterday's digital signature, so any service can verify the token wasn't tampered with — locally, without a database lookup. The crucial, dangerous truth: a JWT is signed, not encrypted — anyone can read the payload, so never put secrets in it. (2) OAuth 2.0 is a delegated-authorization protocol: it lets a client app act on a resource owner's (your) behalf by getting a scoped, expiring access token from an authorization server — without the app ever seeing your password. The headline flow is the authorization code flow: you log in at the real provider, approve specific scopes, the app gets a short code and trades it for tokens. Two tokens matter: a short-lived access token (the valet key you present) and a long-lived refresh token (used only to get new access tokens). The single most common confusion: OAuth is authorization, not authentication — it proves an app may do something, not who the user is; OpenID Connect (OIDC) is the thin layer on top that adds an ID token (a JWT about the user) to do real login. The eternal tradeoff: stateful sessions are easy to revoke but need a central store; stateless JWTs scale beautifully but are hard to revoke early (mitigated by short expiry + refresh tokens + a denylist). The one-line test: is this proving who the user is (authN — use OIDC/JWT) or granting an app limited access to something (authZ — use OAuth scopes)?

Two Problems Authentication Must Solve at Scale

Yesterday ended with authentication as a single guard checking IDs. That works for one server. But a real system has many services and often talks to other companies' systems, and that raises two distinct problems — each solved by one of today's tools.

🎟️ Problem 1: proving identity across many services

The user logged in once — now how does every service trust who they are?

You don't want each microservice to re-check a password, or to hammer one central session database on every single request. You want a portable, verifiable proof of identity the user carries with them. That's the JWT: a signed badge each service can validate on its own.

Solved by: stateless, signed tokens (JWT).

🔑 Problem 2: letting an app act for you — without your password

How does App B get limited access to your data in App A, safely?

Handing your password to a third-party app gives it everything, forever, with no way to scope or revoke. You want to grant narrow, expiring, revocable access. That's OAuth 2.0: delegated authorization via scoped tokens, with your password never leaving the real provider.

Solved by: delegated authorization (OAuth 2.0).

They're often used together: you sign in with OAuth/OpenID Connect, the provider issues you a JWT, and you present that JWT to every service afterward. Keep the two jobs separate in your head — carrying identity (JWT) vs granting delegated access (OAuth) — and the whole landscape snaps into focus.

Sessions vs Tokens — Stateful or Stateless?

Before JWTs, the web ran on sessions, and sessions are still a perfectly good choice. Understanding the difference is the real lesson; the rest is detail.

🗄️ Stateful sessions

The server remembers you.

On login the server creates a session and stores it (in memory, Redis, or a DB), then sends the browser a random, meaningless session ID in a cookie. Every request, the server looks that ID up to find who you are. The "truth" lives server-side.

✔ Trivial to revoke — delete the session row and they're logged out instantly.
✖ Needs a central store every service must reach — a bottleneck and a dependency at scale.

🎟️ Stateless tokens (JWT)

The token remembers you.

On login the server hands you a signed JWT that already contains your identity and roles. Every request carries it; each service verifies the signature locally and trusts the contents. The "truth" lives in the token itself — no lookup needed.

✔ Scales beautifully — any service, anywhere, can verify with no shared database.
✖ Hard to revoke early — the token is valid until it expires, even if you wish it weren't.

This is a textbook tradeoff, not a winner. Sessions trade scalability for easy control; JWTs trade easy control for scalability. The honest summary: reach for stateless JWTs when you have many services and want them independent; reach for server-side sessions when instant revocation and simplicity matter more than horizontal scale. Many real systems do both — a short-lived JWT for fast, local verification, backed by a session/refresh mechanism for control.

Anatomy of a JWT — Three Parts, One Signature

A JWT looks like a long line of gibberish with two dots in it. Those dots split it into three base64url-encoded parts. Base64url is encoding, not encryption — it just makes bytes safe to put in a URL; anyone can decode it in a second.

// HEADER — how is this token signed? { "alg": "HS256", "typ": "JWT" } // PAYLOAD — the claims (WHO you are, WHAT you may do, WHEN it dies) { "sub": "u_881", # subject — the user id "role": "user", # a custom claim "iss": "auth.app.com", # issuer — who minted it "aud": "api.app.com", # audience — who it's for "iat": 1735000000, # issued-at "exp": 1735003600 # expires (1 hour later) — DON'T skip this } // SIGNATURE — the tamper-evident seal HMAC-SHA256( base64url(header) + "." + base64url(payload), serverSecret )

How verification works (and why tampering fails)

To issue a token, the server builds the header and payload and then signs them with a key only it knows (or its private key). To verify, a service recomputes the signature over the header+payload it received and checks it matches the signature attached. Change even one character of the payload — flip "role":"user" to "admin" — and the recomputed signature no longer matches, because you don't have the key. The token is rejected. This is exactly the digital-signature integrity from Day 28, applied to identity.

🔐 HS256 — shared secret (HMAC)

Signed and verified with the same secret key (symmetric). Simple and fast — good when the same party issues and verifies. But every verifier needs the secret, so it doesn't suit "many independent verifiers".

🗝️ RS256 / ES256 — key pair (asymmetric)

The auth server signs with its private key; anyone verifies with the freely-shared public key. Perfect for microservices and third parties — they can verify without ever holding a secret that could mint tokens.

⚠️
Signed ≠ encrypted. The payload is only base64-encoded, so anyone who holds the token can read every claim. Never put passwords, card numbers, or secrets in a JWT. It's a badge you show, not a vault you hide things in.
⏰
Always set exp. A token with no expiry is a wristband that works forever — if it leaks, the attacker has a permanent pass. Short lifetimes limit the damage.
🚫
Reject alg: none and pin the algorithm. A classic attack sends a token claiming "no signature needed". A correct verifier insists on the expected algorithm and never trusts the token's own word for it.

OAuth 2.0 — The Four Roles

OAuth 2.0 is not a login system and not an encryption scheme — it's a delegation protocol. It answers one question: how can I let an app do a specific, limited thing on my behalf in another service, without giving it my password? Four roles do the whole dance, and naming them correctly is half the battle.

ROLE 1

👤 Resource Owner

You. The person who owns the data (your photos) and can grant access to it.

ROLE 2

📱 Client

The app that wants access on your behalf (the Birthday Collage app). It never sees your password.

ROLE 3

🏛️ Authorization Server

Where you log in and approve (Google's accounts/consent server). It issues the tokens.

ROLE 4

🗃️ Resource Server

The API holding your data (the Google Photos API). It accepts the access token and serves the data.

Two more words you must know. Scopes are the limits on the valet key — photos.read is very different from photos.read photos.delete email contacts. Grant the least scope that does the job (yesterday's least-privilege, made concrete). And there are two kinds of token: the access token (short-lived, the valet key you actually present to the resource server — often a JWT) and the refresh token (long-lived, kept safe, used only to quietly get a new access token when the old one expires, so you aren't forced to log in every hour).

A critical modern detail: for mobile apps and single-page apps (which can't keep a client secret hidden), the flow adds PKCE (“pixy”) — a one-time proof the app generates so an attacker who intercepts the authorization code can't exchange it for tokens. If you remember one grant type, remember authorization code + PKCE; it's the recommended default almost everywhere now. The old implicit and password grants are discouraged. For pure service-to-service access (no user involved), there's the client credentials grant.

The Big Confusion — OAuth vs OpenID Connect

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes, including experienced engineers: using OAuth 2.0 as a login system. OAuth answers "is this app allowed to do X?" (authorization). It does not, by itself, reliably tell the app who the user is (authentication). An access token is a valet key — the fact that you hold one doesn't prove your identity to the app.

🔑 OAuth 2.0 — authorization

"This app may access these resources."

Issues an access token scoped to resources. Great for delegated access (let an app read your calendar). It was never designed to prove identity to the client.

🪪 OpenID Connect (OIDC) — authentication

"This is who the user is."

A thin standard layer on top of OAuth 2.0 that adds an ID token — a JWT with verified claims about the user (their id, email, name). This is what actually powers real "Sign in with Google / Apple / GitHub" login.

The clean mental split: OAuth gives you an access token (what an app may do); OIDC adds an ID token (who the user is). When your goal is "let users log in with their Google account", you want OpenID Connect — and under the hood it hands you a JWT, tying today's two halves neatly together.

The Hard Parts

Tokens feel clean until you hit the places where the elegance costs you. The senior skill is knowing these in advance.

The JWT revocation problem

The flip side of "no lookup needed" is "nothing to delete." A stateless JWT is valid until its exp — so if a token is stolen, or you fire an employee, you can't simply invalidate it the way you'd delete a session row. The standard answers: keep access tokens short-lived (minutes) so a leak self-heals fast; use a long-lived refresh token that can be revoked at the auth server to cut off new access tokens; and for emergencies maintain a small denylist of revoked token IDs that services check — which, note, quietly reintroduces the very server-side state JWTs were meant to avoid. There's no free lunch; there's a tradeoff dial.

Where do you store the token in a browser?

A token is a bearer credential — whoever holds it is you. In localStorage it's easy to use but readable by any XSS script (Day 28's cross-site scripting). In an httpOnly cookie JavaScript can't read it (XSS-safe) but it's auto-sent on every request, which invites CSRF (needing SameSite cookies / anti-CSRF tokens). Neither is free — the right answer depends on your threat model, and "just put the JWT in localStorage" is a security smell.

OAuth is genuinely complex — and easy to get wrong

Redirect URIs, state parameters (anti-CSRF for the flow), PKCE, token rotation, correct scope minimization, alg pinning — there are many sharp edges, and subtle mistakes become account-takeover bugs. This is the loudest possible case for yesterday's rule: don't roll your own. Use a vetted library or a managed identity provider (Auth0, Okta, Cognito, Keycloak, Firebase Auth) and let specialists own the sharp edges.

Token size and over-stuffing

Because a JWT is sent on every request, a bloated payload (dozens of roles, big claims) adds bytes to every call and can blow past header size limits. Keep tokens lean: an id, essential roles, expiry — look richer details up when you actually need them.

The through-line: tokens trade a central lookup for self-contained, locally-verifiable identity — buying scale and independence at the cost of easy revocation, careful storage, and real protocol complexity. You reach for them when those costs are worth the scale, you keep them short-lived and lean, and you never hand-build the crypto yourself.

(click to reveal)
The sign-in + delegated access (OAuth 2.0 + OpenID Connect). The app (the client) redirects the user (the resource owner) to Google (the authorization server). The user types their password only into Google's page, and consents to specific scopes (openid, profile, photos.read). Google redirects back with a short authorization code; the app exchanges that code (with its client secret, or PKCE for a mobile/SPA client) for tokens. It gets an ID token (an OIDC JWT that proves who the user is — this is the login) and an access token (the scoped valet key it presents to the Photos API, the resource server, to read photos). A refresh token lets it get new access tokens silently later. The app never sees the Google password and the user can revoke its access anytime from Google settings. Identity across microservices (JWT). After login the app issues its own signed JWT containing the user's id and roles. Every later request carries it (e.g. Authorization: Bearer <jwt>), and each microservice verifies the signature locally (ideally with the auth server's public key, RS256) and reads the identity straight off the token — no central session lookup, so services stay independent and scalable. The downside: a stateless JWT can't be revoked before it expires — if it's stolen or access should be cut, you can't just delete a row, so you lean on short expiry + revocable refresh tokens (+ a denylist in a pinch). Binding idea: OAuth/OIDC gets you in without sharing your password; the JWT carries who you are so every service can check on sight.

Real-World Engineering

🔓 "Sign in with Google/Apple/GitHub" — OIDC everywhere

That button is OpenID Connect on top of OAuth 2.0. You authenticate at the provider, the app receives an ID token (a JWT) proving who you are plus a scoped access token — so billions of logins happen without apps ever storing passwords. It's the clearest proof that good auth is delegated auth.

🏢 Auth0 & Okta — don't roll your own

An entire industry exists because auth is too sharp-edged to hand-build. Auth0, Okta, Cognito, Keycloak and friends implement OAuth/OIDC, token rotation, PKCE, and revocation correctly so product teams don't ship account-takeover bugs. Buying identity is usually the senior call.

🌐 Stateless JWTs at microservice scale

Companies running hundreds of services issue short-lived JWTs signed with a private key; every service validates them with the public key locally. No shared session database to bottleneck — the independence that lets each service scale on its own, the whole point of stateless tokens.

🔑 GitHub & Slack OAuth apps

When you authorize a CI tool or a bot, you grant scoped access (just repo, or just chat:write) via OAuth — revocable from your settings, your password never shared. It's the valet-key model powering the entire integrations economy.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What are the three parts of a JWT, and is it encrypted?
A: header.payload.signature, each base64url-encoded and joined by dots. It is signed, not encrypted — the payload (claims) is readable by anyone holding the token, so never put secrets in it. The signature makes it tamper-evident.
Q: Stateful sessions vs stateless JWTs — the core tradeoff?
A: Sessions store the truth server-side (a session store) → trivial to revoke but need a central lookup every request (bottleneck at scale). JWTs carry the truth in a signed token → verify locally, scale beautifully, but are hard to revoke before they expire.
Q: What problem does OAuth 2.0 solve, and what are its four roles?
A: Letting an app act on your behalf in another service without your password (delegated authorization via scoped, revocable tokens). Roles: resource owner (you), client (the app), authorization server (issues tokens, where you log in), resource server (the API holding your data).
Q: OAuth vs OpenID Connect?
A: OAuth 2.0 = authorization (an access token: what an app may do). OIDC = authentication, a layer on top adding an ID token (a JWT: who the user is). "Sign in with Google" is OIDC. Don't use bare OAuth as a login system.
Q: Access token vs refresh token?
A: The access token is short-lived and presented to the resource server on each call (often a JWT). The refresh token is long-lived, kept safe, and used only to obtain new access tokens when they expire — and it can be revoked at the auth server.

If you only remember one thing: authentication at scale is a signed wristband and a valet key. A JWT is a self-contained, signed badge of identity — header.payload.signature — that any service can verify locally without a central lookup, which is why it scales; but it's signed, not encrypted (never put secrets in it) and hard to revoke before it expires (so keep it short-lived). OAuth 2.0 is delegated authorization: it lets an app get a scoped, expiring, revocable access token to act on your behalf without ever seeing your password, via the authorization-code flow across four roles (owner, client, authorization server, resource server). Keep the two jobs straight: OAuth grants what an app may DO; OpenID Connect adds an ID token for who the user IS — and that ID token is itself a JWT, tying today together. Choose stateless tokens for scale and service independence, sessions for easy revocation and simplicity, and never roll your own auth.
