Authentication: OAuth 2.0 & JWT
Answering "Who Are You?" at Internet Scale

Yesterday's castle guard asked one question at the gate: who are you? Today we answer it — for millions of users, across dozens of services, without a database lookup on every request, and without ever asking you to hand a stranger your password. First we'll see why the old way (the server remembering your session) buckles at scale, and how a JWT — a tamper-evident token you carry yourself, sealed with yesterday's digital signature — fixes it. We'll crack a JWT open to see its three parts, learn why the payload is readable, not secret, and why any service can verify it with no central lookup (the whole reason it scales). Then we'll meet the access/refresh token split and the revocation headache it creates. Finally, OAuth 2.0: the valet-key idea that lets "Log in with Google" and "let this app see your calendar" work — giving an app limited, delegated access without ever revealing your password. We'll walk the real Authorization Code flow step by step, see how OpenID Connect turns OAuth's "what may you access" into a true "who are you," and hit the sharp edges: where to store tokens, the alg=none attack, and the stateless-vs-stateful tradeoff.

Picture this

Picture a huge music festival. At the front gate there's a single box office. You show your real passport there once — that's logging in (authentication, yesterday's "who are you?"). The box office doesn't make you re-show your passport at every stage. Instead, it snaps a tamper-evident wristband onto you — that's a JWT. Printed right on the band, in plain view, is your name, which stages you may enter, and "valid until 11 PM." None of that is a secret — anyone can read it. What makes it trustworthy is that the band is heat-sealed with an un-forgeable hologram — that hologram is yesterday's digital signature. Now every stage has its own guard, and each guard just glances at the band and checks the hologram. No guard ever phones the box office — the band proves itself. That's stateless verification, and it's the entire reason this scales: fifty stages, zero central lookups. The catch? Once the band is on, it's hard to un-issue before 11 PM — if someone steals your wristband, it works until it expires. So the bands are made short-lived, and you quietly swap for a fresh one at the box office (that's a refresh token).

Now a second scene, right beside the first. A food truck's app wants to charge your festival tab. You do not hand it your wristband, and you certainly don't hand it your bank card. Instead, the box office issues the truck a valet key: "this vendor may charge up to \$20, nothing else, expires tonight." The truck can do that one thing on your behalf and nothing more — and never once saw your card. That limited, delegated, revocable key is OAuth 2.0. Burn in the two images: log in once and wear a sealed wristband (JWT) that every guard can check without a phone call; and when an app needs to act for you, hand it a valet key (OAuth) — never your master key.

The Story

Session Sam ran a photo-sharing app, and in the early days login was simple. When you signed in, his server generated a random session ID, stored "session abc123 → user Sam" in its memory, and mailed you back a cookie with abc123. Every later request carried the cookie; the server looked abc123 up in its memory and knew it was you. Clean, and it worked perfectly — on one server.

Then the app grew and Sam added a second server behind a load balancer (Day 5). Now the trouble started: you logged in on server A, which remembered your session — but your next request landed on server B, which had never heard of abc123 and logged you straight out. The state lived in one server's memory, and the fleet didn't share it. Sam's first fix was a shared session store (a Redis from Day 7) that every server could query. It worked, but now every single request made a round-trip to that store just to answer "who is this?" — and that store became a bottleneck and a single point of failure. Authentication had quietly become the hottest path in the system.

A mentor asked Sam a sharp question: "Why are you storing who the user is at all? Why not let the user carry a note that proves who they are — a note nobody can forge?" That reframed everything. Instead of handing out a meaningless ticket number and remembering the match, Sam handed out a self-contained token: a little document that says "this is Sam, expires at 11 PM," signed with a key only his servers hold. Any server — or any of the microservices he'd eventually split into — could verify the signature locally and trust the contents, with no lookup at all. The signature (yesterday's lesson!) made forgery impossible; the expiry kept a stolen token from living forever. The bottleneck vanished. This is a JWT.

Months later Sam hit a different wall. A new printing startup wanted to let users import their photos from Sam's app. The startup's first idea was horrifying: "just have users type their Sam username and password into our form, and we'll log in as them." That would hand a third party the master key to the entire account, forever, with no limits. Sam refused — and reached for OAuth 2.0. Now the user clicks "Connect to PhotoApp," gets bounced to Sam's own login screen, approves a narrow request ("PhotoPrint may read your photos — nothing else"), and the printing app receives a scoped, expiring token — never the password. The user can revoke it any time. The password never left Sam's house.

Sam emerged with the realization every senior engineer reaches about identity: don't make the server remember everyone — give each user a signed token they carry themselves (JWT); and when another app needs to act for a user, give it a limited, revocable key, never the password (OAuth).

What should stay in your head
There are two separate jobs here. (1) Proving who a user is on each request and (2) letting one app act on a user's behalf in another. JWT (JSON Web Token) solves the first: a self-contained, signed token the client carries. It has three dot-separated parts — header.payload.signature — all Base64URL-encoded. The payload holds claims (who you are, when it expires) and is readable by anyone (it is not encrypted — never put secrets in it). The signature is the tamper-evident seal from Day 28: the server signs the header+payload with a secret (HMAC) or a private key (RSA), so any change breaks the signature and any service can verify it without a database lookup — that statelessness is why JWTs scale across a fleet and across microservices. Because a signed token can't be un-issued before it expires, we split into a short-lived access token (sent on every request) and a long-lived refresh token (used only to get a new access token) — trading a little revocation lag for huge scalability. OAuth 2.0 solves the second: delegated authorization — the "valet key." Four roles — Resource Owner (you), Client (the app wanting access), Authorization Server (issues tokens after you log in & consent), Resource Server (holds the data). The app gets a scoped, expiring access token, never your password. OAuth is about authorization (what may this app access); when you need true authentication ("who is this user" — the "Log in with Google" button), you add OpenID Connect on top, which issues an ID token (itself a JWT) describing the user. One-line test for any design: is the client carrying a signed, expiring token I can verify locally — and when a third party is involved, did I give it a scoped key instead of the password?

Sessions vs Tokens — Why We Changed Approach

Before JWTs make sense, you need the problem they solve. HTTP is stateless — each request stands alone, with no memory of the last. So after you log in, how does request #2 know it's still you? There are two answers, and the shift from the first to the second is one of the quiet revolutions of web architecture.

🎫 Stateful sessions (the old way)

The server remembers you.

On login the server stores a session record and gives you a cookie holding only a meaningless session ID. Every request, the server looks that ID up to recover who you are.

✔ Easy to revoke (delete the record). ✖ Every request needs a lookup; the session store must be shared across the whole fleet, becoming a bottleneck and a single point of failure.

🎟️ Stateless tokens (the JWT way)

The user carries the proof.

On login the server gives you a signed token that contains who you are and when it expires. Every request carries it; any server verifies the signature locally — no lookup.

✔ No central lookup → scales across servers & microservices effortlessly. ✖ Hard to revoke before it expires; the token travels on every request.

The crux is the word stateless. With sessions, the server holds the state (the ID→user map), so every server must reach the same store. With tokens, the client holds the state, cryptographically sealed, so any server can trust it alone. That's the trade at the heart of today's lesson: you give up easy instant revocation and you pay a few hundred bytes on every request, and in return you get an auth system that doesn't buckle when you go from one server to a thousand. Sessions aren't "wrong" — for a single app with a modest user base they're simpler and revocation is trivial — but at distributed-systems scale, the self-contained token wins, which is why it dominates modern APIs and microservices.

Anatomy of a JWT — Three Parts, One Seal

A JWT (pronounced "jot") looks like a long, ugly string with two dots in it: xxxxx.yyyyy.zzzzz. Those dots split it into three Base64URL-encoded parts. Base64URL is just a reversible encoding (so the bytes survive travel in a URL or header) — it is not encryption. That single fact clears up the biggest misconception about JWTs, so say it out loud: anyone can decode and read the payload.

① Header

Metadata: which algorithm signed this.

A tiny JSON like {"alg":"HS256","typ":"JWT"}. It names the signing algorithm so the verifier knows how to check the seal. (As we'll see, the server must pin the expected algorithm, not blindly obey this field.)

② Payload

The claims — readable, not secret.

JSON of claims about the user and token. Standard ones: sub (subject = user id), exp (expiry time), iat (issued-at), iss (issuer), aud (audience). Plus your own, e.g. role. Never put a password or secret here — it's plainly readable.

③ Signature

The tamper-evident seal (Day 28).

sign(base64(header) + "." + base64(payload), key). With HMAC the key is a shared secret; with RSA/ECDSA the server signs with a private key and anyone verifies with the matching public key. Change one byte of header or payload and the signature no longer matches.

Why the readable payload is still safe to trust

Newcomers panic: "if anyone can read and even rewrite the payload, how is this secure?" Because trust doesn't come from hiding the data — it comes from the signature. An attacker can absolutely decode the token and change role to admin. But to make the server accept that change, they'd have to produce a matching signature, which requires the signing key they don't have. So their forged token fails verification and is thrown out. This is exactly Day 28's integrity guarantee: you don't need to hide the message to protect it from tampering — you need to detect tampering. (If you genuinely need the contents hidden too, there's a separate encrypted variant, JWE — but the everyday JWT is signed, not encrypted.)

Access & Refresh Tokens — Living With "Can't Un-Issue It"

Statelessness gives us scale but takes away easy revocation: once a signed token is out there, every server will honor it until exp. If a token is stolen, you can't instantly kill it the way you'd delete a session row. The industry's answer is a two-token split that turns this from a disaster into a bounded, manageable risk.

🎟️ Access token

Short-lived. Sent on every request.

A JWT with a short expiry (minutes). It's what the Resource Server checks on each call. Because it's short-lived, a stolen one is only useful for a small window — revocation lag is capped at the expiry time.

🔑 Refresh token

Long-lived. Used rarely, kept safe.

A longer-lived credential sent only to the Authorization Server to mint a fresh access token when the old one expires. It's stored carefully and can be revoked server-side — killing a refresh token stops new access tokens being issued.

The pattern: the access token is the disposable day-pass you flash everywhere; the refresh token is the membership card you keep in the safe and only present to the front desk. This gives you the best of both models — stateless speed on the hot path (access tokens verified locally, no lookup) and a revocation lever (a small, server-side list of valid/blacklisted refresh tokens, consulted rarely). If you need to force-logout a compromised user immediately, you revoke the refresh token and keep access-token lifetimes short, so their access dies within minutes. It's a deliberate trade: perfect instant revocation would mean a lookup on every request (back to stateful sessions); this bounds the risk to a few minutes while keeping the hot path lookup-free.

OAuth 2.0 — The Valet Key for Delegated Access

Everything so far was your app authenticating your users. OAuth solves a different, famous problem: how does app A get permission to use your data in app B — without you giving A your B password? "Let this budgeting app read my bank transactions." "Let this scheduler see my Google Calendar." "Log in to this game with my Discord." Handing over your password would be catastrophic: the app could do anything, forever, and you couldn't take it back without changing your password everywhere. OAuth is the valet key — limited, delegated, revocable access.

The four roles — learn these names, they're on every diagram

👤 Resource Owner

You — the user who owns the data.

The person who can grant access to their own stuff (your photos, your calendar, your transactions).

📱 Client

The app that wants access.

The third-party application (the printing app, the budgeting app) asking to act on your behalf — with limited permissions.

🏛️ Authorization Server

Issues the tokens.

Where you actually log in and approve the request (Google's, Discord's, your bank's auth server). It — and only it — sees your password, and hands the Client a scoped token.

🗄️ Resource Server

Holds the data & honors the token.

The API that actually serves the photos/calendar/transactions, accepting the access token and checking its scopes.

The permissions themselves are called scopes — the fine print on the valet key. A scope like photos:read or calendar.events.readonly says exactly what the Client may do. When you approve the request, you see the consent screen ("PhotoPrint wants to: view your photos") — that's scopes made human. Least privilege from Day 28 lives here: a Client should request the narrowest scopes it needs, and you should be suspicious of one asking for more. And because the token is scoped and expiring, the damage a stolen or misbehaving Client can do is bounded to exactly those permissions, for a limited time — and you can revoke it without touching your password.

The Authorization Code Flow — Step by Step

OAuth defines several flows for different situations, but the one you must know — the default for web and mobile apps — is the Authorization Code flow. The cleverness is that your password and the final token travel on separate channels, so the Client never touches your password and can't intercept the token from the browser URL. Here it is, following "Connect PhotoPrint to your photos":

①
Redirect to the Authorization Server. You click "Connect." PhotoPrint (the Client) sends your browser to Google's auth server, including what it wants (scope=photos:read) and where to return. PhotoPrint never shows you a password box — it hands you off.
②
You log in & consent — on Google's domain. You authenticate directly with Google (where your password lives) and see the consent screen: "PhotoPrint wants to view your photos. Allow?" Your password is only ever seen by the party you already trust with it.
③
Back to the Client with a one-time code. Google redirects your browser back to PhotoPrint carrying a short-lived authorization code — not the token. A code glimpsed in a URL is nearly useless on its own, which is the point.
④
Client exchanges the code for tokens — server-to-server. PhotoPrint's backend calls Google directly, presenting the code plus its own client secret, and receives the access token (and usually a refresh token). This back-channel call never passes through the browser, so the token is never exposed there.
⑤
Use the access token. PhotoPrint calls the Resource Server (Google Photos API) with the access token; the API checks its signature and scopes and returns only your photos, read-only. When the token expires, step 4 repeats quietly with the refresh token.

PKCE — the modern must-have

Mobile and single-page apps can't keep a client secret truly secret (it ships inside the app), so an attacker could try to steal the authorization code and redeem it. PKCE ("pixy," Proof Key for Code Exchange) closes that hole: at step 1 the Client invents a random secret, sends only its hash (Day 28's one-way fingerprint!), and at step 4 must present the original secret to prove it's the same app that started the flow. A stolen code is then worthless without that secret. PKCE is now recommended for all clients — know the acronym and the one-line reason.

OAuth vs OpenID Connect — Access vs Identity

Here's a distinction that trips up even experienced engineers, and nailing it will make you sound senior: OAuth 2.0 is about authorization, not authentication. It was designed to answer "what may this app access?" — not "who is this user?" Using raw OAuth to log people in is a classic mistake: an access token says "the bearer may read these photos," which is not a reliable statement of who the user is.

🗝️ OAuth 2.0

Authorization — "what may you access?"

Issues an access token that grants scoped access to resources. It deliberately says nothing standardized about the user's identity. The valet key opens the glovebox; it isn't a passport.

🪪 OpenID Connect (OIDC)

Authentication — "who are you?"

A thin identity layer on top of OAuth. Alongside the access token it issues an ID token — itself a JWT — with verified claims about the user (sub, name, email). This is what actually powers "Log in with Google."

So the mental mapping is clean: that ubiquitous "Log in with Google / Apple / GitHub" button is OIDC (authentication), which rides on OAuth's flow and adds the ID token so your app learns, verifiably, who just logged in. "Let this app access my Google Drive" is plain OAuth (authorization). Same handshake, two purposes — and the ID token is, satisfyingly, just another signed JWT, so everything you learned in the first half of today applies directly.

The Hard Parts

Auth looks solved until you ship it. The senior skill is knowing where the sharp edges are and choosing deliberately.

Where do you store the token? (XSS vs CSRF)

In a browser you have two bad-ish options. Put the token in JavaScript-readable storage (localStorage) and any XSS flaw (injected script, Day 28) can read and steal it. Put it in an httpOnly cookie (unreadable to JS) and you're safe from XSS theft but now exposed to CSRF (the browser auto-attaches the cookie to forged requests) unless you add anti-CSRF defenses. There's no free lunch — httpOnly cookies with SameSite and CSRF tokens is the common, defensible choice, but it's a choice, not a default you can ignore.

Revocation really is harder

Be honest in interviews: stateless JWTs genuinely trade away instant revocation. Short access-token lifetimes plus revocable refresh tokens bound the risk, but if your domain needs immediate kill-switch semantics (a banking admin session), you may keep a small server-side allow/deny list — which is partly re-introducing state. Pick the point on the spectrum your threat model demands, rather than pretending JWTs made the problem disappear.

Don't trust the token's own alg — and never roll your own

The alg=none attack is real: early libraries read the header, saw "alg":"none", and skipped signature checking entirely — accepting any forged payload. A correct verifier pins the expected algorithm server-side and ignores the attacker-controlled header. The broader rule is Day 28's: never roll your own crypto or token logic — use a vetted library, validate exp, iss, and aud, and keep signing keys in a secret manager.

Fat tokens and the "logout" illusion

Because the payload travels on every request, stuffing it with data bloats every call — keep claims lean (an id and a role, not a user's whole profile). And remember that "logging out" a pure JWT client just deletes the token locally; the token itself stays valid until expiry. Real logout means revoking the refresh token and letting the short access token lapse.

The through-line: identity trades statelessness, scale, and no-password delegation against revocation immediacy, token-storage risk, and request overhead. There is no universally "best" scheme — there's the one that fits your scale, your threat model, and how fast you must be able to say "this session is over, now."

"A JWT is encrypted, so it's safe to put secrets in the payload."A standard JWT is signed, not encrypted. The payload is Base64URL — anyone can decode and read it. Never put passwords or secrets in it; the signature protects integrity, not confidentiality.
"If anyone can edit the payload, JWTs are insecure."They can edit it, but they can't produce a valid signature without the key, so the tampered token is rejected on verification. Trust comes from the seal, not from hiding the data — straight from Day 28.

Active Recall

❓ Your API runs on 20 servers behind a load balancer and you're adding "Log in with Google." Walk through: why wouldn't you store login state in one server's memory? What exactly does Google hand your app, and what does your app hand the browser? When a request arrives at any of the 20 servers, how does it verify the user without a database lookup — and what stops an attacker who edits the token to make themselves an admin? (click to reveal)
Why not in-memory state: the load balancer (Day 5) sends successive requests to different servers; state in one server's memory isn't shared, so the user gets logged out on the next hop. A shared session store (Redis, Day 7) fixes correctness but adds a lookup and a bottleneck on every request. What "Log in with Google" does: that's OpenID Connect over OAuth's Authorization Code flow. The browser is redirected to Google's Authorization Server, where the user logs in and consents — your app never sees the password. Google returns a one-time code to your app's backend, which exchanges it (server-to-server, with its client secret / PKCE) for an access token and an ID token (a JWT identifying the user). Your app then issues the browser its own short-lived access token (plus a refresh token). Stateless verification: on each request, any of the 20 servers checks the JWT's signature with the shared key (or public key) and reads the claims — no database lookup. That statelessness is the whole point: identity no longer bottlenecks on a shared store. Why the admin trick fails: the attacker can decode and rewrite the payload to role: admin, but to be accepted they'd need a matching signature, which requires the signing key they don't have — so the forged token fails verification and is rejected (Day 28's integrity guarantee). Bonus: also verify exp so an old token can't be replayed, and pin the algorithm to defeat alg=none.

Real-World Engineering

🔑 "Log in with Google/Apple/GitHub" — OIDC everywhere

The single sign-on buttons you click daily are OpenID Connect over OAuth. They let apps skip storing passwords entirely (less to leak), give users one strong account to protect, and hand the app a signed ID token proving who logged in. It's the most-used auth pattern on the consumer web.

🏦 Open Banking — OAuth as law

Regulations (PSD2 in Europe) forced banks to let you share account data with budgeting apps via OAuth instead of handing over your bank password. It's OAuth's founding use-case written into law: scoped, revocable, password-free delegated access to your own financial data.

⚙️ Microservices & the gateway

In large systems the API Gateway (Day 27) verifies the JWT once at the edge and forwards the trusted identity inward, so dozens of internal services don't each re-implement auth. Stateless tokens are what make this clean — every service can independently trust a signed token without calling back to an auth service.

💥 The alg=none lesson

Around 2015, researchers found many popular JWT libraries would honor "alg":"none" and skip signature verification, accepting forged tokens. The industry-wide fix — pin the algorithm, never trust the token's header — is now a standard review-checklist item and a favorite interview question.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What are the three parts of a JWT, and which one makes it trustworthy?
A: header.payload.signature, all Base64URL-encoded. Header = algorithm metadata; payload = readable claims (sub, exp, role…); signature = the tamper-evident seal (Day 28). The signature is what makes the readable payload trustworthy — you can't forge it without the key.
Q: Is a JWT payload secret? Why does stateless verification scale?
A: No — the payload is readable (Base64URL is encoding, not encryption); never put secrets in it. It scales because any server verifies the signature locally with no lookup, so auth doesn't bottleneck on a shared session store across the fleet.
Q: Access token vs refresh token?
A: Access token = short-lived JWT sent on every request (caps the damage of theft). Refresh token = long-lived, sent only to the Authorization Server to mint new access tokens, and revocable server-side. The split buys stateless speed and a revocation lever.
Q: What problem does OAuth 2.0 solve, and name its four roles.
A: Delegated authorization — letting an app access your data in another service without your password (the valet key). Roles: Resource Owner (you), Client (the app), Authorization Server (issues tokens after login + consent), Resource Server (holds the data). Access is limited by scopes.
Q: OAuth vs OpenID Connect?
A: OAuth 2.0 = authorization ("what may this app access?" → access token). OIDC = authentication ("who is this user?"), a layer on top that adds an ID token (a JWT). "Log in with Google" is OIDC; "let this app read my Drive" is plain OAuth.

If you only remember one thing: stop making the server remember everyone — give each user a signed token they carry (JWT); and when an app must act for a user, give it a limited, revocable key, never the password (OAuth). A JWT is header.payload.signature: the payload is readable, not secret, and the signature (yesterday's digital signature) is what lets any server trust it without a lookup — the statelessness that makes it scale across a fleet and across microservices. Because a signed token can't be un-issued, we split into a short-lived access token and a revocable refresh token to bound the cost of theft. OAuth 2.0 is delegated authorization — four roles (Resource Owner, Client, Authorization Server, Resource Server), a scoped and expiring token, and the Authorization Code flow (with PKCE) that keeps your password and the token on separate channels. When you need true authentication — "who is this user?" — add OpenID Connect and its ID token (the "Log in with Google" button). Mind the sharp edges: where you store the token (XSS vs CSRF), that logout isn't instant, and that you must pin the algorithm so alg=none can't fool you.
