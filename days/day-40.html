Design a Distributed Cache
The Same Ring, Tuned for Pure Speed

Yesterday we built a key-value store to keep data safe forever. Today we build its speed-obsessed cousin — a distributed cache like Redis and Memcached. It's almost the same machine: consistent hashing to spread keys (Day 14), replicas for availability. But three things flip. The storage is pure RAM instead of disk, so a read takes microseconds rather than milliseconds. Entries expire on a timer (TTL). And because RAM is tiny and expensive, when memory fills up the cache throws old entries away — that's the eviction policy, the one genuinely new idea today. A cache is a small, fast copy of your hottest data sitting in front of a slow database, so the overwhelming majority of reads never touch the database at all. You'll feed keys to a live LRU cache and watch it evict, and dial a hit-ratio calculator to see why going from a 95% to a 99% hit rate matters more than any hardware you could buy — it's the single most important number in the whole design.

Picture this

Imagine the busiest coffee shop in the city at morning rush. Behind the counter is a warming rail with room for maybe six finished drinks. In the back, through a door, is the slow storeroom and espresso machine where every drink is actually made from scratch — grinding, pulling shots, steaming milk. Making a drink from the back takes a couple of minutes. Handing over a drink that's already sitting on the warming rail takes two seconds. That warming rail is a cache; the back room is your database.

A customer orders a flat white. The barista glances at the rail — there's one already made! Hand it over, two seconds, next please. That's a cache hit. The next customer orders something obscure that isn't on the rail. Now the barista has to walk to the back and make it from scratch — slow. That's a cache miss. But here's the clever habit: after making it, the barista puts a second copy on the warming rail, betting someone else will order it soon. The next person who wants it gets the fast path. The cache fills itself with whatever people actually ask for.

Two problems keep the barista honest. The rail only holds six drinks, so when it's full and a new drink needs a spot, the barista clears the one that nobody has ordered in the longest time — the drink gathering dust — to make room. That's eviction, and "throw out the least-recently-used one" is the famous LRU policy. Separately, any drink that's been sitting too long goes cold and gets tossed regardless — that's a time-to-live (TTL) expiry. And there's one nightmare: if headquarters changes the flat-white recipe but an old one is still on the rail, customers get the stale version — the eternal problem of cache invalidation. Scale this to a citywide chain where each drink is always made at one assigned shop (hash the drink name to a shop — consistent hashing, Day 14), and the moment a wildly popular new drink drops and every shop rushes to brew it at once, you get a stampede. Hold this picture: a tiny fast counter in front of a slow kitchen, filled on demand, cleared by least-recently-used, expired by a timer. That is a distributed cache.

The Story

Lena ran the product-page team at a fast-growing shop. Every page load called the database for the same thing — the product's name, price, photos, and rating. The query was correct and even had an index (Day 8), but it still took ~50 milliseconds, and at 20,000 page views a second the database was drowning. Buying a bigger database bought her a few weeks. She needed reads that didn't touch the database at all.

Her first version was almost embarrassingly simple: keep a Map of productId → productJSON in the web server's own memory. On a request, check the map; if it's there, return it in a fraction of a millisecond; if not, query the database, stuff the result into the map, and return it. A cache-aside read path. Latency fell off a cliff and the database exhaled. But two cracks appeared fast. First, the map grew without bound and the web server ran out of RAM and crashed — she had forgotten that memory is finite, so a cache must throw things away. She added a rule: hold only the most-recently-used 10,000 products and evict the rest (LRU). Second, she had ten web servers, each with its own private map, so a price change had to be fixed in ten places and they constantly disagreed.

The fix was to pull the cache out of the web servers into a shared tier all of them talk to — a cluster of Redis nodes. Now there was one source of cached truth. To scale it past one box she sharded keys across the Redis nodes with the exact tool from yesterday: consistent hashing (Day 14), so adding a node moved only a slice of keys. She gave every entry a TTL of five minutes so even if she forgot to update something, it could never be more than five minutes stale. And when the marketing team changed a price, the write path did two things: update the database and delete that key from the cache (invalidate), so the next reader would miss, re-fetch the fresh value, and re-populate.

It worked — until a flash sale. A single hot product's cache entry happened to expire at the peak second, and in the next instant thousands of simultaneous requests all missed and all hammered the database for the same row at once — a thundering herd that nearly took the database down the cache was supposed to protect. Her senior engineer showed her the standard defenses: let only the first missing request rebuild the entry while the others wait (a lock), serve slightly stale data while the rebuild happens, and jitter the TTLs so a thousand keys don't all expire on the same tick. By the next sale the product pages served 99% of reads from RAM in under a millisecond and the database barely noticed the traffic. Lena had, piece by piece, built exactly what Facebook, Twitter, and Netflix put in front of their databases — a distributed cache.

What should stay in your head
A distributed cache is a small, fast, in-memory copy of your hottest data that sits in front of a slow data store, so the vast majority of reads never reach the store. It's a key-value store (Day 39) with three deliberate trades: storage is RAM, not disk (microseconds, but volatile — losing it is OK, it's just a copy); entries carry a TTL and expire; and because RAM is small, when it fills up the cache evicts something to make room — usually the least-recently-used (LRU) entry. The one number that rules everything is the hit ratio: the fraction of reads served from cache. At a 95% hit ratio you've removed 95% of the load from your database; pushing it to 99% removes five times more of the remaining load — which is why hit ratio beats any hardware upgrade. The read path is almost always cache-aside: check cache → on a miss, read the store and populate the cache → return. The write path must keep the two copies honest — the simplest safe choice is update the store, then delete (invalidate) the cache key. You scale the tier across nodes with consistent hashing (Day 14), exactly like yesterday. One-line test: a cache is a KV store in RAM with an eviction policy and a TTL, measured by its hit ratio — and the two hard questions are always "what do I throw away?" and "how do I avoid serving stale data?"

Why This Problem Exists

Databases are built to be correct and durable, not fast. They write to disk, hold locks, honor transactions, and run flexible queries — all of which cost time, typically single-to-double-digit milliseconds per read. That's fine at low traffic. But most large systems are read-heavy and ask for the same data over and over: the same trending tweet, the same product page, the same user profile, the same homepage. Re-computing or re-fetching an identical answer millions of times is pure waste — and eventually the database simply can't keep up, no matter how big you make it.

A cache attacks this with a single idea: keep a copy of the answer somewhere much faster, and reuse it. "Faster" almost always means RAM, which is roughly a hundred times quicker to read than disk, so a cache hit is measured in microseconds. Because popular data is accessed far more than unpopular data (the classic 80/20, or power-law, distribution), a cache that holds just the hot slice can absorb the overwhelming majority of reads. The database shrinks from "serves every read" to "serves only the misses" — often a 10× to 100× reduction in load. The catch, and the entire design problem, is that a cache is a second copy of the truth living in limited, volatile memory. That forces two permanent questions that the rest of this lesson answers: when memory is full, what do I throw away? and when the real data changes, how do I avoid serving the stale copy?

The Design, Step by Step

Same discipline as the last two designs: scope it, size it, sketch the API, then draw the boxes. The data model is a KV store, so the new design effort goes into what to keep, what to throw away, and how to stay fresh.

Step 1 — Scope the requirements

Eviction policy and total memory budget?

Step 2 — Size it (back-of-the-envelope, Day 30)

Suppose a product catalog of 10 million items, each cached entry ~2 KB, with reads at 100,000/sec following an 80/20 pattern, and we want a 99% hit ratio.

# how much RAM? We don't need to cache everything — just the hot working set. hot set ≈ 20% of 10M items × 2 KB = ~4 GB # fits in RAM on a few nodes all items = 10M × 2 KB = ~20 GB # caching ALL of it is wasteful + low return # what does 99% buy us? (the whole point) reads = 100,000/sec to cache = 99% → 99,000/sec served from RAM (microseconds) to DB = 1% → 1,000/sec reach the database # a 100× reduction — the DB is now bored # nodes — RAM-bound, not CPU-bound; replicate hot shards for availability nodes ≈ 4 GB hot set / per-node RAM, ×2 for replicas → a small cluster absorbs 100k/s

The sizing reveals the cache's superpower: you cache the hot 20%, not everything, and still deflect 99% of reads. It also fixes the shape — the cache is RAM-bound (how much hot data fits), the eviction policy decides which 20% stays resident, and the hit ratio is the metric you live and die by.

Step 3 — Sketch the API

GET /cache/{key} response 200 { "value": …, "ttl_left": 142 } # hit — straight from RAM or 404 (miss) # caller then reads the DB & repopulates SET /cache/{key} request { "value": …, "ttl": 300 } # store for 300s, then auto-expire response 204 No Content DELETE /cache/{key} # invalidate after a DB write

In practice you rarely call this over HTTP — you use a Redis/Memcached client speaking a thin binary protocol over a kept-open TCP connection, because the whole value proposition is latency, and an HTTP round-trip per read would defeat it.

Step 4 — Draw the architecture

The cache tier is a shared cluster that all app servers talk to, sharded by consistent hashing (Day 14) so each key lives on a known node. It sits between the app and the database, short-circuiting the slow path for hot keys.

1
App server gets a read request. It doesn't go straight to the database. It first asks the cache tier for the key.
2
Locate the shard (Day 14). The client hashes the key onto the ring and talks to the one cache node that owns it — spreading keys evenly and moving only a slice when a node is added or removed.
3
Hit → done in microseconds. If the node has the key (and it hasn't expired), return it immediately. The database is never touched. This is the fast path 95–99% of reads take.
4
Miss → read-through to the database, then populate. On a miss the app reads the database (slow, Day 39/11), writes the value back into the cache with a TTL, and returns it — so the next reader hits.
5
Writes invalidate. When data changes, the app updates the database and deletes the cache key. Meanwhile each node evicts its least-recently-used entries when full and drops entries whose TTL has passed.

The Heart: In-Memory Storage + Eviction

Two properties separate a cache from the durable KV store we built yesterday. One gives it its speed; the other is the genuinely new idea of the day.

In-memory storage — why it's fast, and what you give up

A cache keeps its data in RAM, not on disk. Reading from RAM is roughly 100× faster than reading from an SSD and far faster than a spinning disk — microseconds versus milliseconds. That single choice is the whole reason a cache exists. The price you pay is that RAM is volatile (a reboot or crash loses everything) and expensive and limited (you get gigabytes, not terabytes). Both are acceptable because the cache is only a copy: if a node dies and its data vanishes, the database still has the truth — the cache just refills from misses. This is the mindset shift from Day 39: yesterday losing data was a catastrophe; today it's a shrug.

Eviction — because memory is finite, you must choose what to throw away

Since the cache can't hold everything, the instant it's full and a new entry arrives, it must evict an existing one. The rule it uses is the eviction policy, and the goal is always the same: throw away the entry least likely to be asked for next, to keep the hit ratio high. You can't see the future, so policies approximate it:

PolicyEvicts…Good when
LRU — Least Recently Usedthe entry not touched for the longest timeThe default. Recently-used data tends to be used again (temporal locality). Simple, effective, used almost everywhere.
LFU — Least Frequently Usedthe entry accessed the fewest timesAccess frequency is stable and some keys are permanently hotter. Guards a steady favorite against a burst of one-offs.
FIFO — First In, First Outthe oldest-inserted entry, regardless of useRarely ideal — ignores how often an entry is used. Simple, but can evict a hot entry just because it's old.
TTL — Time To Liveany entry past its expiry timerFreshness, not space. Pairs with the others — TTL bounds staleness; LRU/LFU manage capacity.

The demo above implements LRU, the workhorse: every access moves a key to the "front," and eviction always takes from the "back." It's cheap to maintain (a hash map plus a doubly-linked list = O(1) per operation) and matches how real traffic behaves — what you used a moment ago, you'll probably want again soon.

✔ RAM for speed, eviction for finiteness, TTL for freshness — these three are what turn a key-value store into a cache. RAM makes reads microseconds; an eviction policy (usually LRU) keeps the right data resident when space runs out; a TTL caps how stale any entry can get.
✘ Don't cache everything or cache forever — a cache with no eviction is a memory leak that crashes the node; a cache with no TTL and no invalidation serves stale data forever. The art is caching the hot slice and bounding its staleness.

Write Strategies & Invalidation

Reads are the easy part — almost everyone uses cache-aside. The writes are where caches get subtle, because now you have two copies of the data (cache and database) and they can disagree. How you keep them in sync is the write strategy.

The four patterns

StrategyHow it worksTrade
Cache-aside
(lazy loading)		App checks cache; on a miss it reads the DB and populates the cache. On a write, it updates the DB and deletes the cache key.The default. Only requested data is cached; resilient to cache failure. Downside: the first read of each key always misses.
Read-throughApp only talks to the cache; the cache fetches from the DB on a miss and stores it.Cleaner app code (caching logic lives in the cache layer). Same first-miss penalty; needs cache support for it.
Write-throughEvery write goes to the cache and the DB synchronously, together.Cache is always fresh; reads are always consistent. Downside: writes are slower (two hops), and you cache data that may never be read.
Write-back
(write-behind)		Write to the cache only; flush to the DB asynchronously later, in batches.Fastest writes, absorbs bursts. Dangerous: if the cache dies before the flush, you lose data — only for loss-tolerant workloads.

Why "update the DB, then delete the cache" is the safe default

For most systems the winning write path is cache-aside with invalidation: write the database (the source of truth), then delete the cache key rather than trying to update it. Deleting is safer than updating because two concurrent writers can race and leave a wrong value cached, whereas a delete just forces the next reader to fetch the fresh value from the database. It's the humble version of the famous quip that cache invalidation is one of the two hard problems in computer science — and the reason a TTL is your safety net: even if an invalidation is somehow missed, the entry can't be stale longer than its TTL.

The Hard Parts

A cache is trivial to add and surprisingly easy to get subtly wrong. Four edges separate a toy from a production cache tier.

Cache invalidation — staleness vs freshness

The moment the database changes, the cached copy is a lie until it's updated or deleted. Invalidate too aggressively and your hit ratio collapses (you're constantly re-fetching); invalidate too little and users see stale data. The pragmatic toolkit: TTL to bound worst-case staleness, delete-on-write to invalidate precisely, and accepting that for most data (a product description, a follower count) being a few seconds stale is completely fine. Decide your staleness budget per kind of data — it's a product decision as much as an engineering one.

Thundering herd / cache stampede

A single hot key expires (or the cache restarts) and suddenly thousands of concurrent requests all miss at once and all stampede the database for the same value — the thing the cache was there to prevent. Defenses: a lock / single-flight so only the first miss rebuilds the entry while the rest wait for it; serve-stale-while-revalidate (hand back the old value and refresh in the background); and TTL jitter (randomize expiry times so a million keys don't all expire on the same second).

Hot keys & cold starts

One wildly popular key (a celebrity post, a flash-sale item) can overwhelm the single shard that owns it no matter how large the cluster is — the hot-key problem, same as yesterday. Fixes: replicate the hot key to multiple nodes, or keep a tiny local in-process cache in front of the shared tier for the very hottest handful. The opposite failure is the cold start: a freshly-restarted or newly-deployed cache is empty, so every read misses and the database is briefly swamped. Mitigate by warming the cache (pre-load known-hot keys) and ramping traffic gradually.

Consistency — a cache is eventual by nature

Because it's a second copy that lags the database, a cache is fundamentally an eventual-consistency tool (echoes of Day 10's CAP). For a bank balance or an inventory count at checkout, do not trust the cache for the decision — read the source of truth, or use a cache only for display with a tiny TTL. Know which data tolerates staleness (almost everything that's read-heavy and display-only) and which doesn't (money, inventory, permissions). The cache is a performance tool, never the arbiter of correctness.

The through-line: get/set is trivial; deciding what to evict and keeping copies honest is the whole discipline. Store in RAM for speed, evict with LRU to keep the hot set resident, bound staleness with TTL plus delete-on-write, and defend the database behind the cache against stampedes and cold starts. Chase the hit ratio — it's the number that turns a struggling database into a bored one.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"A cache is just a hash map in memory."The hash map is the easy 5%. The other 95% is eviction, TTL, invalidation, stampede defense, sharding, and hot keys — managing a limited, volatile second copy of the truth.
"Cache everything to be safe."A cache with no eviction is a memory leak that crashes the node, and caching cold data just wastes RAM. Cache the hot slice; let eviction keep it resident.
"More hit ratio barely matters once it's high."Backwards. 95% → 99% barely changes latency but cuts database load 5× (misses go 5% → 1%). The last few percent are where the cache pays off.
"On a write, update the cached value."Prefer delete (invalidate) the key. Two concurrent updaters can race and leave a wrong value cached; a delete just forces a clean re-fetch next read.
"The cache can be my source of truth."Never for correctness-critical data. A cache is a volatile copy and eventually consistent — trust the database for money, inventory, and permissions.
"TTL alone keeps data fresh."TTL bounds worst-case staleness but doesn't react to writes. Combine TTL + delete-on-write: invalidate precisely, and let TTL be the safety net for missed invalidations.

Active Recall

❓ Your product-page database is overloaded by reads of the same popular items. You add a distributed cache. (1) What read path do you use, and what happens on a hit vs a miss? (2) Your cache fills up — what decides which entry is thrown out, and which policy is the usual default and why? (3) A single hot item's entry expires at peak and the database nearly falls over from the sudden flood of identical misses — what is this called and how do you defend against it? (click to reveal)
(1) Cache-aside. On a read, check the cache first. A hit returns the value from RAM in microseconds — the database is never touched. A miss reads the value from the database (slow), writes it into the cache with a TTL, and returns it, so the next reader hits. Writes update the database and delete (invalidate) the key.

(2) Eviction policy. When memory is full, the cache evicts the entry least likely to be used next. The usual default is LRU (Least Recently Used) — it throws out whatever hasn't been touched for the longest time, because recently used data tends to be used again (temporal locality), and it's O(1) to maintain with a hash map + doubly-linked list.

(3) Thundering herd / cache stampede. Defenses: a lock / single-flight so only the first missing request rebuilds the entry while others wait; serve-stale-while-revalidate; and TTL jitter so many keys don't expire on the same instant. Keeping a high overall hit ratio and warming the cache after a restart (to avoid a cold start) round it out.

Real-World Engineering

⚡ Redis

The most popular distributed cache — an in-memory data store with rich types (strings, hashes, lists, sorted sets), configurable eviction (allkeys-lru, lfu, and more), TTLs, and optional persistence. Redis Cluster shards keys across nodes using hash slots (its flavor of consistent hashing) and replicates each shard for failover.

🧊 Memcached

The classic, deliberately minimal cache: pure in-memory key→value with LRU and TTL, nothing else. Its simplicity makes it blazing fast and easy to scale horizontally — the client hashes keys across a pool of independent nodes. The textbook "just a cache."

📘 Facebook / Meta

Famously runs one of the largest cache tiers on earth — thousands of Memcached servers (the mcrouter and TAO systems) in front of MySQL. Their published work on cache invalidation, leases (stampede control), and regional consistency is the canonical study of caching at hyperscale.

🌐 CDNs & browsers

Caching isn't only a server tier. CDNs (Day 7) cache responses at edge locations near users, and your browser caches assets locally — the same ideas (TTL, eviction, invalidation via cache-control headers) applied at every layer from the client to the database.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What three changes turn a key-value store into a cache?
A: (1) Storage is RAM not disk (microsecond reads, but volatile); (2) entries have a TTL and expire; (3) when full it evicts an entry (usually LRU). It's a copy, so losing it is fine — the database is the source of truth.
Q: Why does hit ratio matter so much, and why do the last few percent count most?
A: Hit ratio is the fraction of reads served from RAM, so miss% is the load that reaches the DB. Going 95%→99% barely changes latency but cuts DB load 5× (5%→1% of traffic). The last few percent are where a cache truly protects the database.
Q: What is LRU eviction and why is it the usual default?
A: Least Recently Used — evict the entry untouched for the longest time. It exploits temporal locality (recently-used data is used again soon), is O(1) with a hash map + doubly-linked list, and matches real traffic better than FIFO.
Q: On a write, should you update the cached value or delete it? Why?
A: Delete (invalidate) the key after writing the DB. Two concurrent writers can race and leave a wrong value if you update; a delete just forces a clean re-fetch on the next read. TTL is the safety net for any missed invalidation.
Q: What is a cache stampede (thundering herd) and how do you prevent it?
A: A hot key expires and thousands of concurrent requests all miss and hit the DB for the same value at once. Defenses: a lock / single-flight (only the first miss rebuilds), serve-stale-while-revalidate, and TTL jitter so keys don't all expire together.

Keep data in RAM for microsecond reads; evict with LRU so the hot set stays resident when memory fills; bound staleness with a TTL plus delete-on-write invalidation; and shard across nodes with consistent hashing (Day 14). Chase the hit ratio — pushing it from 95% to 99% removes five times more load from your database than it sounds like. Everything hard is about the two eternal questions: what do I throw away? and how do I avoid serving stale data? Get those right and a struggling database becomes a bored one.
