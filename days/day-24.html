Rate Limiting & Throttling
Protecting a Service From Its Own Callers

Yesterday, the circuit breaker protected you from a dependency that was failing. Today we flip the arrow around: how do you protect a service from the people calling it? Because your worst day rarely starts with your own bug — it starts with too much traffic: one client stuck in a runaway retry loop, a scraper hammering your API, a viral moment, or a login endpoint under a password-guessing attack. Without a guardrail, that flood exhausts your servers and takes everyone down — the abuser and every well-behaved user beside them. A rate limiter is the bouncer at the door: it caps how fast any one caller can come in, lets the excess wait or bounce with a polite 429 Too Many Requests, and keeps the service standing for everybody else. Today you'll learn the five classic algorithms, why the token bucket quietly runs most of the internet, and the traps that make rate limiting deceptively hard to get right.

Picture this

Picture a popular nightclub with a strict doorman. Next to him sits a small bucket of entry tokens, and a machine drips a new token into it at a steady rate — say, one every few seconds. The rule is simple: to get in, you take a token. If there's a token in the bucket, you walk right in. If the bucket is empty, you wait for the next drip — or, if there's a line out the door, you get turned away with a friendly "come back in a minute." Here's the elegant part: because tokens accumulate when it's quiet, a small burst of friends who all show up at once can pour straight in (there were a few tokens saved up) — but nobody can sustain a flood, because over time you can only enter as fast as the tokens drip. The doorman never lets the club get dangerously overcrowded, so the people already inside keep having a good time. That's a rate limiter, and that bucket is literally the most popular algorithm for building one — the token bucket. Burn in the image: a rate limiter is a doorman with a bucket of tokens that refills at a fixed rate — it welcomes short bursts, but caps the sustained flow so one flood can't ruin the party for everyone.

The Story

Throttle Tara ran a small but growing public API — a weather service that developers loved. It was fast, it was free, and for months it hummed along. Then one Monday it fell over. Not because of a bug, not because a dependency died — but because a single customer's script got stuck in a retry loop and started firing thousands of requests per second. Tara's servers, sized for ordinary traffic, buckled under the flood. And here's the cruel part: the flood didn't just hurt the one bad client — it took the whole API down for every well-behaved developer too. One greedy caller starved everybody.

Tara's first fix was a blunt one: "if any client sends more than 100 requests a minute, block them." She wrote a counter that reset every minute — a fixed window. It worked… until a clever client noticed the seam. It sent 100 requests in the last second of one minute, then 100 more in the first second of the next — 200 requests in about two seconds, twice her intended limit, all technically "within the rules." Her window had a boundary, and traffic clustered on it. The limiter was leaking exactly where two windows met.

A mentor drew her a picture of a bucket. "Stop counting in windows," she said. "Hand out tokens." Tara switched to a token bucket: each client got a bucket that refilled at 100 tokens/minute, up to a cap. Every request spent one token; no token, no entry (a clean 429). Now a client who'd been quiet could still burst — the saved-up tokens absorbed a legitimate spike — but nobody could sustain more than the refill rate. The boundary exploit vanished, because there was no boundary anymore, just a smoothly refilling bucket.

Then Tara scaled to ten servers behind a load balancer, and her limiter got confused all over again. Each server kept its own bucket, so a client bouncing across all ten could send 10× the limit — the fleet had no shared memory of who'd done what. The real fix was to move the counter out of each server and into a single fast shared store (Redis), so all ten servers decremented the same bucket. It cost a few milliseconds per request and made the limiter depend on Redis being up — a real trade — but now the limit meant the same thing no matter which server you hit. Tara had learned the two hard truths of rate limiting: the algorithm is the easy part, and "who is the client, counted where?" is the hard part.

What should stay in your head

A rate limiter answers one question for every incoming request: "has this caller used up its allowance for now — yes or no?" If no, let it through and record it. If yes, reject it fast with HTTP 429 Too Many Requests (ideally with a Retry-After telling them when to come back). The whole craft is in how you count (the algorithm — token bucket, leaky bucket, windows) and what you count by (the key — per user, per IP, per API key, per endpoint, or globally). Get those two right and you turn "one caller can take everyone down" into "every caller gets a fair, predictable slice." The mantra: a rate limiter trades a few rejected requests now for the whole service staying alive for everyone.

The Five Classic Algorithms

Every rate limiter ever built is a variation on five ideas. They trade accuracy, memory, and burst behavior against each other. Learn them as a family and you can reason about any limiter you meet.

🪟 Fixed Window

Count requests in a fixed clock slot (e.g. "100 per minute, resets on the minute"). One integer per key.

✓ Dead simple · tiny memory · easy to reason about.
✗ Boundary burst: up to 2× the limit can slip through across the seam of two windows.

📜 Sliding Window Log

Store the timestamp of every request; to decide, count how many fall in the trailing window (e.g. last 60 s). Perfectly precise.

✓ Exact — no boundary artifact at all.
✗ Memory grows with traffic (one entry per request); expensive at scale.

🌗 Sliding Window Counter

The pragmatic hybrid: keep the current and previous fixed-window counts and blend them by how far into the window you are. Approximates the sliding log with two integers.

✓ Smooths the boundary burst · cheap · what most APIs actually use.
✗ An approximation — slightly off during uneven bursts.

🪣 Token Bucket

The industry default. A bucket refills at a fixed rate up to a cap; each request spends a token. Saved-up tokens allow a controlled burst, then the refill rate caps the sustained flow.

✓ Allows bursts on purpose · O(1) memory (two numbers) · intuitive knobs.
✗ Two parameters to tune (rate and burst); burst can still spike a fragile backend.

💧 Leaky Bucket

Requests pour into a queue that drains at a fixed rate, like a funnel. Output is perfectly smooth; a full queue overflows (drops). Great when the thing behind it hates bursts.

✓ Perfectly steady output · protects burst-sensitive backends.
✗ No bursts at all (can feel harsh) · queueing adds latency.

The mental shortcut: token bucket = "save up, then spend" (bursts welcome, average capped); leaky bucket = "steady drip out" (no bursts, smooth output); windows = "count per clock slot" (simplest, watch the boundary). Below is the token bucket up close — the one you'll reach for 90% of the time.

The token bucket in ~15 lines (per key)
class TokenBucket { // rate = tokens added per second; capacity = max burst constructor(rate, capacity) { this.rate = rate; this.capacity = capacity; this.tokens = capacity; // start full this.last = now(); } allow() { const t = now(); // 1. refill for the time that has passed, capped at capacity this.tokens = min(this.capacity, this.tokens + (t - this.last) * this.rate); this.last = t; if (this.tokens >= 1) { this.tokens -= 1; return true; } // spend a token → allow return false; // empty → 429 } }

Notice how little state it holds: a token count and a "last refilled" timestamp. We don't run a timer adding tokens — we lazily compute how many would have dripped in since we last looked. That's why it's O(1) memory and trivially fast, and why it scales to millions of keys.

What to Actually Return — The 429 Contract

Rejecting a request is only half the job; how you reject shapes whether clients behave well or make things worse. A good rate limiter speaks a clear, kind protocol.

Status 429 Too Many Requests — the standard "you're over the limit, slow down" signal. Distinct from 503 (server overloaded) and 403 (forbidden); a well-behaved client treats 429 as "back off and retry later," not "give up."
Retry-After: 12 — tell the client exactly how long to wait (seconds, or a date). This is the single most important header: it turns a blind retry storm into a polite, coordinated wait. Without it, clients guess — and guessing means hammering.
X-RateLimit-Limit / -Remaining / -Reset — proactively tell good clients their budget: the cap, how many they have left, and when it refills. Now they can self-pace before hitting the wall instead of discovering it by getting bounced.
Fail open, usually — if the limiter's own datastore (Redis) is down, do you block every request or allow every request? For most APIs you fail open (allow) — a brief lapse in limiting beats a total outage. For a login/password endpoint you might fail closed (deny) — there, letting the limit lapse is a security hole. It's a deliberate choice, per endpoint.

The through-line: a 429 with a Retry-After is a conversation — "not now, try again in 12 seconds" — and clients that get a clear answer cooperate. A bare, header-less rejection invites exactly the retry storm (Day 23) you were trying to prevent.

The Hard Part — Limiting Across a Fleet

On one server, a rate limiter is a local counter — easy. But real services run on many servers behind a load balancer, and that changes everything. If each server keeps its own bucket, a client spread across N servers gets N× the intended limit. So where does the count live?

ApproachHow it worksTrade-off
Local (per-server)Each server counts on its own, no coordination.Fastest, no dependency — but the global limit is fuzzy (up to N× if clients spread out). Fine for coarse protection.
Centralized store (Redis)All servers increment/decrement the same counter in a shared, fast in-memory store (often via an atomic Lua script).Accurate global limit, but adds a network hop (~1 ms) and a dependency — decide fail-open vs fail-closed if it's down.
Sticky routingThe load balancer always sends a given client to the same server, so its local counter is the global one.Keeps limiting local & fast, but hurts even load distribution and breaks when that server dies.
Approximate / local + syncCount locally, periodically gossip totals (e.g. give each server limit/N, adjust from a background sync).Cheap and fast at massive scale; the global number lags reality slightly. What big CDNs use.

Where you put the limiter matters as much as where you count. Push it to the edge — a load balancer, an API gateway (Day 27), or a CDN like Cloudflare — and you reject floods before they ever reach (and cost you) your application servers. That's the ideal: the cheapest request to serve is the one you rejected at the front door, far from your expensive backend. A limiter buried deep in your app still spends a thread and a database connection deciding to say no.

The Traffic-Control Toolkit

Rate limiting is one instrument in a small orchestra of "don't let load hurt us" techniques. They're often confused; here's how they differ and combine.

🎟️ Rate limiting

Today. Cap how many requests a caller may make per unit time; reject the excess with 429. Enforces fairness and protects capacity.

"100 requests per minute per API key."

🐢 Throttling

The gentler cousin: instead of rejecting excess, slow it down — delay/queue requests so the effective rate stays under a ceiling. Same goal, softer failure mode.

Leaky bucket is throttling in action.

📊 Quotas

Limits over a long window — per day/month, often tied to a billing tier. Rate limits protect the system (per-second); quotas enforce the plan (per-month).

"1M API calls/month on the Pro tier."

🪫 Load shedding

When you are overloaded, proactively drop low-priority work (return 503) to protect the core. Rate limiting is per-caller; load shedding is whole-system self-defense (Day 23).

"Under stress, drop analytics, keep checkout."

⏪ Backpressure

Signal upstream to slow down when a downstream stage can't keep up — flow control through the whole pipeline, not just at the door (think TCP windows, bounded queues).

"Stop sending until I've caught up."

⚡ Circuit breaker

Yesterday's pattern — the mirror image. A breaker protects you from a dependency you call; a rate limiter protects a service from the callers hammering it.

Breaker = outbound defense; limiter = inbound.

The relationship worth carving in stone: a circuit breaker and a rate limiter point in opposite directions. The breaker sits on your outbound calls and stops you from hammering a sick dependency. The rate limiter sits on your inbound calls and stops others from hammering you. Every robust service has both.

The Hard Parts — What Rate Limiting Costs You

A rate limiter is cheap insurance, but "just add a limit" hides several genuinely hard decisions. Here's the honest ledger.

1. "Who is the client?" is harder than it sounds

You limit per key — but which key? Per IP is easy but wrong when thousands of users sit behind one corporate NAT or mobile carrier (you'd throttle a whole office as if it were one abuser), and trivial to dodge by rotating IPs. Per API key / user ID is fairer but only works once the request is authenticated — and unauthenticated endpoints (login, signup) are exactly the ones that need protecting most. Most real systems layer several keys: a loose per-IP limit at the edge, a precise per-user limit deeper in.

2. Accuracy vs. latency vs. cost — pick two

A perfectly accurate global limit means every request consults a shared store (a network hop and a dependency on every call). A fast, cheap limit counts locally and is only approximately global. At Google/Cloudflare scale, nobody pays for perfect accuracy on every request — they accept a slightly fuzzy global number in exchange for speed. Know which one your use case actually needs: fraud/abuse wants accuracy; capacity protection tolerates approximation.

3. Fail-open vs. fail-closed is a security decision

When the limiter's datastore is down, allowing everything (fail open) protects availability but removes your guardrail; denying everything (fail closed) preserves the guardrail but can take you down on its own. The right answer differs by endpoint — fail open on a read API, fail closed on a password-check endpoint where the limit is the brute-force defense. A one-size default will be wrong somewhere.

4. Bad limits create their own outages and bad UX

Set the limit too low and you throttle legitimate power users and integrations (a self-inflicted outage for your best customers). Forget the Retry-After and clients retry blindly — the very retry storm (Day 23) you were preventing. Limit on a burst-sensitive backend with a big token bucket and a single allowed burst can still knock it over. And a limiter you don't monitor (Day 26) can silently reject real traffic for hours while you think everything's fine.

The through-line: a rate limiter trades a small amount of added latency, an extra dependency, and some genuinely subtle tuning and keying decisions for something essential — the guarantee that no single caller, bug, or spike can consume the capacity that everyone else depends on. Placed at the edge, keyed thoughtfully, and returning a clear 429 contract, it's one of the highest-leverage guardrails in all of systems design. Bolted on carelessly, it throttles your best users, dodges your worst ones, and adds a fragile dependency in front of everything.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"A fixed-window counter (100/min, reset on the minute) enforces 100 per minute."It enforces 100 per window, not per rolling minute. A client can send 100 in the last second of one window and 100 in the first second of the next — ~200 in ~2 seconds. Use a sliding window or token bucket to close that boundary leak.
"Just limit per IP address."Per-IP is easy but blunt: thousands of real users share one NAT/mobile-carrier IP (you'd throttle a whole office as one abuser), and attackers rotate IPs to dodge it. Prefer per-user/API-key where you can; use per-IP only as a coarse edge layer.
"Rate limiting and throttling are the same thing."Same goal, different failure mode. Rate limiting rejects excess (429); throttling delays/queues it to smooth the rate. Leaky bucket throttles; a hard 429 cap rate-limits. Often used together.
"My per-server limiter gives me a global limit."With N servers behind a load balancer, N independent local counters let a client do up to N× the limit by spreading out. A true global limit needs a shared store (Redis), sticky routing, or an approximate-sync scheme.
"Returning 429 is enough."A bare 429 with no Retry-After invites blind retries — a retry storm. Always tell the client when to come back, and expose X-RateLimit-* so good clients self-pace before they hit the wall.
"The token bucket and leaky bucket are basically the same."Opposite burst behavior. Token bucket allows bursts (up to the saved-up capacity) then caps the average — good for user-facing APIs. Leaky bucket forbids bursts, emitting a perfectly steady stream — good for protecting a burst-sensitive backend.

Active Recall

❓ You run a public API on 8 servers behind a load balancer and want to enforce "60 requests per minute per API key." You start with a fixed-window counter stored locally on each server. Two things go wrong: (1) a client bursts past your intended limit right at the top of each minute, and (2) another client somehow sustains far more than 60/min even though every server "counts to 60." Explain the exact cause of each bug, then describe the algorithm and the storage change that fix them — and what new cost that fix introduces. (click to reveal)
(1) The boundary-burst bug — a fixed-window artifact: a fixed window resets on a clock tick (say, on the minute). A client can send 60 requests in the last second of 11:00 and 60 more in the first second of 11:01 — that's 120 requests in ~2 seconds, double your intended rate, all "within the rules," because the two batches live in two different windows. The window has a hard seam and traffic clusters on it. Fix: switch to a token bucket (or sliding-window counter). The token bucket has no boundary — tokens refill continuously at 60/min up to a cap, each request spends one, and an empty bucket returns 429. A client can burst up to the saved-up capacity, but can never sustain more than the refill rate, so the 2× seam exploit disappears. (2) The N×-limit bug — local counters on a fleet: with 8 servers each keeping their own counter, the load balancer spreads a client's requests across all 8. Each server happily allows 60 before it starts rejecting, so the client gets up to 8 × 60 = 480/min — the fleet has no shared memory of the total. Fix: move the counter out of each server into a single shared, fast store — Redis — that all 8 servers decrement atomically (typically a Lua script so the read-check-write is one indivisible step). Now the limit means the same thing no matter which server you hit. (3) The new cost: every request now makes a network round-trip to Redis (adds ~1 ms) and the limiter depends on Redis being up — so you must decide, per endpoint, whether to fail open (allow if Redis is down — protects availability) or fail closed (deny — protects the guardrail). Accuracy bought a hop and a dependency; that's the fundamental rate-limiting trade.

Real-World Engineering

💳 Stripe

Stripe runs a token-bucket limiter and is famous for its developer-friendly 429 contract — clear limits, Retry-After, and guidance to use exponential backoff with jitter. They also separate concerns: a per-second request-rate limiter to protect infrastructure, and concurrency limiters for expensive operations, so one heavy caller can't starve the rest.

🌐 Cloudflare

As a CDN sitting in front of millions of origins, Cloudflare rate-limits at the edge — floods are absorbed and rejected before they ever reach your servers. At their scale, exact global counts are too expensive per request, so they lean on fast, approximate counting synced across their network — the accuracy-vs-latency trade made explicit.

🐙 GitHub API

GitHub's REST API is a textbook of the 429 contract: every response carries X-RateLimit-Limit, -Remaining, and -Reset so clients can self-pace, with different budgets for authenticated vs anonymous callers (a per-user vs per-IP keying decision in the wild) and separate, tighter limits for expensive search endpoints.

⚙️ Nginx, Envoy & Redis

You rarely build a limiter from scratch: Nginx ships a limit_req module (a leaky bucket), Envoy/API gateways offer global rate limiting via a shared service, and a huge fraction of custom limiters are just a Redis counter driven by a small atomic Lua script — the shared-store pattern Tara landed on.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What problem does a rate limiter solve, in one sentence?
A: It stops any single caller (a bug, an abuser, or a traffic spike) from consuming more than its fair share of a service's capacity — protecting the service and every other caller by capping request rate and rejecting the excess (usually with HTTP 429).
Q: How does a token bucket work, and what makes it the default choice?
A: A bucket refills with tokens at a fixed rate up to a capacity; each request spends a token, and an empty bucket returns 429. It's the default because it allows controlled bursts (saved-up tokens) while capping the sustained average, uses O(1) memory (a token count + a timestamp), and has intuitive knobs (rate and burst size).
Q: What is the fixed-window boundary-burst problem?
A: A fixed-window counter resets on a clock tick, so a client can send a full window's worth of requests just before the reset and another full window's worth just after — up to 2× the intended limit in a very short span across the boundary. Sliding windows and token buckets remove the seam.
Q: Why is limiting across many servers hard, and how do you fix it?
A: N servers with local counters let a client spread its traffic and get up to N× the limit, because no server knows the global total. Fixes: a shared store (Redis, atomic increments) for an accurate global limit, sticky routing, or approximate local counting with periodic sync — trading a network hop and a dependency for accuracy.
Q: How is a rate limiter the mirror image of a circuit breaker?
A: A circuit breaker guards your outbound calls — it stops you from hammering a failing dependency. A rate limiter guards your inbound calls — it stops others from hammering you. Outbound defense vs inbound defense; robust services run both.

If you only remember one thing: a rate limiter is a doorman with a bucket of tokens — it protects a service from its own callers. Where yesterday's circuit breaker guarded your outbound calls to a failing dependency, a rate limiter guards your inbound calls so no single client, bug, or spike can eat the capacity everyone else needs. Count with the right algorithm — token bucket (bursts welcome, average capped; the default), leaky bucket (perfectly smooth, no bursts), or sliding-window (avoids the fixed-window boundary burst that lets ~2× slip through) — and key it thoughtfully (per user/API key, not just per IP). Reject with a clear 429 + Retry-After and rate-limit headers so you invite cooperation, not a retry storm. Enforce it at the edge so floods die before they cost you, and remember the two genuinely hard parts: making the limit global across a fleet (a shared Redis counter, at the cost of a hop and a dependency) and deciding fail-open vs fail-closed when that store is down.
