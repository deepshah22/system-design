Rate Limiting & Throttling
The Bouncer That Keeps Your Service Standing

Yesterday's circuit breaker protected you from a dependency that was failing. Today we flip the mirror: a rate limiter protects a service from the callers hammering it. Here's the uncomfortable truth about a public endpoint: you don't control who calls you or how fast. One buggy client stuck in a retry loop, one aggressive scraper, one viral moment — any of them can fire more requests than your server can serve, and when the server falls over, it falls over for everyone, including the well-behaved users (the "noisy neighbor" problem). A rate limiter is the bouncer at the door with a clicker: it counts requests per client and, once you're over your allowed rate, it turns the extras away instantly with a polite 429 Too Many Requests — protecting the server so it can keep serving everyone else. Today you'll learn the four algorithms every interviewer asks about, why bursts and window boundaries are the tricky part, and the real headache: enforcing one limit across a hundred servers at once.

Picture this

Picture a popular nightclub with a strict fire-code capacity — say 100 people. Out front stands a bouncer with a handheld clicker-counter. As people arrive, he clicks each one in. The club fills, music's great, everyone can move and breathe. Then a bus pulls up and forty more people surge toward the door at once. The bouncer doesn't panic and he doesn't let the club get dangerously packed — he simply holds up a hand: "We're at capacity. Wait a few minutes, some folks are leaving." He turns the extras away quickly and politely right at the door. Why is this the kind thing to do? Because the alternative — cramming 140 people into a room built for 100 — doesn't give you a fuller party, it gives you a crushed, miserable room where nobody can get a drink and someone gets hurt. By turning some people away at the door, the bouncer keeps the club good for the 100 people already inside. That's a rate limiter exactly: it sits at the entrance to your service, counts who's coming in and how fast, and once a caller is over their allowed rate it bounces the extra requests fast (a 429) instead of letting them stampede in and take the whole service down for everybody. Burn in the image: a rate limiter is the bouncer with a clicker — it turns a few requests away at the door so the service stays standing for everyone else.

The Story

Throttle Tara ran the public API for a weather startup. Anyone with a free API key could ask for a forecast. For months it hummed along. Then one morning her dashboards went red: response times climbed from 50 ms to 8 seconds, the database was pinned at 100% CPU, and paying customers were getting errors. She hadn't shipped anything. What happened?

She dug into the logs and found it: one single API key was sending 9,000 requests a second. A customer had written a script with a bug — an infinite loop that hammered her forecast endpoint as fast as the network allowed. That one misbehaving client was consuming almost all of her server's capacity, so every other customer's request had to fight for the scraps. This is the noisy neighbor: in a shared system, one greedy tenant degrades the service for everyone. And the cruel part is that Tara's server was trying its hardest — accepting every request, opening a database connection for each — which is exactly what was killing it. It was politely working itself to death.

Her first instinct was to just buy bigger servers. It bought her a few hours, then the same script — or the next scraper, or the next viral news event — buried the bigger servers too. You cannot out-scale abuse; there is always a client that can send more than you can afford to serve. What she needed wasn't more capacity, it was a doorman — something that says "this key gets 100 requests per second, and not one more."

So she added a rate limiter in front of the API. For each API key it kept a small token bucket: 100 tokens that refill at 100 per second. Every request spends one token; if the bucket is empty, the request is turned away instantly with 429 Too Many Requests and a Retry-After: 1 header telling the client when to come back. The buggy client's flood now hit a wall after its first 100 requests — those got fast, cheap rejections that never touched the database — while every other customer, comfortably under their own limit, was completely unaffected. The database CPU dropped back to 20%. One noisy neighbor could no longer ruin the building.

Then Tara hit the twist that catches everyone. She ran the API on ten servers behind a load balancer, and she'd put the token bucket in each server's memory. So a client's "100/second" limit was actually being enforced ten separate times — the real limit had quietly become 1,000 per second. To enforce one shared limit across ten servers, the count had to live somewhere all of them could see. She moved the counters into Redis, shared by every server. Now the limit was real, global, and exact — at the cost of a tiny Redis round-trip on every request. That trade — a little latency for a correct, shared limit — is the heart of distributed rate limiting.

What should stay in your head
A rate limiter caps how many requests a given caller (an API key, a user, an IP, or everyone globally) may make in a given amount of time, and rejects the excess — fast and cheaply — instead of letting it through. It exists to solve one core problem: you don't control your callers, so any of them (a bug, a scraper, an attack, a viral spike) can send more load than you can serve, and an overloaded service fails for everyone, not just the abuser. The limiter is the counter at the door that keeps one noisy neighbor from starving all the others. The standard, polite way to say "you're over your limit" is HTTP 429 Too Many Requests, usually with a Retry-After header so a good client knows exactly when to try again. There are four classic algorithms — Token Bucket (allow bursts, refill steadily; the most popular), Leaky Bucket (force a perfectly smooth output), Fixed Window (simple counter per clock-minute; suffers a burst at the boundary), and Sliding Window (a rolling window that fixes the boundary burst) — and they mostly differ in one thing: how they handle bursts. The subtle, senior-level part isn't the algorithm; it's making one limit hold across many servers, which means the count has to live in a shared store (usually Redis). Keep two words in mind: reject cheaply. A rejection must cost far less than serving the request, or the limiter becomes the very overload it was meant to prevent.

The Four Algorithms — They All Count, They Differ on Bursts

Every rate limiter answers the same question — "has this caller had too many requests recently?" — but the recently and the too many are defined differently, and that changes how they treat a sudden spike. These four come up in almost every system design interview; learn what each optimizes for.

🪙 Token Bucket

The default choice. A bucket holds up to capacity tokens and refills at a steady rate (e.g. 100/sec). Each request spends one token; no token → reject. It allows bursts up to the bucket size (a client that's been quiet banks tokens), but the long-run average is capped at the refill rate. Simple, memory-cheap, burst-friendly — why Stripe, AWS, and most APIs use it.

💧 Leaky Bucket

The smoother. Requests pour into a bucket and leak out at a fixed rate (like water through a hole). The output is perfectly constant no matter how bursty the input; if the bucket fills up, new requests overflow and are dropped. Use it when a downstream system needs a steady, even flow (e.g. protecting a fragile legacy service or a payment processor) rather than tolerating spikes.

🪟 Fixed Window

The simple one. Count requests in each clock-aligned window ("max 100 per minute"). At the top of the minute the counter resets to zero. Dead easy — one integer per key — but it has a nasty flaw: a client can send 100 at 11:59:59 and another 100 at 12:00:00, so 200 requests land in one second right at the boundary. Twice your intended rate, exactly when you least want it.

🎞️ Sliding Window

The accurate one. Instead of a counter that resets on a clock tick, it counts requests in the last 60 seconds continuously — the window slides with time, so the boundary burst disappears. The exact form (sliding log) stores a timestamp per request (accurate but memory-heavy); the practical form (sliding window counter) blends the current and previous windows for near-perfect accuracy at tiny cost.

The token bucket is worth understanding cold, because it's the one you'll reach for by default. Two numbers define it: refill rate (the sustained requests/second you allow) and capacity (the biggest burst you'll tolerate). The whole algorithm is a handful of lines:

Token bucket, sketched
class TokenBucket { constructor(rate, capacity) { this.rate = rate; // tokens added per second (the sustained limit) this.capacity = capacity; // max tokens = biggest burst allowed this.tokens = capacity; // start full this.last = now(); } allow() { const elapsed = (now() - this.last) / 1000; this.tokens = Math.min(this.capacity, this.tokens + elapsed * this.rate); // refill this.last = now(); if (this.tokens >= 1) { this.tokens -= 1; return true; // have a token → serve it } return false; // empty → reject with 429 } }

Notice the beauty: no background timer refilling the bucket. It just records when it was last touched and computes how many tokens should have accrued since, lazily, on the next request. That's what makes it cheap enough to run per-key for millions of keys.

Where It Lives, What Key It Counts, and the 429

A rate limiter is only useful if it sits in front of the expensive work — the earlier you reject, the cheaper the rejection. There's a natural chain of places it can live, and mature systems use several at once.

The client (self-throttling).A polite SDK paces its own requests and backs off on a 429. Cheapest of all, but you can't trust it — a buggy or hostile client won't cooperate. Defense in depth, never your only line.
The edge / load balancer / CDN.Cloudflare, an API gateway, or NGINX rejects floods before they even reach your app servers. This is where you stop volumetric abuse and DDoS — the earlier the wall, the less it costs you.
The API gateway.The most common home for business rate limits ("free tier: 100/min, pro: 10k/min"). One central place enforces per-key policy for every service behind it (Day 27). Kong, AWS API Gateway, and Envoy all do this.
The service itself.Fine-grained, per-endpoint limits close to the code — "this one expensive report endpoint gets 5/min even for pro users." The last, most specific line of defense.

What are you counting per? The limiter key

A limit is always per something, and choosing that key is half the design. Per API key or user is fairest for a paid API (each customer gets their own budget). Per IP address is your only option for anonymous traffic (login pages, signups) — but beware, a whole office or mobile carrier can share one IP, so a legitimate crowd looks like one abuser. Global limits protect a specific fragile backend ("no more than 500/s total hit the payments processor"). Per endpoint lets you guard your most expensive routes tightly while leaving cheap ones generous. Real systems combine these — e.g. per-IP at the edge and per-key at the gateway.

How to say "no" well: the 429 and its headers

Rejecting is a conversation, not a door slammed in silence. The HTTP standard for "you've hit your limit" is 429 Too Many Requests, and a good limiter includes headers so a well-behaved client can adapt instead of blindly retrying (which would be a retry storm — remember Day 23):

HeaderWhat it tells the client
Retry-After: 5"Come back in 5 seconds." The single most important one — it turns a blind retry loop into a patient wait.
X-RateLimit-LimitYour total allowance for the window (e.g. 100).
X-RateLimit-RemainingHow many requests you have left right now (e.g. 0).
X-RateLimit-ResetWhen your allowance refills (a timestamp or seconds).

Rate limiting vs. throttling — a distinction worth having straight: rate limiting usually means rejecting the excess outright (429). Throttling often means slowing it down instead — queuing or deliberately delaying requests to a steady pace (a leaky bucket in spirit) rather than dropping them. Reject when the work is cheap to refuse and the client can retry; throttle when every request matters and you'd rather serve it late than not at all.

The Distributed Problem — One Limit, Many Servers

Here's the part that separates a textbook answer from a real one, and it's exactly the wall Tara hit. Running the limiter in each server's local memory is fast and simple — and wrong the moment you have more than one server. If a client's limit is 100/s but you have 10 servers behind a load balancer, each keeping its own bucket, the client can do 100/s on every server — an effective limit of 1,000/s. The count has to be shared.

Centralized counter (Redis).Every server reads and updates the same counter in a shared, fast store — almost always Redis (in-memory, single-digit-millisecond, atomic operations). This gives an exact global limit. The cost: a network round-trip on every request, and Redis becomes a critical dependency you must make highly available.
Make the update atomic."Read count, check it, increment" across many servers is a classic race condition — two servers both read 99 and both allow the request, blowing the limit. The fix is an atomic operation: Redis INCR (with EXPIRE) for fixed windows, or a small Lua script that does the whole token-bucket check-and-decrement in one indivisible step on the Redis server.
Or go approximate for speed.If a perfectly exact limit isn't worth the latency, each server can rate-limit locally against its share (limit ÷ N) and periodically sync counts. Faster and more resilient (no hard Redis dependency), but the global limit is fuzzy at the edges. A very common senior-level trade: exactness vs. latency vs. resilience.
Distributed fixed-window with an atomic Redis INCR
# key is unique per client + time window, e.g. "rl:key123:min:29384710" count = redis.INCR(key) # atomic: no two servers race if count == 1: redis.EXPIRE(key, 60) # first hit sets the window's 60s TTL if count > LIMIT: return 429 # over the shared limit → reject return 200 # under → serve it

Because INCR is atomic and the count lives in one place, all ten servers now enforce a single, true limit. Swap the fixed window for a token bucket and the idea is identical — you just move the "refill and decrement" logic into a Lua script so it stays atomic. This is why Redis is the near-universal answer to "how do you rate-limit across a fleet?" in an interview.

The Hard Parts — What Rate Limiting Costs You

A rate limiter is one of the highest-leverage things you can put in front of a service, but it is a real system with real failure modes. Here's the honest ledger.

1. The limiter can become the bottleneck

If checking the limit is expensive — a slow database query, a chatty Redis call, a lock — then under a flood you're doing expensive work to reject requests, which is the exact overload you were trying to prevent. The golden rule is rejection must be far cheaper than serving. And the shared Redis is now a critical dependency: if it goes down, do you fail open (allow everything, risking overload) or fail closed (reject everything, causing an outage)? Neither is free, and you must choose deliberately.

2. Picking the limit is genuinely hard

Set it too low and you throttle legitimate power users and hurt real customers. Set it too high and it doesn't protect anything. The "right" number depends on your actual capacity, your traffic shape, and your business tiers — and it drifts as the system changes. Good limits come from measuring real usage (what does the 99th-percentile legitimate user actually do?) and leaving headroom, not from a guess.

3. Bursts, fairness, and the shared-IP trap

Real traffic is spiky, so a limit with zero burst tolerance rejects normal usage and frustrates users; that's why token bucket's burst allowance exists. And keying by IP quietly punishes the innocent: an entire company office, a university, or a mobile carrier's NAT can share one public IP, so thousands of legitimate users look like a single abuser and all get throttled together. Choosing the key is a fairness decision, not just a technical one.

4. It shapes behavior, so it needs monitoring

A wave of 429s might mean an attack — or it might mean you set a limit too low and are silently breaking a paying customer's integration. You can't tell without observability (Day 26): track your 429 rate per key, and alert when it spikes. A rate limiter you don't watch is one that will eventually reject the wrong people quietly for a very long time.

The through-line: a rate limiter trades a small amount of added latency, a shared dependency to run, and the ongoing work of tuning limits and watching 429s for something essential — the guarantee that no single caller, bug, or spike can take your service down for everyone else. For any endpoint exposed to callers you don't control, that's not optional; it's table stakes.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"I'll just buy bigger servers instead of rate limiting."You cannot out-scale abuse. There's always a client (or a spike) that can send more than you can afford to serve, and scaling up just raises the bill for absorbing the flood. A limiter caps the load itself; capacity only raises the ceiling it slams into.
"I put the token bucket in each server's memory — done."With N servers behind a load balancer, a per-server limit means the real limit is N× what you intended. One shared limit needs a shared count — a centralized store (Redis) with atomic updates. Local-memory limiting is only correct on a single instance.
"Fixed window is simplest, so it's fine."Its clock-aligned reset lets a client send the full limit at the end of one window and again at the start of the next — up to 2× the rate in a moment right at the boundary. Fine for rough limits; use sliding window (or token bucket) when the burst matters.
"Rate limiting and throttling are the same thing."Related but distinct: rate limiting typically rejects the excess (429); throttling typically delays/smooths it (queue or pace it). Reject when refusing is cheap and retriable; throttle when every request matters and late beats never.
"Just return a 429 and you're done."A bare 429 makes clients retry blindly — a retry storm (Day 23). Send Retry-After and X-RateLimit-* headers so good clients wait the right amount. And make sure the rejection is cheap — a 429 that runs a DB query is a self-inflicted overload.
"Key everything by IP address."IPs are shared — an office, campus, or mobile carrier NAT puts thousands of real users behind one IP, so they get throttled as a single abuser. Prefer per-API-key/per-user where you can authenticate; reserve per-IP for anonymous traffic and set it generously.

Active Recall

❓ Your public API runs on 10 servers behind a load balancer. You want each API key limited to 100 requests/second. A junior engineer implements it with a token bucket stored in each server's local memory and ships it. In testing on one server it works perfectly, but in production a single key is clearly doing far more than 100/s and one buggy key is still degrading everyone. Explain (1) the exact bug with the local-memory approach and what the real limit became, (2) how you'd fix it so the limit is truly 100/s globally, including the race condition you must avoid, and (3) what you'd return to the throttled client and why a bare 429 isn't enough. (click to reveal)
(1) The bug — per-server buckets multiply the limit: the load balancer spreads a key's requests across all 10 servers, and each server keeps its own bucket that only sees the ~10% of traffic it happens to receive. So the key can do 100/s on each of the 10 servers — the effective global limit is 10 × 100 = 1,000/s, ten times what you intended. Local-memory rate limiting is only correct on a single instance; the instant you have a fleet, the count has to be shared. (2) The fix — a shared, atomic counter: move the counters into a centralized store all 10 servers read and write — almost always Redis (in-memory, fast, atomic). Now there's one true count per key. The race condition to avoid is the classic "read-check-increment": two servers both read 99, both decide "under limit," and both allow — blowing the limit. Make the whole check-and-decrement atomic: a Redis INCR+EXPIRE for a fixed window, or a small Lua script that refills and decrements the token bucket in one indivisible step on the Redis server. Trade-off: a Redis round-trip per request (a little latency, and a dependency you must keep highly available); if that's too costly you can rate-limit locally against limit ÷ N and sync periodically, accepting a fuzzier global limit for lower latency. (3) What to return: HTTP 429 Too Many Requests — but a bare 429 makes clients retry immediately and blindly, a retry storm that piles more load on you. Include Retry-After (when to come back) plus X-RateLimit-Limit/Remaining/Reset so a well-behaved client backs off exactly the right amount. And the rejection must be cheap — reject before any database work, or your "protection" becomes the overload.

Real-World Engineering

💳 Stripe

Stripe rate-limits its API with a token bucket per account and publishes it as a first-class part of the developer experience — clear 429s and guidance to back off with exponential retry. It even separates limiters by concern (a request-rate limiter plus a concurrency limiter), a great example of layering more than one limit for different failure modes.

🐙 GitHub API

GitHub's REST API gives authenticated users 5,000 requests/hour and returns the state on every response via X-RateLimit-Limit, -Remaining, and -Reset headers — the textbook pattern for telling clients exactly where they stand so good citizens self-throttle instead of hammering blindly.

🌩️ Cloudflare

At the edge, Cloudflare rate-limits across a massive distributed network to blunt DDoS and abuse before traffic ever reaches origin servers. Doing this globally is a hard distributed-counting problem — they lean on fast shared state and clever approximation so a rule like "no more than N/min from one source" holds across hundreds of data centers.

☁️ AWS & Redis

AWS API Gateway exposes token-bucket throttling (a steady rate plus a burst) as a config knob on every API. And across the industry, Redis is the near-default engine for distributed limiting — its atomic INCR and Lua scripting make an exact, shared, fleet-wide counter genuinely easy to build.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What problem does rate limiting solve, in one sentence?
A: You don't control your callers, so any of them (a bug, a scraper, an attack, a viral spike) can send more load than you can serve — and an overloaded service fails for everyone. A rate limiter caps how fast each caller may hit you and rejects the excess cheaply (a 429), keeping one noisy neighbor from starving the rest.
Q: Name the four classic algorithms and their one-line difference.
A: Token Bucket — refills tokens steadily, allows bursts up to capacity (the default). Leaky Bucket — leaks at a fixed rate, output perfectly smooth (no bursts). Fixed Window — one counter per clock-window, simple but allows a 2× burst at the boundary. Sliding Window — a rolling window that removes the boundary burst.
Q: Why can't you keep the rate-limit counter in each server's local memory?
A: With N servers behind a load balancer, each enforces the limit on only its slice of traffic, so the effective limit becomes N× the intended value. One true global limit needs a shared count in a centralized store (usually Redis) updated atomically (INCR or a Lua script) to avoid the read-check-increment race.
Q: What's the fixed-window boundary problem?
A: Because the counter resets on clock ticks, a client can send the full limit at the very end of one window (e.g. 11:59:59) and the full limit again at the start of the next (12:00:00) — up to 2× the intended rate packed into one moment right at the boundary. Sliding window fixes it by counting the last N seconds continuously.
Q: What should a good rejection include beyond the 429, and why?
A: A Retry-After header (and ideally X-RateLimit-Limit/Remaining/Reset) so a well-behaved client waits the right amount instead of retrying blindly — a bare 429 invites a retry storm (Day 23). And the rejection itself must be cheap (reject before any DB work), or the limiter becomes the overload it was meant to prevent.

If you only remember one thing: you don't control your callers, so you must control what they can do to you. A rate limiter is the bouncer at your door — it counts requests per caller and, past the allowed rate, rejects the excess cheaply with a 429 so one bug, scraper, or spike can't take the service down for everyone (the noisy-neighbor problem). Four algorithms, differing mainly on bursts: Token Bucket (the default — steady refill, bursts up to capacity), Leaky Bucket (perfectly smooth output), Fixed Window (simplest, but a 2× burst at the boundary), and Sliding Window (rolling, no boundary burst). The senior-level move is making one limit hold across many servers — which means a shared, atomic counter, almost always in Redis — and rejecting well: a 429 with Retry-After, cheap enough that refusing never becomes the overload. Choose your limit from real usage, choose your key as a fairness decision, and watch your 429 rate so you catch a limit that's silently breaking real users.
