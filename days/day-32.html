Consensus: The Raft Algorithm
The Same Guarantee, Finally Understandable

Yesterday Paxos proved the impossible-sounding thing is possible: unreliable machines can agree on a single value and never disagree. But Paxos is a maze — brilliant, correct, and so hard to understand that teams ship subtle bugs for years. In 2014 two Stanford researchers asked a different question: not "what's the cleverest consensus algorithm?" but "what's the most understandable one?" The answer was Raft — the exact same guarantees as Multi-Paxos, rebuilt around three ideas a human can hold in their head: a single strong Leader, numbered Terms like election cycles, and a replicated Log every node copies in the same order. Today we build it from scratch — the three server states, leader election with the one quietly-genius trick (randomized timeouts), log replication, and the two safety rules that guarantee a new leader always already holds every committed entry. This is the algorithm inside etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV — and therefore inside Kubernetes itself.

Picture this

A newsroom. There is exactly one Editor-in-Chief, and a room full of reporters. The Editor calls out the day's headlines in strict order — "Headline 1… Headline 2… Headline 3…" — and every reporter writes each one into an identical notebook, in the same order. The rule of the room: reporters only write what the Editor dictates, and they write it in the Editor's order. That shared, in-order notebook is the whole point — if every notebook matches, the newsroom has one agreed story of the day.

Every few seconds the Editor shouts "still here!" — a heartbeat. As long as reporters hear it, they stay quiet and keep copying. But if the Editor goes silent (steps out, faints, loses their voice), each reporter is counting down a private timer — and here's the trick: everyone's timer is a different, random length. The first one to hit zero stands up and declares, "The Editor is gone — I nominate myself as Editor for Edition #7!" (each new editorship gets the next edition number). If a majority of the room raises their hands for her — and crucially, only if her notebook is at least as complete as theirs — she becomes the new Editor, and the newsroom carries on without missing a headline.

Burn in the image: one editor dictating an ordered notebook, a heartbeat keeping everyone calm, and a random-length timer that lets exactly one reporter stand first. That is Raft. A single leader, a replicated log copied in order, terms numbered like editions, and randomized timeouts so the room never descends into everyone shouting at once. Everything else is the careful rules that make sure the new Editor never erases a headline the old newsroom already agreed on.

The Story

Maya inherited a problem: a home-grown coordination service, built on Multi-Paxos, that the whole company's infrastructure leaned on for leader election and config. The original authors were long gone. The code worked — mostly — but nobody on the team could fully explain it. There were comments like "// do not touch this, it took 3 weeks to get right" next to logic nobody dared change. Twice a year, during a messy network event, it would do something baffling: lose an entry it had already acknowledged, or get wedged with two nodes each convinced recovery was incomplete.

Maya spent a month trying to truly understand it. She drew the message flows. She re-read "Paxos Made Simple" three times. The single-value protocol she could follow — but the real system was Multi-Paxos plus leader election plus log compaction plus membership changes, and the paper left all of that as an exercise. Every engineer who'd touched it had filled the gaps slightly differently. The bugs lived in those gaps. "The algorithm is correct," she told her manager, "but I can't prove our implementation is, and neither could the people who wrote it. That's the actual problem."

A staff engineer pointed her at a 2014 paper with an unusual title: "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm." Its authors, Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout, had started from a premise Maya had never seen stated out loud: that if an algorithm is too hard to understand, it doesn't matter that it's correct — the systems built on it will be buggy, because humans can't reason about what they can't follow. So they designed Raft backwards from understandability. One strong leader (not a committee of equal proposers). Time sliced into numbered terms. A single, concrete log everyone copies. They even ran user studies proving students learned Raft faster than Paxos.

Maya rebuilt the service on Raft over a quarter. The difference wasn't that Raft was more powerful — it gives the exact same guarantees as Multi-Paxos. The difference was that now the whole team could read the code and say, out loud, why it was correct. Code review caught two real bugs before they shipped, because reviewers could actually follow the leader-election and log-matching logic. The mysterious twice-a-year incidents stopped. "We didn't get a better algorithm," Maya wrote in the postmortem-that-never-was. "We got one we could trust, because we could understand it. In a system people have to operate at 3 a.m., that's the same thing."

Maya's lesson is Raft's whole reason to exist: correctness you can't understand is correctness you can't maintain. An algorithm engineered to be followed by a human is an algorithm engineered to be implemented, reviewed, and operated without quiet, catastrophic bugs.

What should stay in your head
Raft solves the same problem as Paxos — getting unreliable machines to agree on a replicated log (an ordered sequence of commands) and never disagree — but is built around three human-sized ideas. (1) One strong Leader: at any moment at most one node is leader, and all client writes go through it; the leader is the single source of order. (2) Terms: time is divided into numbered terms, each beginning with an election; a term has at most one leader, and the term number acts as a logical clock — anyone who sees a higher term immediately steps down and updates. (3) The Log: each entry carries its command and the term it was created in; the leader appends entries and replicates them, and an entry is committed the moment it's stored on a majority of nodes — after which it's applied to the state machine and can never be lost. Nodes live in three states — Follower (passive, obeys the leader), Candidate (running for leader), Leader — connected by one rule: a follower that stops hearing heartbeats becomes a candidate, wins if a majority votes for it, and leads until it crashes or sees a higher term. Randomized election timeouts make split votes rare (nodes rarely time out together). Two safety rules make it bulletproof: the Log Matching Property (if two logs agree on an entry's index and term, they're identical up to there) and the election restriction (you can't win a vote unless your log is at least as up-to-date as the voter's) — so a new leader always already holds every committed entry. One-line test: could a newly elected leader be missing an entry the old cluster already committed? In Raft, no — the vote wouldn't have gone to a node whose log was behind.

Why Raft Exists

Paxos (Day 31) answered the theoretical question — is consensus possible on unreliable machines? — with a resounding yes. But it left engineers with a practical problem. Single-decree Paxos is elegant, but real systems need Multi-Paxos (a whole log of decisions), plus leader election, log compaction, snapshotting, and cluster membership changes. Lamport's papers specify the core and wave at the rest. The result: almost every production "Paxos" is really a custom, under-documented variant, and the bugs hide in the parts the paper didn't pin down.

Raft's designers made understandability a first-class goal, equal to correctness and performance — because an algorithm nobody can fully follow produces systems nobody can fully trust. They achieved it with two techniques:

🧩 Decomposition

Split consensus into independent sub-problems.

Raft breaks the problem into three nearly-separate pieces you can learn one at a time: leader election, log replication, and safety. You can understand each without holding the others in your head. Paxos mixes them all into one flow.

👑 A strong leader

One node is in charge; data flows one way.

Unlike Paxos, where any node can propose at any time (the source of dueling proposers), Raft elects one leader that is the sole source of new log entries. Entries flow only leader → followers. This single constraint eliminates whole classes of confusing cases.

🎲 Reducing state space

Fewer ways things can happen = easier to reason.

Raft deliberately removes nondeterminism wherever it can — for example, logs only ever flow in one direction and are never allowed to have holes. Randomized timeouts replace intricate coordination for split votes. Fewer moving parts means fewer states to reason about, and fewer places for bugs.

The punchline: Raft gives the identical safety guarantee as Multi-Paxos — a consistent replicated log that tolerates f failures with 2f+1 nodes — but is learnable in an afternoon. That is why nearly every consensus system built since 2014 (etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV, MongoDB's replica sets, Redis's newer coordination) chose Raft. Same power; a fraction of the footguns.

The Three States

Every Raft node is always in exactly one of three states. The genius is how few there are and how simply they connect — the entire protocol is this one small state machine plus the rules for the messages that drive it.

👤 Follower

Passive. The default. Obeys the leader.

Followers never initiate anything. They respond to the leader's AppendEntries (which carries log entries and doubles as the heartbeat) and to candidates' RequestVote. If a follower hears nothing from a leader for its election timeout, it assumes the leader is gone and becomes a candidate.

🙋 Candidate

Running for leader of a new term.

On timing out, a follower becomes a candidate: it increments the term, votes for itself, and sends RequestVote to everyone. If it collects votes from a majority, it becomes leader. If it hears from a legitimate leader (equal or higher term), it steps back to follower. If nobody wins, it times out and tries again in the next term.

👑 Leader

In charge. The only one who writes.

The leader handles all client requests, appends them to its log, and replicates via AppendEntries. It sends empty AppendEntries as heartbeats to stop followers from timing out. It stays leader until it crashes or discovers a higher term — at which point it immediately steps down to follower.

Notice the one escape hatch that keeps the whole system consistent: any node, in any state, that sees a message with a higher term number immediately adopts that term and reverts to follower. The term number is a logical clock, and the highest term always wins. An old leader that was network-partitioned and comes back will see the newer term in the first message it receives and quietly stand down — it can't keep leading in the past. This single rule is why Raft never ends up with two active leaders doing damage.

Leader Election

Raft divides time into terms, numbered 1, 2, 3, … Each term begins with an election and lasts until the next one. A term has at most one leader (sometimes zero, if an election fails). Think of terms as numbered editions of the newspaper, or election cycles — the number only ever goes up, and it lets every node order events in time without a shared clock.

1
A follower times out. It hasn't heard a heartbeat within its election timeout, so it assumes there's no leader. It becomes a candidate, increments the term (say 2 → 3), and votes for itself.
2
It requests votes. The candidate sends RequestVote(term, lastLogIndex, lastLogTerm) to all other nodes.
3
Others vote — at most once per term. A node grants its vote if (a) it hasn't already voted this term, and (b) the candidate's log is at least as up-to-date as its own (the safety restriction — more below). Each node votes for at most one candidate per term, which is why two majorities can't both form.
4
Majority wins. If the candidate collects votes from a majority (including its own), it becomes leader and immediately sends heartbeats to assert authority and stop other timeouts.
5
Nobody wins → try again. If votes split and no one gets a majority, the term ends leaderless. Candidates time out and start a new term — but with freshly randomized timeouts, so this time one is very likely to fire first and win cleanly.

The quietly-genius trick: randomized timeouts

Here's the problem randomization solves. If every follower used the same election timeout, they'd all time out at the same instant when the leader died, all become candidates at once, all split the vote — and then do it again, forever. A livelock, exactly like Paxos's dueling proposers. Raft's fix is almost embarrassingly simple: each node picks its election timeout randomly from a range (typically 150–300 ms). So when the leader dies, one follower's timer almost always fires first, it becomes a candidate and wins a majority before anyone else even wakes up. Split votes become rare, and when they do happen, the next round's fresh random timeouts break the tie. No clever coordination — just a dice roll. It's the kind of solution you only find when you optimize for understandability.

Log Replication

Once there's a leader, the real work begins: agreeing on an ordered log of commands. Every log entry holds two things — the command (e.g. set x = 5) and the term it was created in — and sits at a numbered index. The leader's job is to make every follower's log an identical copy of its own.

1
Client → Leader. A client sends a command. The leader appends it to its own log as a new, uncommitted entry.
2
Leader → Followers. The leader sends AppendEntries with the new entry (plus the index/term of the entry just before it, for the consistency check). Followers append it to their logs and acknowledge.
3
Committed at a majority. Once the entry is stored on a majority of nodes, the leader marks it committed. Commitment is the point of no return: a committed entry will never be lost and will survive every future leader.
4
Apply + respond. The leader applies the committed command to its state machine and responds to the client. It tells followers the new commit index on the next AppendEntries, and they apply it too — so every node's state machine executes the same commands in the same order.

The consistency check and repairing stragglers

Every AppendEntries includes the index and term of the entry immediately before the new ones. A follower refuses the append if its log doesn't match there. When a follower rejects, the leader walks backward — resending from an earlier point — until it finds the last index where their logs agree, then overwrites everything after it with its own entries. This is how a follower that fell behind (or a new leader's followers that have conflicting tail entries) gets forced back into line. Logs only ever flow leader → follower, and the leader never changes its own entries — it only ever appends. That one-directional flow is a huge part of why Raft is easier to reason about than Paxos.

The Safety Rules

Leader election and log replication make Raft work; two safety rules make it correct — they guarantee that a committed entry is never lost, even across crashes and elections. This is the part worth slowing down for.

1. The Log Matching Property

Raft maintains an invariant that sounds almost too strong to be true: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and the same term, then the logs are identical in all entries up through that index. So you can verify two logs agree completely just by checking their last matching (index, term) — everything before it is guaranteed to match. This holds because (a) a leader creates at most one entry per index per term, and (b) the AppendEntries consistency check refuses any append that doesn't line up with the previous entry, so inconsistencies can never silently accumulate.

2. The election restriction (the up-to-date rule)

This is the keystone. When a candidate asks for a vote, it includes the index and term of its last log entry. A voter refuses if its own log is more up-to-date (a higher last term, or the same last term but a longer log). Result: a candidate can only win if its log is at least as up-to-date as a majority of the cluster. And because any committed entry is, by definition, on a majority — and any winning majority overlaps that majority in at least one node (the quorum-overlap magic from Day 31) — the winner is guaranteed to already have every committed entry. A node missing a committed entry simply cannot win. The new leader never needs to "catch up" on committed data; it already has it.

N1(leader) log: 1 2 3 committed through 3 N2 log: 1 2 3 N3 log: 1 2 3 N4 log: 1 2 # lagging N5 log: 1 # lagging more # N1 crashes. An election starts for term 3. N4 → RequestVote(term=3, lastLogIndex=2, lastLogTerm=2) N2 → DENIED # N2's log (len 3) is MORE up-to-date than N4's (len 2) N3 → DENIED # same reason — N4 is missing committed entry 3 # N4 can NOT win: it lacks committed entry 3, so the majority refuses it. # Only N2 or N3 (who HAVE entry 3) can gather a majority and lead. N2 → RequestVote(term=3, lastLogIndex=3, lastLogTerm=2) N3 N4 N5 → GRANTED # N2's log is ≥ theirs → N2 becomes leader # Committed entry 3 survived the crash. The new leader already had it.

There's one subtle rule that trips up even careful implementers (Raft's famous "Figure 8"): a leader may only directly commit entries from its own current term by counting replicas. It must not look at an older-term entry sitting on a majority and declare it committed — doing so can, in a specific interleaving of crashes, let that entry be overwritten later. Instead, older entries get committed indirectly: once the leader commits a new entry from its current term, everything before it (including those older entries) is committed along with it, by the Log Matching Property. You don't need to memorize the counterexample — just remember: commit by counting only for your own term's entries; older ones ride along.

The Hard Parts

Raft is dramatically easier than Paxos, but it isn't free of tradeoffs. Knowing them is what separates "I read the paper" from "I'd run this in production."

The strong leader is both the strength and the bottleneck

A single leader is what makes Raft understandable — but it also means every write funnels through one node. That node's capacity and its network latency to a majority bound the whole cluster's write throughput. You can't scale writes by adding Raft nodes; more nodes make each write slower (bigger majority to wait for). The standard answer is sharding: run many independent Raft groups, each owning a slice of the data, so leadership (and write load) spreads across machines. CockroachDB and TiKV do exactly this — thousands of Raft groups, one per data range.

Leader changes cost a beat

When a leader crashes, the cluster is unavailable for writes until a new leader is elected — typically the election timeout plus a round-trip, often well under a second, but not zero. Tuning matters: too-short timeouts cause spurious elections during brief network blips; too-long timeouts mean slower recovery. This is a real "availability dip on failover" that shows up in tail latency, and it's the practical face of the CAP tradeoff (Day 10) — Raft is a CP system that chooses consistency, pausing writes rather than risking two leaders.

Reads need care, too

Naively, a leader can serve reads from its own state — but a stale leader (one that was partitioned out and doesn't yet know it's been replaced) could serve old data. Correct Raft reads require the leader to confirm it's still leader (e.g. by a heartbeat round-trip to a majority, or a "lease") before answering. Many systems offer a choice: linearizable reads (correct, slower) or follower/stale reads (fast, possibly behind) — a direct consistency-vs-latency knob.

Membership changes are genuinely tricky

Adding or removing nodes while the cluster is live can momentarily create two different majorities (old config vs new config) — a split-brain risk. Raft handles this with joint consensus (a transitional phase where decisions need a majority of both the old and new configurations) or single-node-at-a-time changes. It's the one area where Raft is still fiddly, and a common source of real-world bugs.

The through-line: Raft trades raw write scalability for understandability and a clean consistency guarantee. One leader, a replicated log, numbered terms — simple enough to implement correctly, at the cost of a single-writer bottleneck you solve by running many Raft groups rather than one big one.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"Raft is more powerful / safer than Paxos."It gives the exact same guarantees as Multi-Paxos. Raft's advantage is understandability, not strength — which in practice means fewer implementation bugs.
"The leader makes reads fast and always correct."Not for free. A stale leader (partitioned out, not yet aware it's replaced) can serve old data. Linearizable reads need the leader to re-confirm leadership (lease or heartbeat round-trip) first.
"More Raft nodes = more write throughput."The opposite. All writes funnel through one leader, and more nodes mean a bigger majority to wait for — slower. Scale with many Raft groups (sharding), not a bigger group.
"An entry on a majority is always committed."Only when it's from the leader's current term. A leader must not commit an older-term entry by counting replicas (the Figure-8 subtlety); older entries commit indirectly once a current-term entry commits.
"A new leader might miss committed data and corrupt state."Impossible in Raft. The up-to-date election restriction means a node missing a committed entry can't win — the majority refuses it. The new leader always already holds every committed entry.
"Randomized timeouts are a hack / minor detail."They're load-bearing. Without randomization, nodes time out together, split the vote, and livelock forever. Randomness is Raft's clean answer to the dueling-proposers problem.

Active Recall

❓ A 5-node Raft cluster is in term 4 with N1 as leader. A network partition cuts N1 off with only N2 (2 nodes on one side; N3, N4, N5 on the other). N1 keeps trying to replicate new client writes to its side. On the other side, N3 times out and starts an election. Walk through what happens on both sides — and can the cluster end up with two leaders that both commit data? (click to reveal)
No two leaders can both commit. N1's side (N1, N2): N1 is still "leader" but can only reach N2 — just 2 nodes, short of the majority of 3. So when N1 tries to replicate a write, it never reaches a majority, so the entry is never committed. N1 keeps appending uncommitted entries that go nowhere. The other side (N3, N4, N5): N3 times out, increments the term to 5, requests votes, and gets a majority (3 of 5). It becomes the new leader for term 5 and can commit writes, because it has a real majority. When the partition heals: N1 receives a message carrying term 5 — higher than its term 4 — and by the step-down rule it immediately reverts to follower. Its uncommitted tail entries that conflict with the new leader's log get overwritten by AppendEntries. Net result: exactly one leader ever committed anything (the majority side), no committed data is lost, and the minority side's stranded writes — which were never acknowledged to any client as committed — are correctly discarded. This is the same split-brain that Day 31's Sanjay suffered, now prevented by majority votes plus the highest-term-wins rule.

Real-World Engineering

⚙️ etcd & Kubernetes

etcd, the key-value store that holds all of Kubernetes' cluster state, is built on Raft. Every time Kubernetes agrees on what pods run where, a Raft log entry was committed by a majority of etcd nodes. If you've ever run kubectl apply, you've driven a Raft round. This is where most working engineers actually touch consensus.

🪳 CockroachDB & TiKV

These distributed SQL databases shard data into thousands of ranges, each its own independent Raft group with its own leader. This is the textbook answer to the single-leader bottleneck: leadership (and write load) is spread across the whole fleet, so the database scales horizontally while every individual range stays strongly consistent.

🧭 HashiCorp Consul & Nomad

Consul (service discovery, Day 27's neighbor) and Nomad (scheduling) use Raft for their consistent server state via the widely-used hashicorp/raft library. When Consul says "this is the authoritative list of healthy services," a Raft majority agreed on it. The same library powers countless other Go systems.

🍃 MongoDB Replica Sets

MongoDB's replication and automatic failover use a Raft-like protocol: one primary accepts writes and replicates an oplog to secondaries; when the primary fails, secondaries hold an election (with terms and an up-to-date-log restriction) to pick a new one. The concepts you learned today map almost one-to-one.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: Why was Raft created when Paxos already solved consensus?
A: For understandability. Paxos gives the same guarantees but is famously hard to understand and implement correctly, so real systems ship subtle bugs. Raft was designed from scratch to be followable by a human — which makes implementations correct.
Q: Name Raft's three server states and the transitions between them.
A: Follower → (election timeout, no heartbeat) → Candidate → (wins a majority of votes) → Leader. Any node seeing a higher term immediately reverts to Follower.
Q: What problem do randomized election timeouts solve, and how?
A: Split votes / livelock. If all nodes timed out together they'd all run, split the vote, and repeat forever. Random timeouts (e.g. 150–300 ms) mean one node almost always fires first and wins before others wake — and a rare split is broken by the next round's fresh random values.
Q: What does it mean for a log entry to be "committed"?
A: It has been stored on a majority of nodes. A committed entry is permanent — it will never be lost and will survive every future leader. Only then is it applied to the state machine and acknowledged to the client.
Q: What is the election restriction, and why does it matter?
A: A voter refuses a candidate whose log is less up-to-date than its own. Since any committed entry is on a majority and any winning majority overlaps it, the winner is guaranteed to already hold every committed entry — so a new leader never loses committed data.

If you only remember one thing: Raft is Paxos's guarantee, rebuilt to be understandable — one strong Leader, numbered Terms, and a replicated Log everyone copies in order. A Follower that stops hearing heartbeats becomes a Candidate, wins if a majority votes for it, and leads until it crashes or sees a higher term (then it steps down instantly). Writes flow one way, leader → followers; an entry is committed once it's on a majority, and from then it's permanent. Two safety rules make it bulletproof: the Log Matching Property (same index + term ⇒ identical logs up to there) and the up-to-date election restriction (you can't win a vote with a log behind the voter's) — so a new leader always already has every committed entry. Randomized timeouts quietly dissolve the split-vote livelock that plagued Paxos. The price is a single-leader write bottleneck, solved by running many Raft groups. This is the consensus you'll actually meet — etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, Kubernetes.
