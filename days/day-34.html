The Saga Pattern
Undo-Forward Instead of Lock-and-Block

Yesterday, Two-Phase Commit (Day 33) bought us real atomicity across databases — and we felt the price: participants hold locks across the network, a dead coordinator blocks everyone, and you simply cannot enlist Stripe or an email provider into your two-phase commit. For a chain of independent microservices, 2PC is often a non-starter. So today we take the opposite stance. A Saga breaks one distributed transaction into a sequence of local transactions — each service commits to its own database immediately, no shared lock, no global pause. The catch is obvious: once step 2 has committed and step 3 fails, there is no magic rewind. So every step gets a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it — cancel the car, refund the hotel, refund the flight — run in reverse. The trade is stark and worth saying out loud: a saga gives you ACD, not ACID. You keep atomicity (eventually, via compensation) and availability, but you drop isolation — and that missing letter is where all the hard, interesting bugs live. We'll build sagas from scratch, step through one in a simulator (including a failure that compensates in reverse and an orchestrator crash that recovers from its log), meet the two coordination styles — orchestration vs choreography — and face the isolation anomalies head-on.

Picture this

You're booking a trip, and it takes three separate companies: an airline, a hotel, and a car-rental agency. There is no master "trip" button — no shared system that books all three in one atomic click (just like you can't run a two-phase commit across three companies' databases). So you walk down a row of three booking desks, one at a time. At the airline desk you book the flight; they take your money and hand you a confirmed ticket. That booking is done — committed, real, their system already changed. You carry your ticket to the hotel desk and book a room: another confirmed receipt, another committed fact. Then you reach the car desk — and they're sold out.

Now the crucial part. You can't un-happen the flight and hotel bookings. Time doesn't rewind; their systems already committed and already took your money. What you can do is walk back down the row in reverse and run an undo for each: cancel the hotel (they refund the night), cancel the flight (they refund the fare). These cancellations are not a "rollback" — they are new, forward actions. The bookings genuinely existed for a while; the refunds semantically undo them. When you're done walking back, you're whole again — as if the trip was never booked, reached not by rewinding but by a careful sequence of do, do, undo, undo.

Burn in the image: a row of booking desks you move through one at a time, each handing you a committed receipt — and when one desk fails, you walk back down the row tearing up the previous receipts with refunds, in reverse. That is a saga: a chain of local commits, each with a compensating undo, and backward recovery when a later step fails. And notice the uncomfortable bit — for a few minutes, that flight and hotel were really booked and visible to the world. There was no isolation. Hold onto that; it's the whole second half of the lesson.

The Story

Priya owned the Checkout service at a fast-growing online store. In the old days it was a cozy monolith: one database, and placing an order was a single ACID transaction — insert the order, charge the card, decrement inventory, create a shipment — all four writes committed together or not at all. The A and I in ACID (Day 9) were simply there, free, and she never thought about them.

Then the company did what growing companies do: it split the monolith into services. Orders, Payments, Inventory, and Shipping each became its own service with its own database. Suddenly placing an order spanned four databases, and Priya's single transaction was gone. She'd just learned 2PC (Day 33), so she reached for it first — and it went badly. The payment step called Stripe, which has no interest in joining anyone's two-phase commit. Worse, when Inventory got slow during a flash sale, every checkout blocked holding locks, waiting on the prepare phase, and the whole store ground to a halt. 2PC had turned four independent services into one fragile, lock-step chain whose availability was that of its slowest member.

A principal engineer sketched a Saga on the whiteboard. "Stop trying to commit everything at the same instant. Let each service commit locally, immediately — and give each step an undo." The order flow became a sequence: create the order (pending) → charge the card → reserve inventory → schedule shipping → mark the order confirmed. Each step committed on its own and published an event. And each had a compensating transaction: if shipping couldn't be scheduled, release the inventory, refund the charge, and cancel the order — undoing, in reverse, everything that had already happened. No locks held across services, no global pause. When Inventory got slow now, checkouts didn't freeze; they just took the next step when they could. Availability came back.

Then the bug reports started, and they were weird. A customer complained their order "appeared for a second, then vanished." Another was double-charged. A third saw an item go out of stock, then back in, within the same minute. Priya had hit the saga's hard truth: there is no isolation. While a saga is mid-flight, its half-finished state is visible — a reserved-then-released seat looked to another shopper like a real stockout (a "dirty read"); a retried compensation that wasn't idempotent refunded, then refunded the charge a second time. Her fixes were the real curriculum of this lesson: make every step and every compensation idempotent (Day 25) so retries are safe; add a semantic lock — an order.status = PENDING flag that tells everyone else "this isn't final yet, don't trust it"; and reorder the steps so the truly irreversible one (capturing the payment, emailing "your order shipped!") came last, after the point where the saga was guaranteed to succeed.

Her takeaway, written on the whiteboard and never erased: a saga trades the comfort of an instantaneous, isolated, all-or-nothing transaction for the availability and loose coupling you actually need across services — but you pay in eventual consistency and in bugs that only exist because, for a little while, the half-done truth was visible to everyone.

What should stay in your head
A Saga is a distributed transaction implemented as a sequence of local transactions T₁ → T₂ → … → Tₙ, each committing to a single service's database immediately — no shared lock, no global coordinator holding everyone hostage. Because each step commits on its own, there is nothing to "roll back" once a later step fails; instead, every step Tᵢ is paired with a compensating transaction Cᵢ that semantically undoes it (refund the charge, release the reservation, cancel the booking). If step Tₖ fails, the saga performs backward recovery: run Cₖ₋₁, Cₖ₋₂, … C₁ in reverse order. If instead a step just has a transient error, the saga can do forward recovery: retry Tₖ until it succeeds. A durable saga log records what has happened so a crashed coordinator can resume rather than freeze. Two coordination styles: orchestration (a central orchestrator issues commands and decides when to compensate) and choreography (services react to one another's events, no central brain). The defining trade: a saga gives you ACD, not ACID — it drops Isolation. While a saga is in flight, its intermediate state is visible to everyone else, so you get anomalies (dirty reads, lost updates, non-repeatable reads) that you must manage with semantic locks (a "pending" flag), commutative updates, and pivot-transaction ordering (put irreversible steps last). Compensations must be idempotent and effectively always succeed — you can't compensate a compensation. One-line test: 2PC prevents inconsistency by locking and blocking; a saga permits a temporary inconsistency and cleans it up with compensations — it chooses availability over isolation.

Why This Problem Exists

We ended Day 33 with 2PC's bill: participants hold locks from the moment they vote YES until the verdict arrives (across network round-trips); a crashed coordinator blocks everyone in-doubt; the whole transaction is only as available as its least available participant; and it needs every participant to speak one transaction protocol (XA) — which third-party APIs and most microservices simply don't. For money moving between two databases you own, that price can be worth paying. For a chain of independent services calling Stripe and an email provider, it's a non-starter.

So we ask a different question. Instead of "how do I make all these services commit at the same instant?" (2PC's question), we ask "what if I let each service commit on its own, right away — and accept that I'll have to clean up by hand if a later step fails?" That reframing is the saga. It gives up the idea of a single atomic instant and replaces it with a sequence of ordinary local commits, plus an undo plan for when the sequence can't finish.

The cost of that reframing is precise and worth naming. 2PC is a CP protocol (Day 10): it prefers to pause rather than show you an inconsistent state. A saga is the AP-flavored answer: it stays available and loosely-coupled by permitting a temporary inconsistency — a window where the flight is booked but the hotel isn't yet, where an order exists but hasn't been paid — and promising to converge (either forward to success or backward to a clean compensated state). This is eventual consistency (Days 10–12) made operational.

The through-line from Day 33: 2PC and the saga are the two ends of the same spectrum. One buys isolation and atomic-instant at the cost of locking and blocking; the other buys availability and loose coupling at the cost of isolation and immediate consistency. Choosing between them is one of the most common real system-design decisions you'll make.

How a Saga Works

A saga has three moving parts: the forward transactions, their compensations, and a durable log that tracks progress so the saga can survive a crash.

The pieces

1
Local transactions T₁…Tₙ. Each step is a normal, ordinary ACID transaction inside one service's database. It commits immediately and (usually) publishes an event or returns a result. There is no cross-service lock and no "prepared but uncommitted" state — the write is simply done.
2
Compensating transactions C₁…Cₙ₋₁. For each step that can be undone, you define a compensation that semantically reverses it: chargeCard → refundCard, reserveStock → releaseStock, createOrder → cancelOrder. A compensation is itself a local transaction — it is not a database rollback; the original already committed and may have been seen.
3
Backward recovery. If Tₖ fails and the saga decides to abort, it runs the compensations for all already-completed steps, in reverse order: Cₖ₋₁, then Cₖ₋₂, … down to C₁. Reverse order matters — you release the inventory before you cancel the order it belonged to, just as you cancel the hotel before the flight.
4
Forward recovery. Not every failure means abort. If a step hits a transient error (a timeout, a service briefly down), the saga can simply retry it until it succeeds and continue forward. This is why steps and compensations must be idempotent (Day 25): the same step may run more than once.
5
The saga log. Before and after each step, the coordinator records progress durably ("T₁ done, T₂ done, T₃ started…"). If the coordinator crashes, a replacement reads the log and knows exactly where to resume — continue forward, or run the remaining compensations. This is the saga's answer to 2PC's blocking: a crash pauses the saga but never freezes other transactions, because nothing is locked.
A saga as code (orchestrated order checkout)
def place_order_saga(cart): log = SagaLog(cart.id) # durable — survives a crash try: order = orders.create(cart, status="PENDING"); log.done("order") # T1 (semantic lock: PENDING) pay = payments.authorize(cart.total); log.done("pay") # T2 inv = inventory.reserve(cart.items); log.done("inv") # T3 ship = shipping.schedule(order); log.done("ship") # T4 (pivot) payments.capture(pay) # irreversible step, LAST orders.confirm(order) # clear the semantic lock except StepFailed as e: # backward recovery — compensate completed steps in REVERSE if log.has("inv"): inventory.release(cart.items) ↩ C3 if log.has("pay"): payments.void(pay) ↩ C2 if log.has("order"): orders.cancel(order) ↩ C1

Orchestration vs Choreography

There are two ways to decide who drives the saga — who calls each step and who triggers the compensations. It's the same distinction we met in Event-Driven Architecture (Day 21), now applied to transactions.

🎬 Orchestration

A central orchestrator is the brain. It calls service 1, waits, calls service 2, and so on — and if a step fails, it decides to run the compensations. The whole workflow lives in one place.

Good: the flow is explicit and easy to see; one place to reason about failure and compensation; great for complex, many-step sagas.

Risk: the orchestrator is a new component to build, run, and make fault-tolerant (it must persist its state and recover — see the saga log); a thin orchestrator can drift toward being a "god service."

🎭 Choreography

No central brain. Each service listens for events and reacts: Order publishes OrderCreated → Payment hears it, charges, publishes PaymentDone → Inventory hears that and reserves. Compensations are also events (OrderFailed → everyone undoes).

Good: no extra component, maximum loose coupling, services stay autonomous; natural fit for a few simple steps.

Risk: with many services the flow becomes invisible event-spaghetti no one can trace; cyclic dependencies creep in; "what's the whole workflow?" has no single answer. Observability (Day 26) becomes essential.

Rule of thumb: choreography for a couple of steps, orchestration once the workflow gets complex. Many large systems end up orchestrated precisely because, at step 7 of a saga with branches and compensations, you want one place that knows the whole story — which is exactly what tools like Temporal, AWS Step Functions, and Netflix Conductor provide.

The Missing Letter: No Isolation

Here is the saga's defining weakness — the one that produces its strangest bugs. A single-database ACID transaction gives you four guarantees. A saga, built from independent local commits, gives you only three: Atomicity (eventually, via compensation), Consistency, and Durability. The letter it drops is I — Isolation. Because each step commits immediately and visibly, other transactions can observe a saga that is only half-finished.

👀 Dirty reads

Another transaction reads data a saga wrote but that later gets compensated away. A shopper sees "1 seat left" because a mid-flight saga reserved it — then that saga aborts and releases it. The read was of a state that "never really happened."

🔁 Lost updates

Two sagas touch the same record concurrently; one's write silently overwrites the other's because neither held a lock for the duration. The classic account-balance race, now spread across services.

😵 Non-repeatable reads

A saga reads a value early, and by the time a later step reads it again, another saga has changed it — so the saga makes two decisions on two different versions of the same fact.

Countermeasures — getting isolation back by hand

You can't get true isolation without locks, but you can tame the anomalies with design (this is the real craft of writing sagas):

🔒
Semantic lock. Add an application-level flag — status = PENDING — on any record a saga is still working on. Other transactions see the flag and either wait, skip it, or treat it as "not final." It's a lock you enforce in your logic, not the database's. (Priya's order-status fix.)
➕
Commutative updates. Design operations so order doesn't matter — e.g. increment(balance, +10) / increment(balance, −10) instead of set(balance, x). Then a compensation that arrives "late" still produces the right total, and concurrent updates don't clobber each other.
🎯
Pivot transaction + reorder. Split your steps into compensatable (can be undone), the pivot (the go/no-go step — once it commits, the saga will finish), and retriable (must eventually succeed, and come after the pivot). Put irreversible steps (capture payment, send "shipped!" email, launch the rocket) after the pivot, so you never have to undo something you can't.
🔂
Idempotency + re-read. Make every step and compensation idempotent (Day 25) so retries are safe, and have steps re-read current state instead of trusting a value read earlier in the saga.

That last point — the pivot transaction — is the single most useful trick a senior engineer brings to saga design. "Can this step be undone?" decides where it goes. Everything undoable goes first; the one irreversible decision becomes the pivot; everything after the pivot is something you commit to making succeed, retrying forever if you must. Get the ordering right and most isolation pain disappears.

The Hard Parts

Sagas buy availability and loose coupling, but they are genuinely harder to get right than a single transaction. Know the costs before you reach for one.

Some things can't be compensated

A refund undoes a charge; nothing undoes a sent email, a launched missile, or a physical package on a truck. For these you either accept a semantic compensation (send a correction/apology email, recall the shipment) or — far better — order the saga so the irreversible step is the pivot or comes after it, where you'll never need to undo it. "Don't send the confirmation email until the saga is guaranteed to succeed" is this rule in one sentence.

Compensations must effectively always succeed

You can run a compensation for a forward step — but what compensates the compensation? Nothing. So compensations must be designed to be idempotent and retriable until they succeed: a refund that fails is retried, not abandoned. This is why compensations are usually simple, well-tested, and backed by a retry-forever mechanism (a dead-letter queue, a durable workflow engine). A compensation that can permanently fail is a design bug.

No isolation → anomalies you must design around

As the previous section covered: dirty reads, lost updates, and non-repeatable reads are possible by construction. Semantic locks, commutative operations, and careful ordering are not optional polish — they're part of making a saga correct. The absence of isolation is the price of the absence of locking.

Observability and debugging get harder

A monolithic transaction has one stack trace. A saga's story is spread across four services, a message bus, a saga log, and time. "Why is this order stuck?" requires correlation IDs and distributed tracing (Day 26) to answer. Choreographed sagas are the worst here — no single place describes the whole flow — which is a major reason complex sagas migrate to orchestration.

When NOT to use a saga

If all the data lives in one database, use a plain local ACID transaction — don't invent a saga to solve a problem you don't have. Sagas are for when a business operation genuinely spans multiple services/databases and you need availability and loose coupling more than you need isolation. If you truly need cross-database atomicity with isolation and can harden the coordinator, that's 2PC's job (Day 33). The art is knowing which problem you actually have.

The through-line: a saga is not a weaker transaction — it's a different deal. You trade the database's automatic isolation and instant atomicity for availability, loose coupling, and the ability to include services you don't control — and you pay for it with compensations, idempotency, semantic locks, and careful step ordering that you now own, by hand.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"A compensation is just a rollback."No. A rollback erases an uncommitted change as if it never happened. A compensation is a new, forward transaction that semantically reverses a step that already committed and was already visible (and may have had side effects). You refund a charge; you don't un-charge it.
"A saga is just a slower ACID transaction."It's ACD, not ACID — it drops Isolation. Its intermediate state is visible to others, so dirty reads, lost updates, and non-repeatable reads are possible by design and must be managed with semantic locks and ordering.
"Choreography is always simpler, so prefer it."Simpler for 2–3 steps. At scale it becomes event-spaghetti with no single place describing the flow, cyclic dependencies, and brutal debugging. Complex sagas usually want orchestration.
"If a compensation fails, that's fine — just abort."There is nothing to compensate a compensation with. Compensations must be idempotent and retried until they succeed. A compensation that can permanently fail is a design bug, not an accepted outcome.
"Order of steps doesn't matter; just undo everything on failure."Order is everything. Put irreversible steps (send email, capture payment) after the pivot, and run compensations in reverse. Bad ordering forces you to "undo" things you physically can't.
"Use sagas everywhere instead of transactions."If the data is in one database, use a plain local ACID transaction. Sagas exist to cross service/database boundaries where a single transaction is impossible — not to replace transactions you can still have.

Active Recall

❓ An e-commerce checkout saga runs: (1) create order, (2) charge the card, (3) reserve inventory, (4) schedule shipping. Step 4 fails because the shipping provider is down and won't recover. Walk through exactly what the saga does, in what order, and name two things that could go wrong because a saga has no isolation. (click to reveal)
The saga performs backward recovery — compensations in reverse order. Steps 1–3 already committed (order created, card charged, inventory reserved), each in its own database; there's nothing to "roll back." So the saga runs: C₃ release the inventory → C₂ refund (or void) the charge → C₁ cancel the order — reverse of the order they ran in. Each compensation is a new local transaction and must be idempotent, because the saga might retry it (e.g. if the refund call times out). The customer ends up fully refunded with no order, as if nothing happened — reached by undo-forward, not rewind.

Two things that can go wrong from the lack of isolation: (a) a dirty read — while inventory was reserved in step 3, another shopper saw the item as out of stock and gave up, even though the reservation was about to be released; the stockout they saw "never really happened." (b) a double refund / non-idempotent compensation — if C₂ runs twice (retry after a timeout) and isn't idempotent, the customer is refunded twice. The fixes are the lesson's countermeasures: a semantic lock (order.status = PENDING, item shown as "reserved, not sold"), idempotent compensations keyed by a transaction ID (Day 25), and putting the truly irreversible step (capturing payment, emailing "shipped!") after the pivot so it's never compensated.

Real-World Engineering

⏱️ Uber Cadence / Temporal

Uber built Cadence (now the open-source Temporal) specifically to run orchestrated sagas as durable, crash-proof workflows. You write the saga as ordinary code; the engine persists every step to a durable log and resumes after any crash — exactly the "saga log" from this lesson, productized. Compensations are just more workflow steps. It's the modern default for complex orchestration.

🪜 AWS Step Functions

Amazon's managed orchestrator: you define a state machine of steps, and Step Functions calls each, handles retries, and runs catch/compensation branches on failure. It's the cloud-native way to turn "call these five services, undo on failure" into a visible, debuggable diagram instead of event-spaghetti.

🎬 Netflix Conductor

Netflix open-sourced Conductor to orchestrate the many-step workflows behind encoding, content setup, and more — a central engine that sequences tasks across services and handles failure/compensation centrally, chosen precisely because choreography didn't scale to their workflow complexity.

🧩 Eventuate & Seata

Chris Richardson's Eventuate Tram (which popularized the saga pattern for microservices) and Alibaba's Seata provide saga frameworks with both orchestration and choreography, transactional outbox for reliable event publishing, and built-in compensation handling. The transactional outbox — write the event in the same local transaction as the data — is how sagas avoid the "committed but event never published" failure.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What is a saga, in one sentence?
A: A distributed transaction implemented as a sequence of local transactions, each committing to one service's database immediately, where each step has a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it if a later step fails.
Q: How is a compensating transaction different from a rollback?
A: A rollback erases an uncommitted change as if it never happened. A compensation is a new forward transaction that reverses a step which already committed and was visible (e.g. refund a charge). The original really happened; the compensation semantically cancels it.
Q: Which ACID property does a saga give up, and what does that cause?
A: Isolation (a saga is ACD, not ACID). Because each step commits visibly, other transactions can see half-finished sagas → dirty reads, lost updates, non-repeatable reads. Managed with semantic locks, commutative updates, and careful ordering.
Q: Orchestration vs choreography — what's the difference and when do you use each?
A: Orchestration = a central brain issues commands and triggers compensations (explicit, best for complex sagas). Choreography = services react to each other's events, no central brain (loose, best for 2–3 simple steps; becomes event-spaghetti at scale).
Q: What is a "pivot transaction" and why does it matter?
A: The go/no-go step: before it, steps are compensatable; after it, the saga will finish (retriable steps). Put irreversible actions (capture payment, send "shipped!" email) at or after the pivot so you never have to undo something you physically can't.

If you only remember one thing: a saga turns one distributed transaction into a sequence of local commits, each with a compensating undo — on failure it runs those compensations in reverse (undo-forward, not rollback), and it recovers from a durable saga log instead of blocking. It's the opposite trade from 2PC: you gain availability and loose coupling (and the ability to include services you don't control), and you give up isolation — so a saga is ACD, not ACID, its half-done state is visible, and the real work is managing that with idempotent compensations, semantic locks, and putting irreversible steps after the pivot. Use a saga when a business operation genuinely spans services and you need availability over isolation; use a plain local transaction when the data is in one database; use 2PC when you truly need cross-database atomicity with isolation and can harden the coordinator.
