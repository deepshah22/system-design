Design a URL Shortener
The First Time Our Tools Build a Real System

For 37 days we've been collecting parts — DNS, load balancers, caches, databases, hashing, replication, estimation. Today we stop collecting and start building. The URL shortener is the "hello world" of system design: small enough to hold in your head, deep enough to touch nearly every tool we own. The job sounds trivial — turn a long link like https://example.com/articles/2026/distributed-systems?ref=newsletter into something tiny like sho.rt/4Gf2a that redirects back — and that's exactly why it's the perfect first design: underneath the simplicity sit real, revealing decisions. How do you mint billions of short codes that never collide? The elegant answer is to take a single ever-increasing counter and base62-encode it, so a 7-character code addresses 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion links. How do you serve a read-heavy redirect in a few milliseconds? Cache and CDN. And the sneaky-important one: should a redirect be a 301 or a 302? You'll scope it, size it, draw the architecture, and type real URLs into a working shortener — the way you would in an interview or a design doc.

Picture this

You arrive at a grand theater on a winter night in a big, bulky overcoat. You can't carry it to your seat, so you hand it to the cloakroom. The attendant doesn't label your coat with a description of it — "long black wool coat, scarf in left pocket, button missing" — that would be absurd to write and absurd to look up. Instead there's a little ticket machine that spits out the next number in sequence — 1, 2, 3, … — stamped on a tiny stub. Your coat goes on hook #4,815, and you walk away with a stub reading 4815 in your pocket. The whole bulky coat has been replaced, in your hands, by a tiny token.

Here's the clever bit. The cloakroom doesn't hand out tickets like "4815" — too long once there are millions of coats. It writes the number in a compact alphabet that uses digits and letters, so the same hook number fits in two or three characters instead of five. And because the ticket machine only ever counts up and never reuses a number, no two coats can ever get the same ticket — collisions are impossible by construction, not by luck. When you come back, you don't describe your coat; you just present the stub. The attendant reads the number, walks straight to that one hook, and hands your coat back in seconds. Looking a coat up by its ticket is instant; searching for a coat without a ticket would mean checking every hook.

That cloakroom is a URL shortener. The bulky coat is your long URL. The ticket machine is a counter that mints a fresh number for every new link. The compact alphabet on the stub is base62 — the trick that turns a big number into a short code. The hooks are a key→value store mapping each short code to its long URL. Hand over a coat → get a short ticket (that's shorten). Present the ticket → get your coat back fast (that's redirect). Keep that picture; the entire design is just making this cloakroom work for billions of coats at once.

The Story

Nadia got a Friday-afternoon request from the marketing team: "Our campaign links are enormous — full of tracking parameters — and they won't fit in a printed flyer or a tweet. Can you build us a little service that makes short links?" Easy, she thought. She'd have a prototype by Monday.

Her first idea was the obvious one: hash the URL. Run the long URL through MD5, take the first 6 characters of the hex, done — same URL always produces the same short code, no counter to manage. It worked in the demo. Then two problems surfaced almost immediately. First, 6 hex characters is only 16⁶ ≈ 16 million possibilities, and by the birthday paradox she started hitting collisions — two different long URLs truncating to the same short code — long before 16 million links, which meant one campaign's short link could silently redirect to a different campaign's page. Catastrophic. Second, the codes were ugly hex and she couldn't honor marketing's request for a custom alias like sho.rt/spring-sale.

A senior engineer wandered over. "Stop hashing the content," he said. "You don't need the code to depend on the URL at all. You need it to be unique and short. So just count." He sketched it: keep one auto-incrementing counter; every new URL grabs the next integer; base62-encode that integer — digits plus upper- and lower-case letters — into a short string, and store code → long URL. "Counter value 1,000,000,000 becomes a six-character code. Each number is used exactly once, so two URLs cannot collide. And since a 7-character code covers 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion links, you'll never run out."

It was beautiful — and then it broke at scale, in a new way. The single counter lived in one database row, and every single write in the whole system had to go through that one row to get the next number. It became a bottleneck and a single point of failure: if that database hiccuped, nobody anywhere could create a link. So Nadia did the last move — she distributed the counting. Instead of one global counter handed out one integer at a time, a key-generation service handed each application server a whole range of a thousand numbers at once (or used Redis INCRBY, or a Snowflake-style ID). Each server base62-encoded IDs from its own range with no coordination, and when it ran low it grabbed another range. Meanwhile the reads — people clicking links — dwarfed the writes a hundred to one, so she put a cache in front of the store and pushed hot redirects to a CDN. By Monday the "little service" had quietly become a distributed system — and Nadia realized she'd just used almost every tool from the last five weeks.

What should stay in your head
A URL shortener is fundamentally two operations over one giant key→value map. Write (shorten): given a long URL, mint a short, globally-unique key and store key → longURL. Read (redirect): given the key, look up the long URL and send an HTTP redirect — and this path is ~100× more frequent, so it must be fast. The elegant core is key generation: take a single ever-increasing counter and base62-encode it (alphabet 0-9 A-Z a-z, 62 symbols). Because each counter value is used exactly once, codes never collide — by construction, not by luck; and because 62ᴸ grows fast, a mere 7 characters covers ≈ 3.5 trillion links. Everything else is scaling the two paths with tools you already know: size it with back-of-envelope estimation (Day 30), route with DNS + load balancers (Day 5), store in a key-value database partitioned by consistent hashing (Day 14), and absorb the read flood with a cache (Day 7) and a CDN. One-line test: a URL shortener is a globally-unique-key generator bolted to a read-optimized key-value lookup — get the key generation and the read-path caching right and the rest follows.

Why This Problem Exists

Long URLs are everywhere and they're genuinely a problem. A modern link carries a path, query parameters, tracking tags, and session tokens, and can run to hundreds of characters. That breaks in the real world: it won't fit in a 160-character SMS or a character-limited tweet; it wraps into an unclickable mess in email; it's impossible to read aloud on a podcast or print on a flyer or billboard as a QR-free fallback; and a raw link reveals nothing you can measure. A short link solves all of these — sho.rt/4Gf2a is tiny, memorable, printable, and (because every click flows through your service) gives you a natural place to count clicks, detect abuse, and expire links.

So the product is simple, but it forces two hard engineering questions that recur in almost every system you'll ever build. First: how do you mint a unique identifier billions of times without collisions and without a central bottleneck? That's the key-generation problem, and it shows up again for every ID in every distributed system. Second: how do you serve an enormously read-heavy lookup with low latency and high availability? People create a link once but click it thousands of times, so reads outnumber writes by roughly 100:1 — the design must bend entirely toward making that read path fast and cheap. The URL shortener is the smallest system that makes you answer both, which is exactly why it's the classic first design.

The two operations, and their wildly different traffic
# WRITE path — rare. Someone creates a short link. POST /urls { longUrl } → mint unique code, store code→longUrl, return sho.rt/<code> # ~1k/s # READ path — constant. Someone clicks a short link. GET /<code> → look up longUrl by code, HTTP 301/302 redirect # ~100k/s ← design for THIS # reads outnumber writes ~100:1 → the whole architecture bends toward a fast, cacheable redirect.

The Design, Step by Step

This is the first time we walk a full design, so let's do it the way you would in an interview or a design doc: scope it, size it, sketch the API, then draw the boxes. Resist the urge to jump to a diagram — the first two steps decide everything.

Step 1 — Scope the requirements

✅ Functional

Shorten: long URL → short code.

Redirect: short code → original URL.

Nice-to-have: custom aliases, expiry/TTL, click analytics.

🎯 Non-functional

Highly available — a broken redirect breaks everyone's links.

Low-latency redirects — a few ms; it's in the user's critical path.

Scalable & durable — billions of links, lose none.

🤔 Clarify early

Codes unguessable, or is sequential fine?

Read:write ratio? (≈100:1).

Do links expire? Analytics needed?

Step 2 — Size it (back-of-the-envelope, Day 30)

Numbers choose the architecture. Assume 100 million new links per day and a 100:1 read:write ratio.

# traffic (day ≈ 100,000 seconds) writes = 100,000,000 / 100,000 = ~1,000 writes/sec reads = 1,000 × 100 = ~100,000 reads/sec peak = ~2× average = ~200,000 reads/sec ← provision for this # storage over 5 years, ~500 bytes per record (code + long URL + metadata) records = 100,000,000 × 365 × 5 = ~182 billion links storage = 182e9 × 500 bytes = ~90 TB # → must shard (Day 13/14) # key space — how long must a code be? need = 182e9 codes → 62^6 ≈ 56.8e9 (too few), 62^7 ≈ 3.5e12 (plenty) → use 7 chars

Those few lines already told us: ~200K reads/sec means we must cache and load-balance; ~90 TB means we must shard the store; and 182 billion links means a 7-character base62 code. We haven't drawn a single box yet and the shape of the system is decided.

Step 3 — Sketch the API

POST /api/urls request { "longUrl": "https://…", "customAlias": "spring-sale"?, "expiresAt": "2027-01-01"? } response 201 { "shortUrl": "https://sho.rt/4Gf2a" } GET /{code} response 301 Moved Permanently (or 302 Found) Location: https://… # the redirect

Two endpoints. The POST is the write path; the GET /{code} is the whole product for 99% of traffic — a lookup and a redirect header. (This is a clean REST shape from our API lessons: a resource collection you POST to, and a short resolver route.)

Step 4 — Draw the architecture

Now the boxes. The write path mints a key and stores it; the read path resolves a key as fast as possible. Both sit behind DNS and a load balancer.

1
DNS → Load Balancer (Day 5). sho.rt resolves to a load balancer that spreads both writes and reads across a fleet of stateless application servers.
2
App servers (stateless). On a write they ask the ID generator for a unique number, base62-encode it, and write code → longUrl. On a read they resolve the code — cache first, store second.
3
ID generator / key-gen service. Hands out unique integers without becoming a bottleneck — Redis INCRBY, per-server ranges, or Snowflake-style IDs (the heart of this design, next section).
4
Key-value store (sharded). The source of truth: code → longUrl, partitioned by code with consistent hashing (Day 14) so ~90 TB spreads across many nodes and lookups are O(1).
5
Cache (Day 7) + CDN. A Redis cache holds the hot 20% of codes (the 80/20 rule), and a CDN can serve very popular redirects from the edge — absorbing the 100:1 read flood before it ever touches the store.

The Heart: How to Generate Short Keys

Everything above is plumbing you already know. This is the decision that makes a URL shortener interesting: how do you produce the short code? There are three real strategies, and the choice is a genuine tradeoff.

StrategyHow it worksThe catch
Hash the URL
e.g. MD5 → first 7 chars		 Run the long URL through a hash, keep the first few characters. Same URL → same code, no counter to manage, naturally dedups identical URLs. Collisions. Truncating a hash means two different URLs can map to the same code (birthday paradox); you must detect and re-hash, adding reads/complexity. Codes aren't customizable.
Counter + base62
the classic answer		 Keep an ever-increasing integer; each new URL takes the next value; base62-encode it. Each number is used once, so codes are unique by construction and as short as possible. A single counter is a bottleneck/SPOF, and sequential codes are guessable (you can enumerate links). Fix with a distributed counter; fix guessability by encoding with a shuffled alphabet or mixing in bits.
Pre-generated random keys
Key-Generation Service		 A separate service generates billions of random, unique 7-char keys ahead of time into a "available keys" table; app servers grab unused ones. Unguessable and fast, but needs a service to generate, store, and hand out keys without giving the same one twice (used-vs-available bookkeeping, often in ranges).

Why base62, concretely

A short code is just a number written in a bigger alphabet. base62 uses all 10 digits plus 26 uppercase plus 26 lowercase letters = 62 symbols per character. Each extra character multiplies the keyspace by 62, so capacity climbs exponentially with length:

encode(n): # integer → base62 string ALPHABET = "0-9 A-Z a-z" # 62 symbols s = "" while n > 0: s = ALPHABET[n % 62] + s # take the remainder as a digit n = n // 62 return s # capacity by length — why 7 is the sweet spot 62^5 = 916 million 62^6 = 56.8 billion 62^7 = 3.52 trillion ← 7 chars, human-friendly, covers 180B+ links for ~96 years at 100M/day 62^8 = 218.3 trillion
✔ Counter + base62 gives collision-free codes  — because each integer is used exactly once, two URLs cannot receive the same code. No collision checks, no retries. 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion in 7 characters.
✘ But a naive single counter is a bottleneck  — every write serializes on one row. Distribute it: hand each server a range of IDs (e.g. 1–1000, 1001–2000), or use Redis INCRBY 1000, or a Snowflake-style ID (timestamp + machine-id + sequence) so servers mint unique IDs with zero coordination.

Notice a lovely property we get for free: short links are immutable. Once 4Gf2a → longUrl is written, it never changes. Immutable data is the easiest thing in the world to cache — no invalidation headaches — which is exactly why the read path can lean so hard on caching and CDNs. Day 7's hardest problem (cache invalidation) barely exists here.

Scaling the Reads (and the 301-vs-302 Trap)

With ~200K reads/sec at peak, the redirect path is the whole ballgame. Three layers absorb it, each catching traffic before it reaches the next:

①
CDN / edge — the hottest links (a viral campaign) can be served as redirects straight from edge locations near the user, never touching your servers.
②
Cache (Redis) — by the 80/20 rule, a small fraction of codes get most clicks. Keep them in an in-memory cache; a hit is a sub-millisecond lookup. ~10 GB of RAM holds a huge hot set.
③
Sharded KV store + read replicas — the source of truth, partitioned by code (Day 14) with replicas (Day 12) to spread read load and survive node loss.

301 vs 302 — a tiny choice with big consequences

The redirect's HTTP status code is a real design decision, not a detail:

301 Moved Permanently302 Found (temporary)
Browser behaviorCaches the redirect; future clicks skip your server and go straight to the destination.Does not cache; every click comes back through your server.
Load on youLower — browsers absorb repeat clicks. Higher — you see every single click.
AnalyticsPoor — you can't count clicks the browser handled itself.Full — every click is observable (and you can expire/disable a link).

This is the archetypal system-design tradeoff: 301 optimizes for your infrastructure; 302 optimizes for control and measurement. A shortener whose business is analytics (bit.ly) leans toward 302 so it can count and manage every click; one that just wants cheap, fast redirects leans toward 301. There's no universally "best" answer — which is exactly the lesson. Say which you'd pick and why, and you've nailed the question.

The Hard Parts

The shortener looks done, but the interesting edges are where real systems live.

The counter can't be a single point

One global auto-increment row makes every write wait in line and dies if that one node dies. The fixes all trade a little "neatness" for scale: ID ranges per server (simple, but IDs are no longer strictly sequential and a crashed server wastes its range), Redis INCRBY (fast, but Redis is now critical), or a Snowflake scheme (fully decentralized, but IDs are longer and encode a timestamp). Pick based on how much coordination you can tolerate.

Sequential codes are guessable

If code 4Gf2a is just counter N in base62, then 4Gf2b is N+1 — an attacker can enumerate every link in your system, which may leak private URLs. If unguessability matters, don't encode the raw counter: use a shuffled/permuted alphabet, encrypt the integer before encoding, or switch to the pre-generated random key strategy. This is a security-vs-simplicity call you should name out loud.

Custom aliases and collisions

Custom aliases (sho.rt/spring-sale) live in the same keyspace as generated codes, so you must check that a requested alias isn't already taken (and reserve it atomically to avoid two users grabbing it at once). Good practice: keep generated codes long enough, and/or carve custom aliases into a reserved namespace so they can't collide with the counter's output.

Expiry, cleanup, and hot keys

Links may expire (set a TTL and let a background job or the store's TTL reap them). A single viral link is a hot key — millions of clicks on one code — which the cache and CDN handle beautifully precisely because the mapping is immutable. And analytics (counting clicks) is a whole write-heavy subsystem of its own: don't do it synchronously on the redirect path — fire an event onto a queue (Day 18) and aggregate offline, so counting a click never slows the redirect.

The through-line: a URL shortener is deceptively simple. The product is two verbs, but every hard part — unique IDs without a bottleneck, read-heavy scaling, guessability, hot keys, async analytics — is a pattern you'll meet in every system after this. That's why it's the first one we build.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"Just hash the URL and take the first few characters."Truncated hashes collide (birthday paradox) long before you'd expect, silently sending one link to the wrong destination. You'd need collision detection and re-hashing. A counter avoids collisions by construction.
"The short code should be derived from the long URL."It doesn't need to be — and shouldn't be. The code only has to be unique and short. Decoupling the code from the content is what lets you use a simple counter and offer custom aliases.
"Use base62 to make the code unguessable."base62 is about shortness, not secrecy. Encoding a sequential counter in base62 is still perfectly enumerable. Unguessability needs random keys or a shuffled/encrypted encoding — a separate decision.
"One auto-increment counter in the database is fine."It's a bottleneck and single point of failure at scale — every write serializes on it. Distribute the counting (ranges, Redis, Snowflake).
"301 and 302 are basically the same."301 is cached by the browser (low load, no click analytics); 302 routes every click back through you (full analytics, higher load). It's a real tradeoff you must choose deliberately.
"Optimize the write path."Reads outnumber writes ~100:1. The architecture should bend toward a fast, cacheable redirect; writes are comparatively rare.

Active Recall

❓ You're designing a URL shortener for 100 million new links per day with a 100:1 read:write ratio, to run for 5 years. (1) Roughly what write QPS, read QPS, and total storage are you sizing for, and how long must the short code be? (2) Which key-generation strategy would you choose, and how would you keep it from becoming a single point of failure? (3) Would you use 301 or 302 redirects, and what are you trading? (click to reveal)
(1) Sizing. 100M/day ÷ ~100,000 s/day ≈ 1,000 writes/sec; ×100 ≈ 100,000 reads/sec (≈200K at peak). Storage: 100M × 365 × 5 ≈ 182 billion links × ~500 B ≈ ~90 TB, so the store must be sharded. Keyspace: you need ~182 billion codes; 62⁶ ≈ 56.8B is too few but 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion is plenty → 7-character base62 codes.

(2) Key generation. Counter + base62 is the clean choice — each integer is used once so codes never collide, and base62 keeps them short. To avoid a single-counter bottleneck/SPOF, distribute it: hand each app server a range of IDs at a time (or use Redis INCRBY, or Snowflake-style IDs) so servers mint unique codes with little or no coordination. (If links must be unguessable, encode with a shuffled alphabet or switch to a pre-generated random-key service.)

(3) 301 vs 302. 301 lets browsers cache the redirect → far less load on you, but you lose per-click analytics and the ability to change/expire a link cheaply. 302 routes every click through your service → full analytics and control, but higher load. Choose 302 if the product is measurement (like bit.ly); 301 if you just want cheap, fast redirects. The point is naming the trade.

Real-World Engineering

🔗 bit.ly & TinyURL

The canonical shorteners. bit.ly's whole business is analytics, so it favors redirects it can measure (302-style) and layers click tracking, link management, and expiry on top of the core code → URL map — exactly the design above.

🐦 Twitter / X — t.co

Every link posted is wrapped in a t.co short link so the platform can count clicks and screen for malware/spam before sending users on — a shortener used as a safety and measurement layer, not just for brevity.

▶️ YouTube video IDs

Those 11-character IDs like dQw4w9WgXcQ are essentially a base64 encoding of a unique number — the same "encode a unique integer in a compact alphabet" trick that gives short, collision-free, URL-safe identifiers at massive scale.

📸 Instagram shortcodes

Instagram famously built a sharded unique-ID service (timestamp + shard-id + per-shard sequence, Snowflake-style) and base64-encodes it into the media shortcode — a textbook distributed counter solving exactly the bottleneck Nadia hit.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: At its core, what two operations is a URL shortener, and which one do you optimize for?
A: Write (shorten): long URL → mint a unique short code, store code → URL. Read (redirect): code → look up URL → HTTP redirect. Optimize for the read path — reads outnumber writes ~100:1.
Q: Why is "counter + base62" the classic key-generation strategy?
A: Each counter value is used exactly once, so codes are unique by construction (no collision checks). base62 (0-9 A-Z a-z) keeps them short: 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion codes in just 7 characters.
Q: What's wrong with a single auto-increment counter, and how do you fix it?
A: It's a bottleneck and single point of failure — every write serializes on one row. Distribute it: per-server ID ranges, Redis INCRBY, or a Snowflake-style ID (timestamp + machine + sequence).
Q: Does base62 make short codes unguessable?
A: No. base62 is about shortness. A base62-encoded sequential counter is still fully enumerable. For unguessability, use random pre-generated keys or a shuffled/encrypted encoding.
Q: 301 vs 302 for the redirect — what's the trade?
A: 301 (permanent) → browser caches it, so fewer hits reach you but you lose click analytics. 302 (temporary) → every click returns through your service, giving full analytics and control at higher load.
