Design a Web Crawler
A Polite Swarm Walking the Entire Web

Yesterday we served the hottest data fast. Today we go out and gather it — at planetary scale — with a web crawler like Google's Googlebot. Strip away the mystique and a crawler is something you already understand: a breadth-first walk over a graph. Fetch a page, pull out its links, visit the links you haven't seen yet, repeat — the web is just an enormous graph where pages are nodes and hyperlinks are edges. The hard part isn't the walk; it's doing it politely (don't get your crawler banned), without visiting the same page twice (there are billions of URLs — a Bloom filter, Day 36, earns its keep), without storing the same content twice, and in parallel across thousands of machines without two of them hammering the same website. You'll step a live crawler through a tiny web and watch it dedup cycles in real time, then dial a "how long to crawl the web" estimator to feel the scale. By the end, "crawler" will mean one precise thing: BFS over the web graph, with a dedup set and a politeness policy.

Picture this

Imagine an endless city where every house has doors leading to other houses, and those lead to still more — a city with no map and no edges, just houses connected by doors. You send in a swarm of polite little robot explorers to map the whole thing. The city is the web; each house is a web page; each door is a hyperlink. Mapping the city means visiting every reachable house and photographing what's inside.

Each robot carries a clipboard — a to-visit list of addresses it still needs to reach. That clipboard is the URL frontier, the crawler's work queue. The robot takes the top address off the clipboard, walks to that house, photographs the contents (downloads and stores the page), then notes down every door it can see — the addresses those doors lead to — and adds the new ones to the bottom of its clipboard. Then it grabs the next address and repeats. That "visit, note the doors, move on" rhythm is a breadth-first walk, and it's the entire engine of a crawler.

Three habits keep the swarm sane. First, the city is full of loops — door A leads to house B, which has a door right back to A. If robots re-visited houses, they'd walk in circles forever. So the town hall keeps a giant ledger, and every visited address gets stamped; before walking anywhere, a robot checks the ledger — already stamped? skip it. That ledger is the seen-set, and because there are billions of addresses, the robots use a clever "probably-stamped" shortcut instead of a full list — a Bloom filter (Day 36). Second, a robot never bangs on one neighborhood's doors over and over — it reads the "house rules" sign posted at each neighborhood's entrance (that's robots.txt) and waits a few seconds between knocks so it doesn't overwhelm anyone. That restraint is politeness, and it's what keeps the swarm from being thrown out (IP-banned). Third, some houses are identical twins at different addresses — same furniture, same everything. A robot that already has that exact photo doesn't take it again; it keeps one copy. That's content deduplication. Hold this picture: a polite swarm doing breadth-first exploration of an endless city, with a to-visit clipboard, a stamped ledger, and one photo per unique house. That is a web crawler.

The Story

Priya was asked to build a crawler to feed a price-comparison search engine: go fetch millions of product pages across the web so they could be indexed and searched. "How hard can it be," she thought, and wrote the obvious thing — a function that fetches a page, finds every link on it with a regex, and calls itself on each link. Fetch, find links, recurse. It worked beautifully on a three-page test site and then, pointed at the real web, it died within minutes.

The first death was a stack overflow: recursion goes as deep as the web is linked, which is effectively infinite. She replaced the recursion with an explicit queue (Day 18) — put seed URLs in, pull one out, fetch it, push its links on the back. That turned the walk into a clean breadth-first loop she could reason about and pause. But the loop never ended: page A linked to B, B linked back to A, and her crawler bounced between them forever, re-fetching the same handful of pages millions of times. The web is full of cycles. She added a seen set and checked it before enqueuing any URL — only fetch what you haven't fetched. Suddenly the crawl made progress... until the seen set, now holding hundreds of millions of URLs as fat strings, ate all the RAM on the box. A teammate reminded her of Day 36: she didn't need to store the URLs exactly, only answer "have I probably seen this?" — a Bloom filter did that in a fraction of the memory.

Then the complaints arrived. One e-commerce site emailed to say her crawler had hit them thousands of times a second and they'd blocked her IP — and, by the way, she was ignoring their robots.txt, which politely asked crawlers to stay out of certain paths and wait between requests. She was being a bad citizen. She added politeness: read and respect each site's robots.txt, and never hit the same host more than once every few seconds (a per-host crawl delay). Crawling got slower per site — but she stopped getting banned, which meant she actually finished.

Finally, scale. One machine fetching one page at a time would take years to crawl millions of pages, let alone the web. She needed many worker machines fetching in parallel. But the instant she split the work randomly, two workers would both grab URLs from the same host and hammer it together — breaking the politeness she'd just fixed. The elegant fix: partition the frontier by host so that all of a given site's URLs are owned by exactly one worker, which paces itself — the same consistent hashing (Day 14) she used for the cache ring, now hashing on the hostname. She added one more polish: many different URLs served identical content (print versions, tracking-parameter copies), so she fingerprinted each page and stored only one copy of each unique document. The little recursive toy had become a distributed system — and Priya realized she had, step by step, rebuilt exactly what Googlebot does to crawl the web.

What should stay in your head
A web crawler is a massively parallel, polite breadth-first walk over the web graph: fetch a page, extract its links, enqueue the ones you haven't seen, repeat. Three data structures rule it. The URL frontier is the queue of what to fetch next — prioritized by importance and freshness, and organized so each host is paced politely. The seen-set stops you fetching the same URL twice; at billions of URLs it's a Bloom filter (Day 36), not a plain set. And storage holds the fetched pages plus a content-fingerprint set so identical pages at different URLs are kept once. Everything else is making that walk survivable: obey robots.txt and a per-host crawl delay so you don't get banned; partition the frontier by host across workers with consistent hashing (Day 14) so politeness still holds in parallel; detect spider traps (infinite URL spaces) with depth and budget limits; and re-crawl changed pages to stay fresh. One-line test: a crawler is BFS over the web graph with a dedup set and a politeness policy — the frontier is the queue, the Bloom filter is the "already seen," and robots.txt is the "please be gentle."

Why This Problem Exists

Almost everything built on top of the web first needs a copy of it. A search engine can't rank pages it hasn't read; a price-comparison site can't compare prices it hasn't fetched; an AI training set, a broken-link checker, a plagiarism detector, a web archive — all of them start the same way: go out and systematically download pages, following links, until you've covered what you care about. There is no central directory of every web page. The only way to discover them is the way a person browsing would — start somewhere and follow the links. That's a crawler.

The core loop is almost insultingly simple, and you can write it in a few lines. The difficulty is entirely in the scale and the manners. The web has on the order of tens of billions of reachable pages, it's riddled with cycles (so a naive walk never terminates), it contains traps that generate infinite URLs on purpose, the pages change constantly (so the job is never "done"), and every page you fetch belongs to someone else's server that you can accidentally knock over if you're greedy. So a real crawler is the simple loop wrapped in four disciplines: don't revisit (dedup), don't overload anyone (politeness), don't drown in duplicates (content fingerprinting), and spread the work without breaking the first three (distribution).

The entire crawler, in pseudocode — everything else is scaling this loop
frontier = Queue(seed_urls) # the to-visit list (URL frontier) seen = BloomFilter() # "have I queued this URL before?" (Day 36) fingerprints = Set() # "have I stored this exact content before?" while frontier is not empty: url = frontier.pop() # next URL to fetch (BFS order) if not robots_allow(url): continue # obey robots.txt wait_for_politeness(host(url)) # per-host crawl delay — don't hammer page = fetch(url) # download it fp = fingerprint(page.content) if fp not in fingerprints: # content dedup store(url, page); fingerprints.add(fp) for link in extract_links(page): if link not in seen: # URL dedup — this is what stops cycles seen.add(link); frontier.push(link)

The Design, Step by Step

Same discipline as the last three designs: scope it, size it, sketch the interfaces, then draw the boxes. The "data model" here is a queue plus two dedup sets; the real design effort goes into the frontier (what to fetch next, and politely) and distribution.

Step 1 — Scope the requirements

✅ Functional

Start from seed URLs; fetch each page.

Extract links and follow new ones.

Store page content for downstream use (index, archive).

🎯 Non-functional

Scalable — billions of pages, thousands of workers.

Polite — respect robots.txt & per-host rate limits.

Robust & fresh — survive traps; re-crawl changed pages.

🤔 Clarify early

Whole web, or one domain / a topic? (Changes scale wildly.)

HTML only, or also render JavaScript pages?

How fresh must it be — one-shot, or continuous?

Step 2 — Size it (back-of-the-envelope, Day 30)

Suppose we target 1 billion pages, averaging 100 KB each, and we want a full pass inside a 30-day freshness window.

# throughput we need pages / window = 1e9 / (30 × 86400 s) = ~386 pages/sec # sustained, for a month # a polite worker, with crawl delay + latency, manages maybe ~50 pages/sec workers ≈ 386 / 50 = ~8 workers # to merely keep up at 1B/month # want 10× headroom / bigger web? scale to hundreds or thousands of workers # storage — raw pages (before compression) raw = 1e9 × 100 KB = ~100 TB # compresses ~5–10× for text # bandwidth at 386 pages/sec bw = 386 × 100 KB × 8 bits = ~0.3 Gbps # scales linearly with crawl rate # the seen-set — URL dedup for 1B URLs exact set (avg 70-byte URLs) ≈ 70 GB → Bloom filter ≈ ~2 GB # Day 36 pays off hugely

The sizing reveals the shape: a crawler is I/O- and network-bound, not CPU-bound, and politeness caps per-host throughput, so you scale by adding workers across many hosts in parallel. It also shows why the dedup set must be a Bloom filter — an exact set of a billion URLs is tens of gigabytes you'd rather not pay for just to answer "seen it?"

Step 3 — The core interfaces

frontier.push(url, priority) # add a discovered URL (priority = importance × freshness) frontier.pop() → url # next URL whose host is due for a polite fetch seen.contains(url) → bool # Bloom filter: "probably seen" or "definitely not" robots.allowed(url) → bool # cached per host; refreshed periodically fetch(url) → {status, html, headers} # with timeout + retry + redirect handling

Notice the frontier's pop() isn't a plain FIFO — it returns the next URL whose host is allowed to be hit right now. Politeness lives inside the frontier, which is why the frontier is the most interesting component in the whole design.

Step 4 — Draw the architecture

The crawler is a loop: the frontier feeds fetchers, fetchers feed a parser, the parser's links flow back into the frontier, and its content flows into storage. Everything runs as pools of workers partitioned by host.

1
Seed the frontier. Start with a handful of known URLs (a few popular sites, a sitemap). The URL frontier is the prioritized queue of everything waiting to be fetched.
2
Fetchers pull URLs — politely. A worker takes a URL whose host is due, resolves DNS, checks the cached robots.txt, honors the per-host crawl delay, and downloads the page (with timeouts and retries).
3
Parse the page. Extract the text/content to store and the outbound links to follow. Normalize each link to a canonical URL (resolve relative paths, strip tracking params, lowercase the host).
4
Two dedup gates. Content: fingerprint the page; if that fingerprint is new, store it — otherwise it's a duplicate, drop it. URLs: for each extracted link, check the seen-set (Bloom filter) — if it's new, mark it seen and push it onto the frontier.
5
The loop closes. New URLs are now in the frontier, so the cycle repeats — potentially forever, since the web keeps changing. A scheduler re-queues important pages for re-crawl to keep the copy fresh.

The Heart: The URL Frontier + Deduplication

Two components separate a real crawler from the three-line toy: the frontier (how it decides what to fetch next, politely) and the dedup sets (how it avoids re-fetching URLs and re-storing content). Get these right and the rest is plumbing.

The URL frontier — not just a queue

A plain FIFO queue would work for a toy, but a real frontier has to do two jobs at once: fetch important pages before unimportant ones (prioritization), and never hit a single host too fast (politeness). The classic design (from the Mercator crawler) solves both with two layers of queues. A set of front queues holds URLs by priority — a prioritizer assigns each discovered URL a rank (homepage of a popular site = high; a deep, rarely-changing page = low) and drops it into the matching queue. A set of back queues holds URLs grouped one host per queue; a politeness timer releases a URL from a host's queue only once that host's crawl delay has elapsed. Front queues decide what matters; back queues decide what's polite to fetch right now.

URL deduplication — why a Bloom filter

Before a discovered link is enqueued, the crawler must answer: have I already seen this URL? If yes, drop it — that single check is what turns an infinite loop over the web's cycles into a terminating walk. An exact set of a billion-plus URLs is tens of gigabytes, and you consult it on every link of every page — so it has to be fast and small. A Bloom filter (Day 36) is the perfect fit: it answers "definitely new" or "probably seen" in a couple of gigabytes for a billion URLs. Its one quirk — a small rate of false positives (claiming you've seen a URL you haven't) — is harmless here: the worst case is skipping a page you could have crawled, never crawling one twice or corrupting anything. You trade a tiny bit of completeness for an enormous memory saving, which is exactly the right trade at this scale.

Content deduplication — same page, many URLs

URL dedup isn't enough, because the web serves identical content under many different URLs: a print version, the same article with a ?utm_source=twitter tag, mirror sites, session IDs baked into the path. Fetching those again is unavoidable, but storing them all wastes space and pollutes a search index with duplicates. So the crawler fingerprints each page's content — a hash of the normalized text — and stores a page only if its fingerprint is new. For near-duplicates (pages that differ only by an ad or a timestamp) production crawlers use a similarity hash like SimHash, which produces close fingerprints for close content so you can detect "almost the same" cheaply.

✔ The frontier paces, the Bloom filter forgets nothing important, the fingerprint set dedups content — these three turn a loop into a crawler. The frontier prioritizes and enforces one-host-at-a-time politeness; the Bloom filter answers "seen this URL?" in gigabytes instead of terabytes; the fingerprint set keeps one copy of each unique page.
✘ Don't use a plain FIFO and a plain hash-set at web scale — a FIFO ignores priority and politeness (you'll crawl junk and get banned), and an exact URL set won't fit in memory. Scale forces the Bloom filter and the layered frontier.

Politeness & robots.txt — Being a Good Citizen

A crawler reaches into servers that aren't yours. Fetch too aggressively and you'll slow or crash someone's site, waste their bandwidth, and get your IP blocked — which ends your crawl. Politeness isn't optional courtesy; it's what lets the crawl survive. It has three parts.

RuleWhat it meansWhy
robots.txtBefore crawling a host, fetch /robots.txt and obey its Disallow rules and Crawl-delay. Cache it per host and refresh periodically.It's the web's standard, machine-readable "house rules." Ignoring it is how you get banned, blocklisted, and sometimes sued.
Per-host rate limitNever hit one host faster than, say, once every few seconds — regardless of how many URLs you have for it. Enforced by the frontier's back queues.A single site can't absorb your full fleet. The limit is per host, not global — you can still crawl thousands of different hosts in parallel.
Identify yourselfSend an honest User-Agent with a contact URL, and back off on 429/503 responses.Lets site owners understand the traffic and ask you to adjust instead of just blocking a mystery bot.

Politeness is also why distribution is subtle. If you split work randomly across a thousand workers, several will independently hold URLs for the same popular host and hammer it in concert. The fix is to partition the frontier by host — hash the hostname (consistent hashing, Day 14) so every URL of a given site lands on the same worker, which paces that site by itself. Partition by host, and politeness that holds on one machine automatically holds across the whole fleet.

The Hard Parts

The loop is easy. These four edges are what separate a weekend script from a crawler that can actually run against the open web.

Spider traps & infinite URL spaces

Some sites generate infinite URLs — a calendar with a "next month" link forever, faceted-search pages with every combination of filters, or URLs that embed their own growing path. A naive crawler falls in and crawls one site forever, starving everything else. Defenses: cap crawl depth per site, cap the number of URLs per host (a budget), detect suspicious URL patterns (ever-growing length, repeating segments), and lean on the content-dedup fingerprint — trap pages are often near-identical, so SimHash catches them.

Freshness & re-crawling

The web changes, so a crawl is never "done." You must re-crawl — but re-crawling everything equally is wasteful: a news homepage changes every minute, an old archived post never changes. Smart crawlers estimate each page's change rate and re-crawl high-value, fast-changing pages far more often than the static long tail, spending a fixed crawl budget where it buys the most freshness. This is a scheduling/prioritization problem baked into the frontier.

JavaScript-rendered pages

Modern sites often ship a near-empty HTML shell and build the real content in the browser with JavaScript. A simple fetch-and-parse crawler sees nothing. To crawl them you must render the page in a real (headless) browser engine first — which is far more CPU- and memory-expensive than a plain HTTP GET. The trade-off: render everything and your throughput collapses; render nothing and you miss huge swaths of the modern web. Real crawlers render selectively, usually based on signals that a page needs it.

Distributed coordination & dedup at scale

Across thousands of workers, the seen-set and frontier are shared state. You can't have every worker consult one global set on every link without a bottleneck. The usual answer is the same partitioning idea: shard the frontier and the Bloom filter by host (consistent hashing, Day 14), so each worker owns a slice of the URL space and dedups locally. Then the hard distributed-systems questions return — a worker dies holding part of the frontier (Day 12 replication / durable queues), two workers briefly disagree about "seen" (a tolerable, eventually-consistent, Day 10 kind of fuzziness), and the whole thing must survive restarts without re-crawling the web from scratch (checkpoint the frontier and dedup sets to durable storage).

The through-line: fetch → extract → enqueue is trivial; doing it politely, without repeating yourself, across thousands of machines, forever is the whole discipline. Pace with the frontier, dedup URLs with a Bloom filter and content with fingerprints, obey robots.txt, partition by host so politeness survives parallelism, and bound the traps. Get those right and a three-line loop becomes Googlebot.

Real-World Engineering

🔍 Googlebot

Google's crawler continuously crawls tens of billions of pages, prioritizing by importance and change rate, obeying robots.txt, and rendering JavaScript pages in a headless Chromium when needed. Its output feeds the index that powers Search. The Mercator design's layered frontier (priority front queues + per-host back queues) is the blueprint most large crawlers still follow.

🗄️ Common Crawl

A nonprofit that crawls the web every month and publishes the raw result — petabytes of pages — for free. It's the backbone of countless research projects and a major source of LLM training data. A concrete, public example of exactly the system in this lesson, at full scale.

📚 Internet Archive

The Wayback Machine crawls and archives snapshots of pages over time, so you can see what a site looked like years ago. Same crawling machinery, a different goal: freshness here means capturing many versions, not just the latest.

🕷️ Scrapy & Apache Nutch

Open-source crawlers you can run yourself. Scrapy (Python) is the go-to for focused, single-site scraping; Nutch (Java/Hadoop) is a distributed, web-scale crawler that pairs with the Solr/Elasticsearch search stack. Both implement the frontier + dedup + politeness pattern.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: In one sentence, what is a web crawler?
A: A massively parallel, polite breadth-first walk over the web graph — fetch a page, extract its links, enqueue the ones you haven't seen, repeat — built on a URL frontier (queue), a seen-set (Bloom filter), and storage with content dedup.
Q: What is the URL frontier, and why isn't it a plain FIFO queue?
A: The frontier is the queue of URLs waiting to be fetched. It must do two jobs: prioritize important pages (front queues by rank) and enforce politeness (back queues, one host each, released on a per-host crawl delay) — so pop() returns the next URL whose host is due, not just the oldest.
Q: Why use a Bloom filter for the seen-set, and what's the downside?
A: A billion URLs as an exact set is tens of GB; a Bloom filter (Day 36) answers "seen?" in ~2 GB. Downside: a small false-positive rate — it may claim you've seen a new URL and skip it. Harmless here: it never causes a double-crawl, only a rare missed page.
Q: Why partition the crawl frontier by host, and with what tool?
A: So politeness survives parallelism: every URL of a given site lands on one worker that paces it, instead of many workers hammering it at once. Use consistent hashing (Day 14) on the hostname — the same ring from the cache lesson.
Q: What is a spider trap and how do you defend against it?
A: A site that generates infinite URLs (endless calendar, faceted search) that a naive crawler falls into forever. Defenses: cap crawl depth and URLs-per-host (a budget), detect ever-growing URL patterns, and use content fingerprinting/SimHash to catch the near-identical trap pages.

If you only remember one thing: a web crawler is BFS over the web graph with a dedup set and a politeness policy. A URL frontier (queue) holds what to fetch next — prioritized and paced one-host-at-a-time; a seen-set (a Bloom filter, Day 36) stops you fetching the same URL twice and makes the walk terminate over the web's cycles; content fingerprints keep one copy of each unique page. Wrap that loop in robots.txt and a per-host crawl delay so you don't get banned, partition by host with consistent hashing (Day 14) so politeness survives thousands of parallel workers, and bound the spider traps. The three-line loop is easy; doing it politely, without repeating yourself, across a fleet, forever, is the craft.
