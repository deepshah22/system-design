Fault Tolerance & High Availability
Building Things That Refuse to Die

For twenty-one lessons you've been building parts — load balancers, caches, databases, brokers, event pipelines. Here's the uncomfortable truth about every one of them: it will fail. A disk dies, a process crashes, a network link drops, a whole data center loses power. The only real question is what happens next — does one dead part take the whole system down with it, or does the system shrug and keep serving your users? Today's two ideas are how grown-up systems answer that. Fault Tolerance is keeping the system working correctly even while a part is broken, by having spares that quietly take over. High Availability is the measurable result — being up and serving for a very high fraction of the time, counted in famous "nines." You'll learn why stringing components together multiplies their failure rates, why redundancy multiplies the nines back, and the very real bill — money, complexity, and a nasty trap called split-brain — that every extra nine charges you.

Picture this

Two aircraft on the runway. The first is a small single-engine plane. It's cheap, it's simple, and it flies beautifully — right up until that one engine coughs. When it does, there is no plan B: the engine is the plane, and the flight is over. That single engine is a single point of failure — one part whose death is the whole system's death. The second is a twin-engine airliner. It costs far more and carries a second engine it hopes never to need. But when one engine fails at 35,000 feet — and engines do fail — the plane simply keeps flying on the other one, lands safely, and most passengers never even know. The failure happened; it was just masked by a spare. That is fault tolerance: not "nothing breaks," but "when something breaks, the system carries on." And the airline's availability? It's the fraction of its scheduled flights that actually take off and land as promised — the reliability you can measure, which the whole fleet of spare engines and backup systems exists to push as close to 100% as money allows. Burn in the image: a single-engine plane is a single point of failure; a twin-engine plane keeps flying when one dies — that spare is redundancy, and it's the only reason the system stays up.

The Story

Uptime Uma ran the backend for a growing payments startup, and on paper her architecture was clean: a load balancer in front, one application server behind it, and one database holding every transaction. It was easy to draw, easy to reason about, and it handled the traffic just fine. For months, nobody thought about failure at all — because nothing had failed yet.

Then, at 2:11 a.m. on a Tuesday, the database server rebooted itself to apply a kernel patch. It was down for forty minutes. During those forty minutes the application server, healthy and willing, had nothing to talk to — so every single payment failed. The next month a routine deploy crashed the lone app server, and even though the database was perfectly fine, customers again saw nothing but errors, because the one machine that served them was gone. The CEO pulled Uma aside with a simple, brutal question: "Why do we keep going completely dark every time one thing hiccups?"

Uma sat down and did the math, and it stopped her cold. Her load balancer was up about 99.9% of the time. So was her app server. So was her database. "Three nines each — that's great," she thought. But they were wired in a series: a payment only succeeds if the LB and the app server and the database are all up at once. So the real number was 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 99.7% — and every single one of those three machines was a single point of failure that could black out the whole company by itself. She hadn't built one reliable system; she'd built a chain of three, and a chain is only as strong as the product of its links.

So Uma added spares — redundancy — at every tier. Two load balancers instead of one. Three app servers behind them, with the load balancer running health checks so that the instant a server stopped answering, it was pulled out of rotation and the survivors kept serving. And for the database, a standby replica (the streaming replication from Day 12) that could be promoted to primary automatically if the primary died. Now the math flipped in her favor: with two independent load balancers, the chance both are down at once is 0.001 × 0.001 = 0.000001 — one in a million. Redundancy didn't just help; it turned three nines into six. The whole point: a redundant tier fails only when all its copies fail at once, and that's exponentially rarer.

But Uma also met the bill — and it was steeper than she expected. Her cloud spend roughly doubled; spares cost real money whether they're needed that day or not. Worse, the very first time her database failover fired for real, it created a nightmare: the network briefly split, the standby couldn't hear the primary, assumed it was dead, and promoted itself — but the original primary was actually alive the whole time. For ninety seconds she had two databases both convinced they were the primary, each accepting conflicting writes. This is split-brain, and it corrupted data until she added fencing and a quorum so that a node can only lead if a majority agrees it should (a preview of the leader-election and consensus lessons coming in Phase 2). And one more humbling lesson: her "two redundant servers" had both been sitting in the same data center, so when that whole building lost power, her redundancy bought her nothing. She learned to spread copies across failure domains — separate availability zones — so that no single fire, flood, or power cut could take them all. Uma had stopped asking "will it fail?" and started designing for "when it fails, what keeps serving?"

What should stay in your head
Fault tolerance and high availability are cause and effect. Fault tolerance is the mechanism: the system keeps working correctly while a component is broken, because a redundant spare masks the failure. High availability is the measured result: the fraction of time the system is up and serving, counted in nines (99.9% = three nines ≈ 8.8 hours of downtime a year; each extra nine cuts downtime 10×). The single most important intuition: components wired in series — where the request needs all of them — multiply their availabilities, so five 99% parts give only 0.99⁵ ≈ 95%, and every part is a single point of failure (SPOF). The cure is redundancy in parallel: put N copies of a component so the tier is down only when all N are down (availability = 1 − (1−a)ᴺ), which turns two 99% copies into 99.99%. Redundancy needs three companions to actually work: health checks (to notice a copy died), failover (to route around it — active-passive keeps a warm standby; active-active serves from all copies at once), and independent failure domains (copies in separate AZs so one disaster can't kill them together). And it charges a real price: money (you pay for spares that mostly sit idle), complexity (failover logic, the split-brain risk when two copies both think they're in charge), and diminishing returns (each extra nine costs exponentially more). You don't buy 100% — you buy the number of nines your business actually needs.

Redundancy, Single Points of Failure & Failover

Everything in high availability grows from one seed idea: a single point of failure (SPOF) is any component whose failure takes down the whole system. The entire discipline is (1) find every SPOF, and (2) eliminate it by adding a redundant copy — and then make sure the traffic actually moves to that copy when the original dies. Redundancy without automatic rerouting is just an expensive paperweight.

Redundancy.Have more than one of everything that matters — two load balancers, N app servers, a primary and a replica database. The tier survives as long as at least one copy is alive. This is the raw material of fault tolerance.
Health checks.You can't route around a dead copy you don't know is dead. Something must continuously probe each copy ("are you alive and answering correctly?") — the load-balancer health checks from Day 5, or heartbeats between database nodes. A copy that fails its checks is marked down and taken out of rotation.
Failover.The act of shifting work off a failed copy onto a healthy one. For stateless app servers behind a load balancer this is trivial (just stop sending it traffic). For a stateful database it's the hard part — you must promote a replica to primary and repoint everyone at it, ideally in seconds and without losing writes.
Failback.Once the original recovers, deciding whether to move work back to it (or leave it as the new standby). Done carelessly, an over-eager failback can cause a second outage — so mature systems make it deliberate, not automatic.

Active-Passive vs Active-Active

There are two fundamentally different ways to run redundant copies, and the choice comes up in every senior design discussion. In active-passive (a.k.a. primary/standby), one copy does all the work while the spare sits warm and idle, ready to take over on failure. It's simpler and avoids conflicts (only one node writes), but the standby is money spinning idle, and there's a brief failover gap while it wakes up and gets promoted. In active-active, every copy serves live traffic at once. There's no idle hardware and no wake-up delay — a dead node just means the survivors carry more load — but now you must handle concurrent writes to multiple nodes (conflict resolution, the multi-leader problem from Day 12) and make sure losing one node doesn't overload the rest (capacity planning: N+1, not just N).

The diagram traces an active-passive database failover in time. Notice the shape of the outage: it isn't "the database is gone forever," it's a gap — the seconds between the primary dying and the standby being detected-as-needed, promoted, and reconnected. Shrinking that gap is the whole job. The two numbers that define it are MTBF (mean time between failures — how often it breaks) and MTTR (mean time to recovery — how fast you heal). Availability is literally MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR): you push availability up either by failing less often or by recovering faster — and for most real systems, getting MTTR down (fast automatic failover) is the cheaper, bigger win than chasing components that never break.

The Language of Nines

"Highly available" is meaningless until you attach a number, and the industry counts availability in nines — the count of leading 9s in the uptime percentage. The magic of the table below is that each extra nine cuts your allowed downtime by 10×, and the jump in engineering effort to earn it grows just as fast. This is the vocabulary of every SLA you'll ever sign.

SLA, SLO, and SLI — the three that get confused

These three acronyms travel together and mean different things. An SLI (Service Level Indicator) is the measurement — e.g. "the percentage of requests that succeeded in under 200ms." An SLO (Service Level Objective) is your internal target for that indicator — "99.9% of requests succeed." An SLA (Service Level Agreement) is the contract with your customer, usually a slightly weaker promise than your SLO, with penalties (refunds, credits) if you miss it. Teams pick an SLO below 100% on purpose, and the gap between it and 100% is your error budget — the amount of failure you're allowed to spend on risky deploys and experiments. Run out of error budget and you freeze risky changes until reliability recovers. This is the beating heart of Google's SRE practice, and it reframes reliability from "never fail" to "fail no more than we budgeted."

Patterns for High Availability

Redundancy is the foundation, but real systems layer several patterns on top. Here are the ones you'll be expected to name and reach for in an interview.

Eliminate SPOFs (N+1 redundancy)

For every tier, run one more copy than you need to serve peak load, so losing one leaves you still fully capable — not scrambling. "N+1" (or N+2) is the baseline; a single of anything is a SPOF by definition.

Two LBs, 3 app servers for a 2-server load, primary + replica DB.

Spread across failure domains

Copies in the same rack, room, or data center fail together when that domain dies. Distribute redundancy across availability zones (and, for disasters, regions — Day 64) so no single power cut or flood takes them all.

AZ-a + AZ-b + AZ-c; a whole-AZ outage still leaves two-thirds serving.

Health checks + load-balancer eviction

The load balancer (Day 5) continuously probes each backend and instantly stops routing to any that fail — so a crashed instance becomes invisible to users within seconds, not minutes.

Deep health checks test the DB connection too, not just "is the port open?"

Graceful degradation

When a non-critical dependency dies, serve a reduced experience instead of an error page. Better a page without personalized recommendations than no page at all — partial beats nothing.

Recommendations service down → show a generic "popular items" list.

Bulkheads & isolation

Named after a ship's watertight compartments: partition resources so a flood in one area can't sink the whole vessel. One overloaded feature shouldn't drain the thread pool every feature shares.

Separate connection pools / thread pools per dependency; one hang ≠ total outage.

Timeouts, retries & circuit breakers

Never wait forever on a sick dependency (timeout), retry carefully with backoff and jitter, and when a dependency is clearly down, stop hammering it — the Circuit Breaker of Day 23, tomorrow's lesson.

Timeout 500ms → 2 retries w/ jitter → open the breaker if it keeps failing.

Notice how these stack: redundancy gives you spare capacity, health checks and failover route to it, failure domains keep the spares independent, and degradation, bulkheads, and breakers keep one sick part from spreading its illness to the whole system. High availability isn't a single feature — it's a discipline of removing every way the whole can die with a part.

The Hard Parts — What Availability Costs You

Reliability is not free, and pretending it is gets teams burned. Every nine you chase charges you in four currencies.

1. Each nine costs exponentially more

Going from 99% to 99.9% might mean adding a standby and a load balancer. Going from 99.99% to 99.999% can mean multi-region deployments, follow-the-sun on-call, chaos testing, and eliminating human steps from every recovery path — vastly more expensive for 47 fewer minutes of downtime a year. The right question is never "how do we get 100%?" (you can't) but "what's the cheapest architecture that meets the nines our business actually needs?" A hobby blog and a payments core have very different correct answers.

2. Failover is a mini-outage, and it can go wrong

The moment of failover is itself risky. There's a detection delay (health checks take time to be sure), a promotion delay (a replica catching up and taking over), and a real chance of getting it wrong — most infamously split-brain: a network partition makes a standby believe the primary is dead, so it promotes itself, but the primary is actually alive. Now two nodes both accept writes and your data diverges. The fixes — fencing (forcibly disabling the old primary), quorum (a node may only lead if a majority agrees), and proper leader election — are exactly the consensus machinery of Paxos and Raft (Days 31–32). This is why "just add a standby" is harder than it looks.

3. Correlated failures break the math

The whole availability calculation assumes copies fail independently — that both LBs going down at once is one-in-a-million. But if both live in the same rack, run the same buggy deploy, share the same certificate that expires, or depend on the same overloaded DNS, they fail together, and your "redundant" system has the availability of a single copy. Real outages are overwhelmingly correlated: bad configs pushed everywhere at once, a shared dependency, a whole-AZ power event. Genuine redundancy means independent everything — hardware, zone, software version, and deploy timing.

4. The CAP tax during a partition

When the network splits (Day 10's P), a highly available system faces a forced choice: keep serving on both sides and risk inconsistency (choose A), or refuse writes on the minority side to stay consistent (choose C). "High availability" and "strong consistency" pull against each other precisely at failure time — the worst possible moment to discover you never decided which you wanted. Every failover design is implicitly a CAP decision.

The through-line: high availability trades money and complexity for the system's refusal to die. Spares sit idle, failover logic can misfire, and the last nines cost a fortune — so you buy exactly as much reliability as your users and your revenue actually demand, design every copy to fail independently, and invest in recovering fast (MTTR) at least as much as in never breaking.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"Fault tolerance and high availability are the same thing."They're cause and effect. Fault tolerance is the mechanism — masking a component failure with redundancy so the system keeps working correctly. High availability is the measured outcome — the fraction of time you're up, in nines. You build the first to achieve the second.
"Each of my components is 99.9%, so my system is 99.9%."Only if they're wired in parallel. In series (a request needs all of them) availabilities multiply: five 99.9% components give 0.999⁵ ≈ 99.5%. Chaining reliable parts produces a less reliable whole. Every serial dependency is a SPOF.
"I added a backup, so I'm covered."A backup you never fail over to is a paperweight. Redundancy only helps with health checks to detect the failure and automatic failover to route around it — and only if you've actually tested that the failover works. Untested failover usually fails when you finally need it.
"Two copies means we're twice as safe, always."Only if they fail independently. Copies in the same rack, on the same deploy, sharing the same expiring cert or the same DNS fail together — a correlated failure — and your redundancy is an illusion. Spread copies across real failure domains.
"We should aim for 100% uptime."100% is impossible and chasing it bankrupts you — each extra nine costs exponentially more. Pick the nines your business needs, set an SLO below 100%, and spend the resulting error budget on shipping features. Reliability is a business trade-off, not a moral absolute.
"Automatic failover can't make things worse."It absolutely can. A network blip can trigger a needless failover, and a botched one causes split-brain — two nodes both acting as primary, accepting conflicting writes and corrupting data. Safe failover needs fencing, quorum, and leader election (Days 31–32).

Active Recall

❓ Your system is a load balancer → app server → database, one of each, wired in series, and each machine is up 99.9% of the time. (a) What's the system's real availability, and roughly how much downtime per year is that? (b) The CEO wants "four nines." Describe concretely what you'd change and why it raises the number. (c) You add a standby database with automatic failover — name one new, serious failure mode you've just introduced and how you'd defend against it. (click to reveal)
(a) Real availability: the three are in series, so a request succeeds only if all three are up — availabilities multiply: 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 0.997, i.e. ~99.7%, which is roughly 26 hours of downtime a year (about a full day). And each of the three machines is a single point of failure: any one dying blacks out everything. Three nines each did not give you three nines overall. (b) Reaching four nines: eliminate the SPOFs with redundancy in parallel at every tier — 2+ load balancers, 3+ app servers behind them with health checks so the LB evicts any dead one instantly, and a primary + standby database with automatic failover. A redundant tier is down only when all its copies are down at once, e.g. two 99.9% LBs → 1 − 0.001² = 99.9999%. Multiplying those much-higher per-tier numbers gets the whole system to four nines (~52 min/year). Crucially, spread the copies across separate availability zones so a single data-center outage can't take a whole tier — otherwise the failures are correlated and the math is a lie. (c) New failure mode: split-brain — a network partition makes the standby think the primary is dead, so it promotes itself while the primary is still alive; now two databases accept conflicting writes and your data diverges. Defend with fencing (forcibly disable the old primary before promoting), and quorum / leader election so a node may only become primary if a majority of nodes agree — the consensus machinery of Days 31–32. Mantra: redundancy without safe failover just trades one outage for a worse, quieter kind of corruption.

Real-World Engineering

☁️ AWS Availability Zones

AWS regions are split into multiple availability zones — physically separate data centers with independent power, cooling, and networking, close enough for fast links but far enough that one disaster can't hit two. The entire cloud playbook for HA is "run in ≥2 (ideally 3) AZs" so a whole-AZ failure leaves you serving. It's failure domains, productized.

🐒 Netflix & Chaos Monkey

Netflix built Chaos Monkey to randomly kill production instances during business hours — deliberately causing failures to prove the system survives them. If you can't tolerate a random node dying at 2pm on a Tuesday, you'll find out on your own terms, not during an incident. Fault tolerance you haven't tested is just a hope.

🔍 Google SRE & Error Budgets

Google's Site Reliability Engineering reframes availability as a budget: pick an SLO (say 99.99%), and the 0.01% you're allowed to fail becomes an error budget teams spend on risky launches. Burn it and risky deploys freeze until reliability recovers — turning "never fail" into a shared, quantified trade-off between speed and stability.

🏦 Payments & five nines

Card networks and core banking chase five nines (~5 min/year) because a minute of downtime is millions of failed transactions and broken trust. They run active-active across regions, with automated failover, fencing, and consensus-backed leader election — and treat every human step in recovery as a bug, because humans are slow when the clock is the enemy.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What's the difference between fault tolerance and high availability?
A: Fault tolerance is the mechanism — the system keeps working correctly while a component is broken, by masking the failure with a redundant spare. High availability is the measured result — the fraction of time the system is up and serving, counted in nines. You build the first to achieve the second.
Q: Why is a system of components in series less available than any single one, and what's the fix?
A: In series a request needs all components, so their availabilities multiply (five 99% parts → 0.99⁵ ≈ 95%), and each is a single point of failure. The fix is redundancy in parallel: N copies per tier, so the tier is down only when all N are down — availability = 1 − (1−a)ᴺ, turning two 99% copies into 99.99%.
Q: What does "three nines" mean, and how much downtime is it?
A: 99.9% availability ≈ 8.8 hours of downtime per year (~1.4 min/day). Each additional nine cuts downtime 10×: four nines (99.99%) ≈ 52 min/year, five nines (99.999%) ≈ 5 min/year — and costs exponentially more to achieve.
Q: Active-passive vs active-active redundancy?
A: Active-passive = one copy serves, a warm standby waits and is promoted on failure (simpler, no write conflicts, but idle hardware + a failover gap). Active-active = every copy serves live traffic (no idle capacity, instant survival), but you must handle concurrent writes/conflicts and size for N+1 so survivors aren't overloaded.
Q: What is split-brain and how do you prevent it?
A: A network partition makes a standby believe the primary is dead, so it promotes itself while the primary is still alive — now two nodes accept conflicting writes and corrupt data. Prevent it with fencing (disable the old primary), quorum (a node may only lead with majority agreement), and proper leader election (Raft/Paxos, Days 31–32).
