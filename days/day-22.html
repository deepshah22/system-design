Fault Tolerance & High Availability
Building Systems That Refuse to Die

For twenty-one lessons we built systems that work. Today we face the uncomfortable truth every senior engineer lives with: everything fails. Servers crash, disks rot, networks blink out, whole data-centers lose power. You cannot prevent failure — you can only decide whether a single failure takes your whole system down with it. Fault tolerance is the art of building systems that keep serving users while parts of them are broken. We'll make "uptime" precise (the famous nines), learn the surprisingly simple math that turns a flaky 99% server into a rock-solid 99.99% service, and then hit the trap that quietly wrecks most "redundant" systems: redundancy only saves you when failures are independent. This is the day the word reliability stops being a vibe and becomes engineering.

Visual Memory Anchor

🎄💡

Picture this

Think of two strings of Christmas lights. The old string is wired in series: the electricity flows through every single bulb in a line, so when one bulb burns out, the circuit breaks and the entire string goes dark — and then you spend an hour hunting for the one dead bulb. That's a system with a single point of failure: every part must work or the whole thing dies. The modern string is wired so each bulb has its own little bypass — one bulb burns out and the rest stay lit. You barely notice. That's fault tolerance: the failure of one part is contained; the system keeps shining. Now hold a second image beside it: a jetliner has two, three, even four engines not because it needs them all to fly, but so it can lose one at 35,000 feet and land safely anyway. The spare engine is never "wasted" — it's the whole reason you'd get on the plane. Burn in the pair: a fault-tolerant system is the light string that stays lit and the plane that keeps flying when one part dies. And the deepest twist you'll learn today: two engines only help if they don't both run dry from the same empty fuel tank.

The Story

Downtime Dana launched an online ticket store, and for a while a single server ran the whole thing beautifully — one box in one data-center, humming along. Then her store got popular, and the failures began. One morning that server's disk died, and the store was down for six hours while she scrambled to restore a backup onto new hardware. Customers hit a dead page, tweeted angrily, and bought their tickets elsewhere. Dana had built a system where one broken part meant everything was broken — a classic single point of failure.

So Dana did the obvious thing: she added a second server behind the load balancer from her earlier lessons. Now if one crashed, the other kept serving. Her math felt magical: a server that's up 99% of the time (down ~3.6 days a year — embarrassing) became, with two of them, a service up 99.99% of the time (down under an hour a year). She hadn't made either server more reliable; she'd made it so that both had to fail at the same instant for customers to notice — and two independent 1%-chances of being down collide only 0.01% of the time. One cheap extra box bought her two extra nines. Dana felt invincible.

Then came the night that taught her the real lesson. Both her servers lived in the same data-center — the same building, on the same power feed. A transformer blew, the building went dark, and both of her "redundant" servers died in the same second. Her beautiful 99.99% evaporated into a total outage, because her two failures weren't independent — they shared a hidden common cause. Redundancy that all fails together isn't redundancy at all; it's just a more expensive single point of failure. Dana spread her servers across three availability zones (separate buildings, separate power, separate networks), and only then did losing one zone become a shrug instead of an outage.

But redundancy was only half the fight. When a server did die, how fast did traffic stop flowing to the corpse? Dana added health checks — the load balancer pinging each server every few seconds and yanking any that stopped answering — so a dead box was drained in seconds instead of serving errors for minutes. She set up a standby database that a failover would promote if the primary died. And she learned to think in two numbers that govern every uptime figure on Earth: how often things break (MTBF) and how fast you recover (MTTR) — and that shrinking the second is usually cheaper and more powerful than chasing the first. Dana never stopped things from failing. She stopped failures from mattering.

What should stay in your head
You cannot build components that never fail — so build a system that survives its components failing. Availability is the fraction of time the system is up, measured in nines (99.9% = ~8.8 hours down/year; 99.99% = ~53 min; 99.999% = ~5 min). The master lever is redundancy: run more than one of everything so no single box is a single point of failure (SPOF). The math is startlingly good — if one instance is up a of the time and you run N of them behind a load balancer, the tier is down only when all fail, so availability = 1 − (1 − a)N. Two 99% servers → 99.99%. But this only works if the failures are independent. Put both copies in the same rack, same zone, same bad deploy, or behind the same dependency, and one root cause kills them together (a correlated / common-mode failure) — your redundancy is an illusion. So you spread copies across failure domains (zones, regions) and detect death with health checks / heartbeats, then fail over to a healthy copy — active-passive (a standby waits) or active-active (all copies serve at once). Every availability number reduces to two quantities: MTBF (mean time between failures — fail less) and MTTR (mean time to recovery — recover faster): Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Recovering fast usually beats failing rarely. And when you can't stay fully up, degrade gracefully — serve less rather than nothing.

What "Availability" Actually Means — The Nines

Before you can make a system reliable, you have to measure reliability. Availability is simply the fraction of time a system is working and reachable: uptime / (uptime + downtime). We write it as a percentage, but engineers count the nines because each extra nine is roughly ten times harder and more expensive than the last — and, more usefully, each nine translates into a very concrete amount of allowed downtime per year. Memorize this table; it comes up in interviews constantly and it anchors every SLA (Service Level Agreement) you'll ever negotiate.

AvailabilityNameDowntime / yearDowntime / monthFeels like
90%"one nine"~36.5 days~3 daysA hobby project. Unusable for business.
99%"two nines"~3.65 days~7.2 hoursDown for a full working day, twice a year. Painful.
99.9%"three nines"~8.76 hours~43 minA solid internal tool. Common baseline SLA.
99.99%"four nines"~52.6 min~4.3 minA serious production service. The usual target.
99.999%"five nines"~5.26 min~26 secTelecoms, payments. Very hard & costly.

Two things to internalize. First, the jump from 99% to 99.9% removes ~3 days of downtime a year; the jump from 99.99% to 99.999% removes only ~47 minutes — yet often costs far more. Chasing nines has brutally diminishing returns, so a Principal Engineer asks "how many nines does this feature actually need?" rather than reflexively maximizing. A background analytics job might be fine at 99%; a payments API might need 99.99%+. Second, availability is measured as the user sees it. If your servers are up but the database they need is down, you are down. Which brings us to the two levers underneath every one of these numbers.

MTBF and MTTR — the two dials behind every nine

Every availability figure decomposes into just two quantities:

MTBF — Mean Time Between Failures.On average, how long does the system run before it breaks? Bigger is better. You raise it with quality hardware, testing, redundancy, and careful change management (most outages are caused by deploys, not cosmic rays).
MTTR — Mean Time To Recovery.Once it breaks, how long until it's serving again? Smaller is better. You lower it with health checks, automated failover, good runbooks, fast rollbacks, and practice.
The formula.Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). A system that fails once a month (MTBF ≈ 720 h) but recovers in 5 minutes is more available than one that fails once a year but takes a full day to fix.
The insight most people miss.You can't always stop things from breaking, but you can almost always recover faster. Halving your MTTR is usually cheaper and more reliable than doubling your MTBF. "Design for recovery, not just for prevention."

Redundancy, SPOFs & the Independence Trap

The single most important idea in fault tolerance is the elimination of single points of failure. A SPOF is any one component whose failure takes down the whole system: one server, one database, one load balancer, one network link, one data-center — even one shared config file or one overloaded on-call engineer. The cure is redundancy: run N > 1 of the thing so that losing one still leaves a working copy.

Why one extra copy buys so much

Here's the math that made Dana feel invincible. Suppose one instance is available a fraction a of the time, so it's down with probability (1 − a). If you run N independent copies and you only need one alive, the tier is down only when every copy is down at once — probability (1 − a)N. So:

# One instance at 99% available → down 1% of the time 1 - 0.99 = 0.01 # 1 copy → 99% (~3.65 days/yr down) 1 - 0.01^2 = 0.9999 # 2 copies → 99.99% (~53 min/yr down) 1 - 0.01^3 = 0.999999 # 3 copies → 99.9999% (~32 sec/yr down) # Each independent copy multiplies your DOWNTIME by the (small) failure rate. # Redundancy attacks downtime geometrically — that's why it's the master lever.

This is the whole reason we run fleets of servers, replicate databases (Day 8–9), and put copies in multiple zones. But notice the load-bearing word in the derivation: independent.

The independence trap (correlated / common-mode failure)

The formula (1 − a)N assumes each copy fails on its own, for its own random reason. Real copies often share a hidden common cause, and when they do, they fail together — turning your carefully multiplied nines back into a single point of failure. This is the mistake that quietly defeats most "redundant" architectures:

Same rack / same zone / same power.Two servers in one building die together when the building loses power. Fix: spread across availability zones and, for the highest tiers, regions.
Same bad deploy.You push a broken release to all N copies at once — all N crash identically. This is why the biggest outages are self-inflicted. Fix: staged / canary rollouts, so a bad change hits 1% before 100%.
Same upstream dependency.All your "redundant" app servers call the same single database, or the same auth service, or the same DNS. That shared dependency is the real SPOF. Fix: make the dependency redundant too, or degrade gracefully when it's gone.
Same overload / thundering herd.One copy dies, its traffic piles onto the survivors, which overload and die, cascading through the fleet. Fix: capacity headroom, load shedding, and the circuit breakers we'll meet tomorrow (Day 23).

The rule: redundancy multiplies your nines only across independent failure domains. Ask of any redundant design: "What single thing, if it failed, would take all of these down at once?" — and remove it. That question separates real fault tolerance from expensive theater.

Failover & Health Checks — Detecting Death and Routing Around It

Redundancy gives you a spare. Failover is the act of actually switching to it when the primary dies — and the speed and correctness of that switch is a huge chunk of your MTTR. Two things have to happen: you must notice the failure (detection), then redirect traffic to a healthy copy (recovery).

Health checks & heartbeats — how the system notices

A system can't route around a dead node it doesn't know is dead. So healthy components constantly prove they're alive. A health check is a load balancer (or orchestrator) periodically asking each instance "are you OK?" — a simple GET /healthz that returns 200. Miss a few in a row and the instance is marked unhealthy and drained (traffic stops going to it) within seconds. A heartbeat is the same idea between peers: a standby watching a primary's regular "I'm alive" pings, ready to take over when they stop. The tuning here is a real trade-off: check too aggressively and a brief hiccup gets a healthy node killed and the system "flaps"; check too slowly and a dead node serves errors for a long minute before anyone notices. Good health checks also test the real path (can I reach the DB?), not just "is the process running?" — a server can be up but useless.

Active-Passive vs Active-Active

There are two classic ways to arrange your redundant copies, and the choice is a staple of design interviews.

Active-Passive (failover)

One copy actively serves; a standby waits idle, kept in sync, ready to be promoted. On failure, a heartbeat times out and the standby takes over. Simpler and safer for stateful systems (only one writer), but you pay for a machine that does nothing most of the time, and failover isn't instant — there's a detection + promotion gap.

Classic for: primary/replica databases, where one writer avoids conflicts.

Active-Active

All copies serve traffic at once behind a load balancer; losing one just means the rest absorb its share. No wasted capacity, near-instant "failover" (traffic was never on the dead node exclusively), and it doubles as horizontal scaling. But it's harder: every copy is a live writer, so you must handle concurrent state, conflicts, and consistency.

Classic for: stateless web/app tiers; multi-region read/write with care.

N + 1 / N + M redundancy

Provision one (or M) more than you strictly need to carry the load, so a failure doesn't drop you below capacity. If 4 servers handle peak, run 5 — the "+1" absorbs a death without overloading the rest and triggering a cascade.

The honest capacity math behind "just add redundancy."

Graceful degradation

When you can't stay fully up, serve less instead of nothing. Recommendations service down? Show the page without recommendations. Search down? Fall back to cached results. A partial, slightly-worse experience beats an error page — failing soft, not hard.

Best for: non-critical features on a critical page (e.g., checkout).

Bulkheads (isolation)

Named after a ship's watertight compartments: partition resources so one flooded section can't sink the ship. Give each tenant/feature its own pool of connections/threads so one runaway can't starve everything. Contains the blast radius of a failure.

Best for: shared services where one noisy neighbor could take out all.

Fencing / quorum (anti split-brain)

To stop two nodes both thinking they're the primary (split-brain) after a network glitch, require a majority vote to be leader, and fence (forcibly cut off) the old primary. This is where consensus (Raft, Day 32) enters the story.

Best for: any single-writer system with automatic failover.
A health check + failover, sketched
// The load balancer polls each backend; unhealthy ones are removed from rotation. every 5s: for (server of pool) { ok = httpGet(server + "/healthz", { timeout: 2000 }); // does the REAL path work? if (!ok) server.misses++; else server.misses = 0; if (server.misses >= 3) drain(server); // 3 strikes → stop routing here (recover fast) if (server.misses === 0) restore(server); // healthy again → add back } // Standby DB watches the primary's heartbeat; promotes itself if it goes quiet — // but only after winning a quorum vote, so a network blip can't create two primaries.

The HA Toolkit — A Field Guide

Put together, high availability is a layered discipline: you push failures down the stack until the user never sees them. Here's the mental checklist, roughly from "prevent" to "contain" to "recover."

Eliminate SPOFs

Two of everything on the critical path — servers, load balancers, databases, network paths. Ask "what one thing, if it dies, kills us?" and remove it.

The foundation. Everything else assumes this.

Spread across failure domains

Copies in different zones/regions so no shared power, network, or building fails them together. Independence is what makes redundancy real.

Multi-AZ by default; multi-region for the top tiers.

Detect fast (health checks)

Constant liveness + real-path checks so death is noticed in seconds, not minutes. Detection is the front half of MTTR.

Tune to avoid flapping vs. slow detection.

Recover automatically (failover)

Promote a standby or shift traffic to healthy copies without a human in the loop at 3 a.m. Automation is the back half of MTTR.

Active-passive or active-active per state needs.

Contain the blast radius

Bulkheads, cell-based architecture, per-tenant limits — so one failure stays a small failure and doesn't cascade across the whole fleet.

Isolation turns an outage into an incident.

Degrade gracefully

When something's gone, serve a reduced experience (stale cache, disabled extras) instead of an error. Keep the core alive even when the edges break.

Fail soft. Users forgive "slower," not "down."

Notice the through-line to your other lessons: load balancers do the health-checking and traffic routing, replication (Day 8–9) makes the redundant data copies possible, caches / CDNs power graceful degradation with stale data, and the whole thing rests on the independence you learned to demand today. Fault tolerance isn't one feature — it's a property that emerges when every layer refuses to be a SPOF.

The Hard Parts — What Availability Costs You

High availability is never free, and treating it as a pure good leads engineers to over-build. Here are the real prices and traps.

1. Redundancy costs money — and every nine costs ~10× more

Running two of everything roughly doubles infrastructure cost; running three across three regions triples it plus the networking and operational overhead. Going from three nines to four to five is not linear — each nine is roughly an order of magnitude more expensive in money, complexity, and engineering time. The Principal-level move is to right-size availability per component: spend your nines on the checkout path, not the "users who viewed this" widget.

2. Redundancy adds complexity, and complexity causes outages

Every failover mechanism, health check, and standby is itself new code and new machinery that can misbehave — health checks that flap and kill healthy nodes, failovers that trigger on a network blip, standbys that were silently out of sync when finally needed. It's entirely possible to make a system less reliable by adding reliability machinery you don't test. Untested failover is not failover — which is why serious shops practice it (chaos engineering, Day 67).

3. Split-brain — the danger of automatic failover

The nastiest HA bug: a network partition makes the standby think the primary is dead, so it promotes itself — but the primary is actually fine and still taking writes. Now you have two primaries diverging, and merging them later can lose or corrupt data. Preventing this needs a majority quorum and fencing — which is exactly the consensus problem (leader election, Raft) we'll tackle in Day 32. Automatic failover without split-brain protection is a data-loss machine.

4. The correlated-failure blind spot

As we hammered above, the math of redundancy assumes independence, and the real world is full of hidden shared causes: a shared dependency, a shared deploy, a shared config, a shared cloud control plane. Your architecture diagram shows independence; your incident report reveals the shared cause you missed. Auditing for common-mode failure is ongoing work, not a one-time checkbox.

The through-line: fault tolerance trades money, capacity, and complexity for the ability to lose parts of your system without losing your users. Bought wisely — where the business actually needs it — it's the difference between a blip and a headline. Bought reflexively everywhere, it's a fragile, expensive machine that fails in ways your single server never would.

Active Recall

❓ Your service runs on a single server at 99.9% availability and leadership wants "four nines." You add a second identical server behind the load balancer and celebrate — but a month later a data-center power outage takes the whole service down for 4 hours, and your yearly availability is worse than before you started. Explain (1) why two 99.9% servers should give ~99.9999% and what that means in downtime, (2) why it didn't in this case, and (3) three concrete changes to actually reach and keep four nines. (click to reveal)
(1) The redundancy math: one server is down 0.1% of the time (1 − 0.999 = 0.001). Two independent servers are both down only 0.001² = 0.000001 of the time → availability ≈ 99.9999% (~32 seconds/year), because the service is only down when both fail at the same instant. That's the whole appeal of redundancy: it attacks downtime geometrically. (2) Why it failed: the two servers were not independent — they shared a data-center (same power, same building), so the outage was a correlated / common-mode failure that killed both at once. The (1−a)² math assumes independent failures; a shared root cause collapses your two copies back into a single point of failure. A single 4-hour outage alone is ~0.05% of a year, blowing four nines on its own. (3) Fixes: (a) Spread across availability zones/regions so no shared power or building can down both — this restores real independence. (b) Make shared dependencies redundant (the database, the load balancer itself, DNS) since those are the remaining SPOFs behind "redundant" app servers. (c) Attack MTTR: health checks to detect death in seconds, automated failover (with quorum/fencing to avoid split-brain), and staged/canary deploys so a bad release can't take all copies down together. Mantra: redundancy only counts across independent failure domains — and recovering fast beats never failing.

Real-World Engineering

☁️ AWS Availability Zones

The entire cloud is built on this lesson. A "region" is split into multiple Availability Zones — physically separate data-centers with independent power, cooling, and networking — precisely so you can run redundant copies that don't share a common cause. "Multi-AZ" is AWS's whole answer to the independence trap; the big historical outages were almost always a shared control plane that broke the independence assumption.

🎬 Netflix & Chaos Monkey

Netflix assumes servers will die, so they built Chaos Monkey to randomly kill production instances during business hours — forcing every service to be fault-tolerant by default. If your failover only works in theory, chaos engineering finds out on a Tuesday afternoon instead of during a real outage (a preview of Day 67).

🗄️ Databases: primary/replica failover

Postgres, MySQL, MongoDB, and friends run a primary with hot standbys that stream replication and promote automatically when a heartbeat is lost. This is textbook active-passive — and the reason these systems obsess over quorum and fencing is to avoid the split-brain that would corrupt the ledger.

🛒 Amazon: graceful degradation

On a product page, the "buy" path is guarded fiercely, but the recommendations, reviews summary, and "customers also bought" widgets are allowed to fail soft — if their services are down, the page still renders and you can still check out. Protecting the critical path while degrading the edges is how a giant stays "up" even when pieces are broken.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What is fault tolerance, and how does it differ from just "reliable hardware"?
A: Fault tolerance is designing a system that keeps working while some of its components fail — by removing single points of failure through redundancy, failover, and graceful degradation. It accepts that individual parts will fail and makes those failures not matter, rather than trying to build parts that never fail.
Q: If one instance is 99% available, what's the availability of 2 independent copies behind a load balancer, and why?
A: ~99.99%. The tier is down only when both fail at once: 1 − (1 − 0.99)² = 1 − 0.0001 = 0.9999. Each independent copy multiplies the small downtime probability, attacking downtime geometrically. Crucially, this only holds if the copies fail independently.
Q: What is a correlated (common-mode) failure and why does it break redundancy?
A: When "redundant" copies share a hidden cause — same zone/power, same bad deploy, same upstream dependency — so one root cause fails them together. The (1−a)ᴺ math assumes independence; a shared cause collapses your copies back into a single point of failure. Fix by spreading across failure domains.
Q: Write the availability formula in terms of MTBF and MTTR, and say which lever is usually cheaper.
A: Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Raising MTBF (fail less) or cutting MTTR (recover faster) both help, but cutting MTTR is usually cheaper and more effective — automated failover, fast rollback, and health checks beat trying to never break. "Design for recovery."
Q: Active-passive vs active-active — one line each.
A: Active-passive = one copy serves, a standby waits and is promoted on failure (simpler, safe for single-writer state, but wasted capacity + failover gap). Active-active = all copies serve at once (no wasted capacity, near-instant, doubles as scaling, but must handle concurrent writes/conflicts).

The master tool is redundancy — run N > 1 of everything so no single box is a SPOF, which drives availability to 1 − (1 − a)N and turns flaky parts into a solid whole. But the non-negotiable catch: redundancy only counts across independent failure domains — copies that share a zone, a deploy, or a dependency fail together and give you expensive theater instead of safety. Detect death fast with health checks/heartbeats, recover automatically with failover (active-passive or active-active, guarded against split-brain by quorum), and when you can't stay fully up, degrade gracefully — serve less, never nothing. Every uptime number reduces to MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR), and recovering fast usually beats failing rarely.
