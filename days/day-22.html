Fault Tolerance & High Availability
Staying Up When Parts Fall Down

For twenty-one lessons we quietly assumed the machines stay up. They don't. Disks die, processes crash, cables get unplugged, a whole data center loses power in a storm. At any real scale, something is always broken right now — the only question is whether your users can tell. Today we learn the two ideas that answer that question. Fault Tolerance is a system's ability to keep working when its parts fail. High Availability is how we measure and engineer for staying up — the language of "nines," redundancy, failover, and killing every single point of failure. You'll see why 100% is a fantasy, why one un-redundant piece caps your whole system no matter how much you harden the rest, and how the pros put a number on "always on."

Picture this

Two strings of holiday lights. The old string is wired in series: current flows through bulb one, then two, then three, in a single line. It's cheap and simple — and the moment any one bulb burns out, the circuit breaks and the entire string goes dark. You then get to hunt, bulb by bulb, for the one that died. Every single bulb is a single point of failure for the whole string. The modern string is wired in parallel: each bulb has its own path to power. One bulb dies and the rest keep glowing — you might not even notice until you look closely. That's the entire idea of high availability in a picture. A system wired "in series" — one web server, one load balancer, one database, each depending on the next — goes fully dark when any one piece fails. A system built with redundancy — spare bulbs on their own paths — keeps the lights on when a piece dies, because the load simply flows around it. Burn in the image: series lights fail as one; parallel lights fail one-at-a-time and nobody's left in the dark. Fault tolerance is wiring your system in parallel.

The Story

Reliable Rita launched her startup's API on a single, sturdy server. It was fast, it was cheap, and for months it just worked. One night at 2:07 a.m., that server's disk failed. The API went completely dark. No orders, no logins, nothing — a flat, total outage. Rita's phone screamed with pager alerts; she stumbled to her laptop, spun up a replacement, restored from backup, and limped back online at 4:40 a.m. By morning the damage was done: two and a half hours of zero revenue, a wall of angry tweets, and a churned enterprise customer who'd been mid-demo. One server. One disk. One single point of failure — a component whose death takes the whole system with it.

So Rita did the obvious thing: she ran two identical app servers and put a load balancer (Day 8) in front to split traffic between them. Now she had redundancy — a spare. She configured the load balancer with a health check: every two seconds it pinged each server's /health endpoint. When server A froze the following week, the load balancer noticed within seconds, quietly stopped sending it traffic, and routed everything to server B. Users saw nothing. This is failover: automatically shifting work off a dead thing onto a healthy one. Rita slept through that failure — the first real proof her system had become tolerant of a fault instead of crippled by it.

Then she looked harder and got a chill. Two app servers, yes — but they both sat behind one load balancer. If it died, everything died, exactly like before. And both app servers talked to one database. If that box's disk failed, the outage was total again, spare app servers or not. She'd removed one single point of failure and left two standing. She learned the rule the hard way: your system is only as available as its least-redundant piece. A chain of "in-series" tiers is capped by its weakest link — three app servers behind a single database is barely more available than one of everything.

So Rita went tier by tier. She ran the load balancer as a redundant pair (active-passive, with a floating IP that fails over). She promoted the database to a primary with a replica (Day 12) that could be promoted if the primary died. She spread her servers across two availability zones so a single data-center power cut couldn't take them all at once. And for the pieces she couldn't perfectly protect, she designed graceful degradation — if the recommendations service was down, the product page still loaded, just without the "you might also like" row. Better a slightly emptier page than a blank error.

Finally, Rita's boss asked the question every business asks: "So how reliable are we, exactly?" Rita stopped saying "pretty reliable" and started measuring. She computed her availability — the fraction of time the system is up — and translated it into the industry's shorthand: nines. Her old single server managed maybe "two nines" (99%) — 3.65 days of downtime a year. Her new redundant stack was pushing "four nines" (99.99%) — under an hour a year. She wrote that number into an SLA, set an internal SLO a little stricter, and watched the SLIs on a dashboard. "Always up" had stopped being a hope and become a budget she could design against.

What should stay in your head
Fault tolerance is the property; high availability is the measured goal. Failures are not rare edge cases — at scale they are the steady state, so you design expecting parts to die. The enemy is the single point of failure (SPOF): any component whose failure takes down the whole system. The cure is redundancy — run spare copies (N+1: one more than you need; N+2: two more) so work can fail over to a healthy copy, detected by health checks. The crucial math: components in a request path are wired "in series," so their availabilities multiply and the whole is less available than any part — which means one un-redundant tier caps your entire system. Redundancy within a tier is "in parallel," so the tier only fails if every copy fails, which adds nines. You measure it all in the language of nines (99% = 3.65 days down/year; 99.99% = ~52 min/year), governed by SLA (the promise), SLO (your internal target), and SLI (what you actually measure). And when you can't prevent a failure, degrade gracefully — lose a feature, not the whole page. The one-line law: kill every SPOF, measure your nines, and plan for the failure you can't prevent.

The Building Blocks of Staying Up

High availability isn't one trick — it's a small kit of ideas that stack. Learn these five and you can reason about the reliability of almost any architecture.

Single Point of Failure (SPOF).Any one component whose failure takes the whole system down. The single server, the single load balancer, the single database, the single network link, the single region. Finding SPOFs is step one of every availability review: trace a request and ask at each hop, "if this dies, are we down?" If yes, it's a SPOF.
Redundancy (N+1, N+2).Run more copies than you strictly need so a death is survivable. N is the capacity you need; N+1 means one spare (survive one failure); N+2 means survive two at once (or one failure while another node is down for maintenance). Redundancy is the raw material of every other technique here — no spare, no failover.
Failover.Automatically shifting work off a failed component onto a healthy one. The key metric is how fast it happens: detection time + switchover time. Fast, automatic failover is the difference between "users never noticed" and "we were down for ten minutes while someone woke up."
Health checks.How the system knows a component died so it can fail over. A load balancer or orchestrator repeatedly probes each instance (GET /health every few seconds); a few failed probes in a row and the instance is ejected from rotation. Good health checks test real readiness (can it reach the DB?), not just "is the process alive?"
Graceful degradation.When you can't keep a feature up, shed it instead of failing the whole request. Recommendations down? Show the page without them. Search slow? Serve cached results. The system gets worse under failure but never goes fully dark — a partial service beats a blank error page.

Two related terms you'll hear alongside these. Reliability is the broader idea of "does it behave correctly, including under stress" — availability is one part of it. And resilience is the ability to recover from failure, not just survive it: heal, restart, catch up. Fault tolerance keeps you standing during the punch; resilience gets you back to full strength after.

Graceful degradation, in code — lose a feature, not the page
// BAD — one flaky dependency fails the whole product page async function productPage(id) { const product = await db.get(id); const recs = await recommender.get(id); // if this throws… the whole page 500s return render(product, recs); } // GOOD — the core loads; the optional feature degrades to nothing async function productPage(id) { const product = await db.get(id); // core: must succeed let recs = []; try { recs = await withTimeout(recommender.get(id), 200); // optional: 200ms budget } catch (e) { metrics.inc("recs_degraded"); // note it, don't fail } return render(product, recs); // page loads either way }

Failover & Health Checks — How the System Routes Around Death

Redundancy is potential energy; failover is what turns it into staying up. The mechanism is almost always the same shape: something out front watches the backends with health checks, and the moment one stops answering, it stops sending traffic there and reroutes to the survivors. The user's request that would have hit the dead node hits a live one instead — often without even a visible error.

There are two classic ways to arrange the spares, and knowing the difference is table stakes in an interview. In active-passive (a.k.a. failover pairs), one node serves all the traffic while a standby sits idle, ready to take over — simple, and no risk of two nodes disagreeing, but you're paying for hardware that does nothing until disaster strikes, and the switchover has a brief gap. In active-active, every node serves traffic all the time and the load is shared — you use all your capacity and failover is instant (the survivors just absorb the dead node's share), but every node must be able to run simultaneously without stepping on the others, which is harder for stateful things like databases.

The subtle danger lurking under all failover is split-brain: if the standby wrongly believes the primary is dead (maybe just a network hiccup between them) and promotes itself, you now have two primaries both accepting writes — and they diverge. This is why serious failover systems use a tie-breaker (a quorum or witness) to decide who's really in charge, a problem we'll solve properly with consensus and leader election in Phase 3. For now, just hold the tension: detecting failure too slowly means downtime; detecting it too eagerly means split-brain. Reliability lives in that balance.

The Language of Nines — Putting a Number on "Always Up"

Availability is simply the fraction of time a system is working: Availability = uptime / (uptime + downtime). Because the numbers get tiny, the industry counts them in nines — how many leading 9s the percentage has. The jump from one nine to the next is a 10× reduction in downtime, and it gets exponentially harder and more expensive each step. Memorize the rough downtime-per-year for each level; it comes up constantly.

Why one un-redundant tier ruins your nines

Here's the math that surprises everyone. Components a request must pass through are in series, so you multiply their availabilities. Three tiers at 99.9% each give 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 0.997 — only 99.7%, worse than any single tier. Add more dependencies and it keeps dropping. But redundant copies within a tier are in parallel: the tier is down only if all copies are down, so its unavailability is (1 − a)ⁿ. Two 99% nodes in parallel give 1 − (0.01)² = 99.99% — you turned two nines into four just by adding a spare. That's the whole game: series multiplies your risk, parallel divides it. And it's why one single-node tier caps everything — its availability is a low ceiling the product can't rise above.

MTBF, MTTR, and the SLA / SLO / SLI trio

Two clocks define availability. MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) is how long a component typically runs before breaking; MTTR (Mean Time To Recovery) is how long it takes to get back up. Availability is MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) — and notice you can raise availability two ways: fail less often (bigger MTBF) or recover faster (smaller MTTR). Fast, automatic failover is really an MTTR play. Finally, three acronyms people constantly mix up: an SLI (Indicator) is the actual measured metric ("99.95% of requests succeeded this month"); an SLO (Objective) is your internal target ("stay above 99.9%"); and an SLA (Agreement) is the contractual promise to customers, usually with penalties if you miss it. Smart teams set the SLO stricter than the SLA so they get an early warning before they'd ever breach the contract — the gap is their error budget.

Patterns for Staying Up

The building blocks combine into named patterns you'll deploy over and over. Here's the toolkit, from cheapest to most robust.

Redundancy & Load Balancing

Multiple identical instances behind a load balancer (Day 8), traffic split across them with health checks. The foundational pattern — turns a SPOF tier into a survivable one and gives you scaling for free.

Best for: stateless app/web tiers. The default starting move.

Active-Passive Failover

One node serves; a standby waits, ready to be promoted. Simple, no coordination risk, but idle capacity and a short switchover gap. A floating/virtual IP or DNS flip moves traffic on failover.

Best for: databases, stateful services where two-active is risky.

Active-Active

Every node serves traffic all the time; survivors absorb a dead node's share instantly. Full capacity use, instant failover — but every node must run concurrently without conflict.

Best for: stateless tiers, and multi-region read paths.

Replication

Keep copies of your data on multiple nodes (Day 12) so a disk or node death doesn't lose it and a replica can be promoted. The data-tier's answer to redundancy.

Best for: every database that matters. Sync vs async is a tradeoff.

Multi-AZ / Multi-Region

Spread copies across independent availability zones (separate power/network in one region) or whole regions (separate geography). Survives a data-center or regional outage — the biggest, scariest SPOF of all.

Best for: raising the ceiling past what one data center allows.

Graceful Degradation

When a dependency can't be kept up, drop the feature, not the request. Fallbacks, cached responses, timeouts, and default values keep the core working while the periphery fails.

Best for: optional features (recs, personalization) on a critical path.

Notice the through-line: almost every pattern is a flavor of "have more than one, and route around the dead one." The art is choosing which tiers get how much redundancy, because each nine costs real money — the next section is about that price.

The Hard Parts — What Availability Costs You

High availability sounds like a pure good — who doesn't want "always up"? But every nine is bought with money, complexity, and new failure modes. Chase it blindly and you'll build something fragile and expensive.

1. Every nine costs exponentially more

Going from 99% to 99.9% might mean a load balancer and a second server. From 99.9% to 99.99% means redundant load balancers, multi-AZ, database replicas with fast promotion, and automation to remove humans from the recovery path. From four nines to five means multi-region, and rewriting for split-brain safety. The last nine can cost more than all the earlier ones combined. So the first design question is never "how do we get five nines?" — it's "how many nines does this system actually need?" A photo-sharing app and a payments ledger have very different answers, and over-buying availability is a real, common waste.

2. Redundancy adds complexity — and complexity fails

Every spare you add is another thing that can break, another failover path to test, another config to drift. A redundant pair with a broken failover mechanism is worse than a single node, because you've paid for safety you don't actually have — and you'll discover it at the worst moment. Untested failover is a myth of safety. Serious teams practice failure on purpose (see Chaos Engineering, Day 67) precisely because redundancy you never exercise tends not to work when it counts.

3. Correlated failures defeat naive redundancy

Redundancy only helps if the copies fail independently. Three servers in the same rack share one power supply and one top-of-rack switch — a single failure kills all three at once, and your "redundancy" was an illusion. The same trap scales up: same AZ, same region, same deploy that ships the same bug to every node, same shared dependency (one DNS provider, one certificate). True fault tolerance means spreading risk across independent failure domains, which is exactly why AZs and regions exist.

4. Split-brain and the consistency tax

The moment you have redundant stateful nodes, you inherit the hardest problem in the field: keeping them in agreement while tolerating the network dropping between them. Fail over too eagerly and you get split-brain (two primaries, divergent data); fail over too slowly and you eat downtime. And there's a fundamental limit here — the CAP theorem (Day 10) says when the network partitions, you must choose consistency or availability, you can't have both. High availability isn't free of the consistency tradeoffs you've already met; it collides with them head-on.

The through-line: fault tolerance trades simplicity and cost for survival. The right amount is a business decision, not a heroic one — match the nines to what the system is actually worth, kill the SPOFs that matter, and don't pay for a nine nobody needs.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"We can build a system that never goes down — 100% uptime."100% is impossible; you can only add nines, each exponentially harder. The goal is never "never fail" — it's "fail rarely, recover fast, and degrade gracefully." Even the biggest clouds publish SLAs of "four nines," not 100%.
"We added a second server, so now we're highly available."Only that one tier is redundant. If the load balancer, database, or region in front of or behind it is still a single node, your availability is capped by that weakest link. You must remove every SPOF on the request path.
"More nines is always better."Each nine costs exponentially more money and complexity. Five-nines infrastructure on a hobby app is waste; two nines on a payments ledger is negligence. The right number is a business decision matched to what the system is worth.
"Three servers in a rack means we survive three failures."Only if they fail independently. Same rack = shared power and switch = one failure kills all three. Redundancy only counts across independent failure domains (different racks, AZs, regions). Correlated failure is the silent killer of naive redundancy.
"We have a standby, so failover is handled."Untested failover is a myth of safety. A standby with a broken promotion path, stale data, or a misconfigured health check fails exactly when you need it. If you've never actually killed the primary and watched it recover, you don't know that it works.
"High availability is a networking/ops problem, not my code's job."Graceful degradation, timeouts, idempotent retries, and health endpoints all live in application code. The most available systems are designed for failure at every layer — the app decides whether a dead dependency means a missing feature or a blank error page.

Active Recall

❓ Your service runs three identical app servers behind a load balancer — nicely redundant. But those app servers all talk to a single database, and the load balancer itself is a single box. Each individual machine is about 99.9% available on its own. Explain (1) roughly how available this whole system is and why it's lower than 99.9%, (2) which components are your single points of failure and why adding a fourth app server won't help, and (3) the concrete steps to push toward four nines. (click to reveal)
(1) System availability & why it's lower: the load balancer, the app tier, and the database are all in series — every request must pass through all three, so their availabilities multiply: 0.999 (LB) × 0.999+ (app tier) × 0.999 (DB) ≈ 0.997, about 99.7% — worse than any single component, because a failure of any tier is an outage. The app tier is nearly perfect (three redundant nodes in parallel: 1 − (0.001)³ ≈ six nines), but the two single-node tiers drag the product down. (2) SPOFs: the single load balancer and the single database — if either dies, the whole system is down regardless of how many app servers you have. Adding a fourth app server takes the app tier from ~six nines to ~eight nines, which is invisible against a three-nines DB and LB — you're pouring redundancy into the one tier that's already fine. (3) Path to four nines: make the load balancer a redundant pair (active-passive with a floating IP, or two LBs in DNS), promote the database to a primary + replica with automatic failover/promotion, and spread all of it across two availability zones so a single data-center outage can't take a whole tier. Now every tier is parallel-redundant, no SPOF remains, and the product of per-tier availabilities climbs into the four-nines range. Mantra: you're only as available as your least-redundant tier — kill every SPOF, don't gold-plate the one that's already redundant.

Real-World Engineering

☁️ AWS — AZs & Regions

Amazon's entire reliability model is independent failure domains: multiple Availability Zones (separate power, cooling, and network) inside each Region, and many regions worldwide. The core advice — "deploy across at least two AZs" — is redundancy across independent domains made concrete. Each service publishes an SLA in nines, with credits when they miss.

🐒 Netflix — Chaos Monkey

Netflix built Chaos Monkey to randomly kill production instances on purpose, during business hours. The philosophy: the only way to know your redundancy and failover actually work is to constantly break things and prove the system heals. It forces every service to be fault-tolerant by default — untested failover isn't allowed to exist.

🔍 Google — SRE & Error Budgets

Google's Site Reliability Engineering formalized nines into practice: an SLO defines the target, and the gap to 100% is an error budget the team can "spend" on risky launches. Run out of budget and feature launches freeze until reliability recovers — turning availability into a shared, measurable currency between product and ops.

🏦 Banks & Payments

Payment networks target five nines (~5 min/year) because a card that won't swipe is a business emergency. They run active-active across multiple data centers, synchronous replication for the ledger, and elaborate split-brain protection — the extreme end of the cost curve, justified because every minute of downtime is measured in millions.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What's the difference between fault tolerance and high availability?
A: Fault tolerance is the property of continuing to work when components fail. High availability is the measured goal — designing and quantifying (in "nines") how much of the time the system is up. Fault tolerance is how you achieve high availability.
Q: Why does one un-redundant tier cap the whole system's availability?
A: Tiers on the request path are wired in series, so their availabilities multiply — the whole is less available than any part, and a single-node tier is a low ceiling the product can't exceed. Redundancy within a tier is parallel (1 − (1−a)ⁿ), which adds nines — but only for that tier.
Q: Active-passive vs active-active failover?
A: Active-passive: one node serves, a standby waits to be promoted — simple, no conflict risk, but idle capacity and a switchover gap. Active-active: all nodes serve at once, survivors absorb a dead node instantly — full capacity use and instant failover, but nodes must run concurrently without conflict (hard for stateful data).
Q: How much downtime per year is 99%, 99.9%, and 99.99%?
A: 99% (two nines) ≈ 3.65 days/year; 99.9% (three nines) ≈ 8.8 hours/year; 99.99% (four nines) ≈ 53 minutes/year. Each nine is a 10× reduction in downtime — and exponentially harder to reach.
Q: SLA vs SLO vs SLI, and what's an error budget?
A: SLI = the measured metric ("99.95% of requests succeeded"). SLO = your internal target ("stay above 99.9%"). SLA = the contractual promise to customers (with penalties). The error budget is the gap between 100% and your SLO — the failure you're allowed to spend before you must stop and fix reliability.

If you only remember one thing: at scale, something is always broken — so design for it. Fault tolerance is keeping the system working when its parts fail; high availability is measuring and engineering for that with the language of nines. The enemy is the single point of failure: any component whose death takes everything down. The cure is redundancy (N+1, N+2) plus fast, automatic failover driven by health checks — and the crucial math is that request-path tiers are in series (availabilities multiply, so one un-redundant tier caps the whole system) while redundant copies are in parallel (only all-fail is a failure, so nines pile up). You must therefore kill every SPOF, not just some, and spread copies across independent failure domains so a shared power supply or region can't take them together. Chase only the nines the business actually needs — each one costs exponentially more — and when you can't prevent a failure, degrade gracefully: lose a feature, never the whole page.
