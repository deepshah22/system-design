Fault Tolerance & High Availability
Designing for the Day It Breaks

For twenty-one lessons we've been building things that work. Today we confront the truth every senior engineer lives by: at scale, everything breaks. Disks die, networks drop packets, processes leak memory and crash, and once in a while an entire data center loses power. The question is never "will a component fail?" — it will — but "what happens to my users when it does?" Fault Tolerance is designing a system that keeps working correctly even when parts of it fail. High Availability is keeping it up and responsive, measured in the famous "nines." Today you'll learn to hunt single points of failure, add redundancy, read the surprisingly beautiful math of availability, and pay for it all with money, complexity, and the split-brain trap.

Picture this

Hold two images side by side. The first is a chain pulling a heavy load. A chain is strong — but it is only ever as strong as its single weakest link. One link snaps and the whole chain fails; the load drops. That's a system built as a straight line of dependencies: web server → app server → database → payment API. If any one link breaks, the entire request fails. Every link is a single point of failure. The second image is an airliner at cruising altitude with four engines. It is deliberately built so that if one engine flames out, the plane keeps flying on the other three — the passengers may never even notice. The engineers didn't assume engines never fail; they assumed they will and made the plane survive it. That's redundancy: more than you strictly need, on purpose, so that losing one part is survivable instead of fatal. Burn in the pair: a system built as a chain fails at its weakest link; a system built like an airliner keeps flying when a part dies. Fault tolerance is the art of turning every chain into an airliner.

The Story

Uptime Uma launched her startup's API on a single, well-tuned server. It was fast, it was cheap, and for months it was flawless — so flawless she stopped thinking about it. Then, at 2:47 a.m. on a Saturday, that one server's disk filled with logs and the process died. There was no second server to take over, so the entire product went dark. Her pager screamed, customers tweeted, and Uma spent forty bleary minutes SSH-ing in to clear logs and restart. The outage lasted 52 minutes end to end. One machine had been the whole company's single point of failure, and nobody had noticed because it had simply never failed before.

So Uma did the obvious thing: she ran two app servers behind the load balancer from Week 1, with health checks so the balancer would stop sending traffic to a sick instance. Now one app server could die and users wouldn't feel a thing — the other carried the load while it restarted. She felt invincible for exactly one week, until the database — still a single machine — ran out of connections and fell over. Two healthy app servers were now both uselessly pointed at a dead database. Uma learned the first hard law of reliability: redundancy at one layer is worthless if the next layer down is still a single point of failure. A system is only as available as its weakest link, and you have to hunt SPOFs at every layer — load balancer, app tier, database, and even the network in between.

She added a database replica (the followers from Day 12) and an active-passive failover: if the primary dies, a monitor promotes the replica to primary and the app reconnects. She felt invincible again — until a storm knocked out power to her entire cloud data center, and every redundant thing she owned went dark at once because it all lived in one building. That taught her the second law: redundancy only helps against failures it doesn't share. Two servers on the same power strip both die when the strip does. So she spread her copies across availability zones — separate buildings with separate power, cooling, and networks — so that no single physical event could take down more than one copy. Now a whole zone could burn and the system would shrug and route around it.

With the architecture solid, Uma finally learned to measure what she'd built. The business asked for "four nines" — 99.99% availability — and she discovered what that actually costs: at four nines you're allowed just 52 minutes of downtime per year, which means failovers must be automatic and fast, deploys must be zero-downtime, and even routine maintenance can't take the system offline. She learned to think in MTBF and MTTR (how often things break, and how fast you recover) and realized a humbling truth: past a point, you improve availability far more by recovering faster than by failing less often. Chasing the next nine got exponentially more expensive.

And then Uma met the subtlest bill of all. During one messy failover, the network split so that the old primary and the freshly-promoted replica were both alive and both accepting writes — a split-brain — and they diverged into two conflicting versions of the truth that took a painful day to reconcile. She realized that "highly available" is not the same as "correct": a system that stays up while quietly serving wrong data can be worse than one that honestly goes down. From then on, Uma designed not just for uptime but for uptime she could trust — which is exactly where the rest of this course is headed.

What should stay in your head
Fault tolerance is designing a system to keep working correctly when components fail; high availability (HA) is keeping it up and responsive, measured as a percentage of time it's usable (the "nines"). They're related but not identical — availability is the goal, fault tolerance is one of the main ways you buy it. The enemy is the single point of failure (SPOF): any one component whose death takes the whole system down. The cure is redundancy — deliberately running more copies than you need — combined with failure detection (health checks, heartbeats) and failover (automatically shifting work to a healthy copy). The math has two faces: dependencies in series multiply their failure rates, so a chain gets less available with every link you add (A₁ × A₂ × A₃…); redundant copies in parallel multiply their safety, so availability climbs fast with each spare (1 − (1−A)ⁿ). Availability is also MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) — so you raise it by breaking less often (bigger MTBF) or recovering faster (smaller MTTR), and past a point fast recovery is the cheaper lever. Redundancy only protects against failures your copies don't share, so you spread them across failure domains (racks, availability zones, regions). And the trap on top: "up" is not "correct" — a badly-handled failover can cause split-brain, where the system stays available while serving conflicting data. Aim for availability you can trust, not just uptime.

Single Points of Failure & the Redundancy That Kills Them

The single most valuable habit in reliability engineering is embarrassingly simple: walk every path a request takes and, at each hop, ask "what if this one thing dies right now?" Any component where the honest answer is "the whole system goes down" is a single point of failure, and your job is to eliminate it — usually by making sure there's more than one of it. Do this for the DNS, the load balancer, each service tier, the database, the message broker, the network links, even the power and the humans on call.

Find the SPOF.Trace the request end to end. Wherever there is exactly one of something in the critical path — one load balancer, one primary database, one data center — you've found a single point of failure. If it dies, everything above it dies with it.
Add redundancy.Run N+1 (one more than you need) or N+2 copies so that losing one (or two) leaves enough to carry the load. Redundancy is capacity you pay for and hope to never fully use — like the spare engine on the airliner.
Detect failure.Redundancy is useless if nothing notices the death. Health checks (the load balancer pings each server) and heartbeats (nodes signal "I'm alive") are how the system learns a copy has failed — the health checks you met with load balancers in Week 1.
Fail over.On detecting a failure, automatically shift work to a healthy copy: stop routing to the dead server, or promote a replica to primary. The time this takes — detect + switch — is a big part of your recovery time (MTTR).
Degrade gracefully.When you can't fully serve a request, serve a smaller correct one instead of an error. Netflix without recommendations still plays movies; a store without "you may also like" still sells. Partial function beats total failure.

Active-Active vs Active-Passive

There are two classic ways to run redundant copies, and the difference comes up in every interview. Active-active means all copies serve traffic at the same time (like the two app servers behind a load balancer). You get redundancy and extra capacity, and failover is instant because the survivors are already working — but every copy must be able to handle shared or partitioned state, which is harder for things like databases. Active-passive (a.k.a. primary-standby, or "hot/warm/cold standby") means one copy serves while another waits idle, ready to be promoted if the active one dies. It's simpler and avoids write-conflict headaches — the classic choice for a relational database primary — but the standby is capacity you pay for and don't use, and failover takes a moment (detect the death, promote the standby, redirect traffic). Databases usually go active-passive for writes; stateless app servers usually go active-active.

The Math of Nines — Why Chains Hurt and Copies Help

Availability is a number: the fraction of time the system is usable, written as a percentage. Engineers talk in "nines" because each additional 9 is roughly a 10× reduction in allowed downtime, and each one is dramatically harder and more expensive to reach. Memorize this table — it comes up constantly, and it reframes "how reliable are we?" as a concrete downtime budget.

Availability"Nines"Downtime per yearDowntime per dayFeels like
90%one nine36.5 days2.4 hoursa hobby project
99%two nines3.65 days14 minutesa small internal tool
99.9%three nines8.77 hours1.4 minutesa solid SaaS product
99.99%four nines52.6 minutes8.6 secondsserious business-critical
99.999%five nines5.26 minutes0.86 secondstelecom / core infra

Now the two formulas that the simulator above brings to life. When components sit in series — the request needs all of them, one after another — their availabilities multiply:

Asystem = A₁ × A₂ × A₃ × … × Aₙ

Because every factor is less than 1, adding a dependency can only make the product smaller. Three services at 99.9% each give 0.999³ ≈ 99.7% — you lost two-thirds of a nine just by chaining them. This is the hidden tax of microservices and long call chains: every hop in the critical path drags the whole system's availability down. When components sit in parallel — the request needs only one of the redundant copies to be alive — you multiply their failure probabilities instead, and subtract from 1:

Asystem = 1 − (1 − A)ⁿ

Here each spare shrinks the chance that everything is down at once. Two 99% replicas fail together only 1% × 1% = 0.01% of the time, i.e. 99.99% available — you gained two nines from one spare. This is the mathematical reason redundancy is the master tool of high availability.

MTBF, MTTR, and the cheaper lever

There's a second, equally useful way to write availability, in terms of how often things break and how fast you recover:

Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)

MTBF is Mean Time Between Failures (how long, on average, between breakages) and MTTR is Mean Time To Recovery (how long, on average, to get back up). The insight hidden in this formula is career-defining: you can raise availability by making things break less often (bigger MTBF) — which is hard, expensive, and hits diminishing returns — or by making recovery faster (smaller MTTR), which is often cheaper and more achievable. Cutting your recovery from 30 minutes to 30 seconds does more for your nines than heroically doubling how long your servers last. That's why mature teams pour effort into fast detection, automated failover, instant rollbacks, and good runbooks: recovering fast is the lever most people under-invest in.

Redundancy Patterns & Failure Domains

Redundancy only protects you against failures your copies don't share. Two servers in the same rack both die when the rack loses power; two racks in the same building both die when the building floods. So you spread copies across widening failure domains — blast radii that fail together — trading cost and latency for isolation. Here's the ladder, plus the redundancy styles.

🖥️ Multiple instances

Run several copies of a stateless service behind a load balancer. Kills the "one process/one machine" SPOF. Cheap, easy, the first thing you do — but they share a rack, a zone, a data center.

Protects against: a crashed process or a dead server.

🏢 Availability Zones (AZ)

Spread copies across separate buildings within a region, each with its own power, cooling, and network. A whole building can fail and you route around it. The standard bar for "highly available" in the cloud.

Protects against: power loss, fire, or flood in one data center.

🌍 Multi-Region

Copies in geographically distant regions (US-East, EU-West). Survives an entire region outage or a natural disaster, and cuts latency for far-away users — at the cost of real complexity: cross-region replication lag and data consistency (Days 10–12).

Protects against: a whole cloud region going dark.

⚡ Active-Active

All copies serve traffic simultaneously. Instant failover (survivors already work) and extra capacity, but every copy must handle shared/partitioned state. The default for stateless app tiers.

Best for: stateless services, read-heavy replicas, global edge.

🛟 Active-Passive

One copy serves; a standby waits to be promoted. Simpler, avoids write conflicts, but the standby is idle capacity and failover isn't instant. The classic database-primary pattern.

Best for: single-writer databases, leader-based systems.

🧱 Bulkheads & Blast Radius

Partition resources so one overloaded tenant or failing feature can't sink everything — like watertight compartments in a ship. Isolate thread pools, connection pools, and tenants so a fire in one room stays in that room.

Best for: shrinking how much one failure can take down.

The decision is a ladder of "how bad a failure must I survive, and what will I pay for it?" A crashed process → multiple instances. A dead data center → multiple availability zones. A dead region or a global audience → multi-region, eyes open to the consistency cost. And regardless of layer, decide active-active vs active-passive per component based on whether it holds mutable state — and use bulkheads so no single failure's blast radius is bigger than it has to be.

The Hard Parts — What High Availability Costs You

Reliability is never free, and chasing it blindly creates its own outages. Four currencies to spend wisely.

1. Money and complexity scale with every nine

Redundancy means paying for capacity you hope to never fully use — double the servers, a standby database, a second data center. And each additional nine is exponentially harder: going from 99.9% to 99.99% might mean automating every failover and eliminating all planned downtime; 99.99% to 99.999% might mean multi-region and a dedicated reliability team. The right target is a business decision, not a badge — most products do not need five nines, and pretending you do burns money and adds fragile complexity. Match the nines to what an outage actually costs you.

2. Failover isn't instant — and isn't free

"We have a replica" doesn't mean zero downtime. Failover takes time: detect the failure (health checks have an interval), decide it's really dead (not a blip), promote the standby, and redirect traffic. That whole window is your MTTR, and it's often seconds to minutes. Worse, failover is a rare, under-tested code path — the one that runs at 3 a.m. during a real incident, having never been exercised. If you don't practice failure (Netflix famously kills its own servers with Chaos Monkey), your failover will fail exactly when you need it.

3. "Up" is not "correct" — the split-brain trap

The subtlest danger: a system that stays available while going wrong. In a network partition, an automatic failover can promote a replica while the old primary is still alive and accepting writes — now you have two primaries (split-brain) diverging into conflicting truths. This is exactly the consistency-vs-availability tension of the CAP theorem (Day 10) made painfully concrete. The cures — fencing the old primary, quorums, and consensus algorithms (Raft/Paxos, later in the course) — are their own deep topic. For now, the lesson is: available-but-wrong can be worse than honestly-down, and safe failover is genuinely hard.

4. Redundancy can create correlated failure

Adding copies can reduce reliability if you're careless. Copies that share a dependency fail together (both app servers behind one load balancer → the LB is still a SPOF). A retry storm from redundant clients can overload the very service they're trying to reach. An auto-scaler and a failover can fight each other. Redundancy only helps when failures are independent — so the real skill is hunting shared dependencies and hidden coupling, not just adding boxes.

The through-line: high availability is the deliberate purchase of survival of failure with money, complexity, and correctness risk. Buy exactly as much as your users and business actually need — no more chains than necessary in the critical path, enough redundancy across enough failure domains to survive the failures that matter, and fast, tested recovery so your MTTR stays tiny. And never forget that staying up while serving garbage isn't availability — it's an outage in disguise.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"Fault tolerance and high availability are the same thing."Related, not identical. Availability is the goal — the fraction of time the system is usable. Fault tolerance is one of the main techniques for achieving it: keeping the system working correctly through component failures. You can be highly available without deep fault tolerance (just get lucky and recover fast), and fault-tolerant designs are how you make high availability reliable rather than lucky.
"We added a second server, so now we're highly available."Redundancy at one layer is worthless if the next layer is still a SPOF. Two app servers pointed at one database, or two servers behind one load balancer, or everything in one data center — each leaves a single point of failure. You must hunt SPOFs at every layer and across failure domains.
"More components means more reliability."Only when they're redundant (parallel). Components in series (a chain of dependencies) multiply failure — each one you add makes the whole system less available. More microservices in the critical path is a reliability tax, not a bonus.
"We have a replica, so failover is instant and free."Failover takes time — detect, decide, promote, redirect — and that window is your downtime (MTTR). It's also a rarely-run, under-tested code path that tends to fail exactly when a real incident triggers it. If you never practice failure, your failover will break when you need it.
"As long as the system stays up, we're fine.""Up" is not "correct." A botched failover can cause split-brain — two primaries accepting conflicting writes — so the system stays available while corrupting data. Available-but-wrong can be worse than honestly-down. Aim for uptime you can trust.

Active Recall

❓ Your API runs on two app servers behind a load balancer, both talking to a single primary database, all in one cloud data center. Leadership asks you to hit "four nines" (99.99%). Walk through: (1) where the single points of failure still are, (2) what you'd change to remove them, (3) the availability math that explains why the current chain is the problem, and (4) the one failure mode your fixes could introduce. (click to reveal)
(1) Remaining SPOFs: the database is a single primary (dies → both app servers are useless), the load balancer may be a single instance (dies → no traffic reaches anyone), and the data center is a single failure domain (power/network loss → everything dies at once). Two app servers fixed only the app tier; the chain below and around them is still single-threaded. (2) Fixes: add a database replica with active-passive failover (promote on primary death); make the load balancer redundant (a second LB / a managed HA load balancer); and spread every copy across at least two availability zones so no single building outage takes you down. That turns each layer from a chain link into a redundant pair. (3) The math: in series, availabilities multiply — even if every layer were 99.9%, chaining LB × app × DB gives 0.999³ ≈ 99.7%, well short of four nines, because the request needs all of them up at once. Redundancy flips the math: two 99% replicas in parallel fail together only 0.01% of the time → 99.99%, because now only one needs to survive. You reach four nines by making the critical path short and every link redundant across failure domains. (4) The new failure mode: automatic database failover can cause split-brain — if the network partitions, the old primary and the newly-promoted replica may both accept writes and diverge. You must fence the old primary (stop its writes) before promoting, or use quorum/consensus, so you don't trade downtime for data corruption. Mantra: short chains, redundant links, spread across failure domains — and fence before you promote.

Real-World Engineering

🎬 Netflix & Chaos Monkey

Netflix built a tool that deliberately kills its own production servers at random during business hours. The logic is brutal and brilliant: if you can't survive a single instance dying on a Tuesday afternoon, you've built fragility you don't know about. By practicing failure constantly, failover becomes a well-worn, trusted path instead of an untested 3 a.m. gamble.

☁️ Cloud Availability Zones

AWS, GCP, and Azure structure every region as multiple availability zones — physically separate data centers with independent power, cooling, and networking. The entire cloud reliability model rests on spreading copies across AZs so a single building failure can't take your system down. "Multi-AZ" is the industry's baseline for a serious production system.

🏦 Banks & five nines

Core banking, payment rails, and telecom switches genuinely target 99.999% — about five minutes of downtime a year. They pay for it with multi-region redundancy, hot standbys, rigorous failover drills, and dedicated reliability teams. It's a vivid reminder that the last nines are astronomically expensive and only justified when an outage costs millions per minute.

📉 The single-region outage

Every few years a major cloud region has a big outage and a huge slice of the internet goes down together — because countless companies ran everything in one region. The survivors are the ones who'd invested in multi-region redundancy. These events are the industry's recurring, public lesson in failure domains: redundancy only helps against failures your copies don't share.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What's the difference between fault tolerance and high availability?
A: High availability is the goal — the fraction of time a system is usable (the "nines"). Fault tolerance is a key technique for achieving it: designing the system to keep working correctly when components fail. Availability is what you promise; fault tolerance is largely how you keep the promise.
Q: Why does adding a service to the critical path lower availability, but adding a replica raise it?
A: Dependencies in series multiply availabilities (A₁×A₂×…) — the request needs them all, so each factor <1 shrinks the total. Redundant copies in parallel follow 1−(1−A)ⁿ — the request needs only one, so each spare shrinks the chance everything's down at once. Series multiplies risk; parallel multiplies safety.
Q: What is a single point of failure, and how do you eliminate one?
A: A SPOF is any one component whose death takes the whole system down. You eliminate it with redundancy (N+1/N+2 copies) plus failure detection (health checks/heartbeats) and failover, spread across failure domains so the copies don't die together. Hunt them by tracing a request and asking "what if this dies?" at every hop.
Q: What does Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) tell you to optimize?
A: You raise availability by breaking less often (bigger MTBF) or recovering faster (smaller MTTR). Past a point, fast recovery is the cheaper, higher-leverage lever — invest in fast detection, automated failover, instant rollback, and good runbooks rather than only trying to make things never fail.
Q: What is split-brain and why is it dangerous?
A: During a network partition, a failover can promote a replica while the old primary is still alive — leaving two primaries both accepting conflicting writes, which then diverge. It's dangerous because the system stays up while going wrong: available-but-corrupt. Cures include fencing the old primary and using quorum/consensus. ("Up" ≠ "correct.")

Fault tolerance keeps a system working correctly through component failures; high availability keeps it up and responsive, measured in nines (99.9% = 8.7 hours down a year; 99.99% = 52 minutes; 99.999% = 5 minutes).
