Bloom Filters & HyperLogLog
Remembering Almost Everything With Almost Nothing

Yesterday (Day 35) we learned to track the order of events cheaply. Today we track two other things cheaply: membership — "have I seen this exact item before?" — and cardinality — "how many different items have I seen?" The honest answer to both is a giant set holding every item you've ever seen. It's perfectly accurate and ruinously expensive: a billion items is gigabytes of RAM and a fat cloud bill. So we cheat — carefully. A Bloom filter is a bit array plus a handful of hash functions that answers "is X in the set?" with a hard guarantee: it never says no when the answer is yes (no false negatives), but it may occasionally say "probably yes" when the answer is actually no (a false positive). "Definitely not here" is a certainty you can bank on — skip the disk read, skip the recommendation — while "probably here" just means go confirm against the real store. A HyperLogLog estimates how many distinct items you've seen — billions of uniques in about 12 KB, within ~2% — by remembering the longest run of leading zeros across many little registers, and two of them merge by taking the max of each register, so you can roll hourly counts up to monthly without rescanning a thing. The single idea underneath both: trade a sliver of accuracy for an enormous saving in space. We'll play with a live Bloom filter until you watch a false positive appear, draw the machinery, and learn the one rule that keeps you out of trouble.

Picture this

A wildly popular nightclub. The line is around the block, and the doorman has a problem: he needs to know, instantly, whether someone is already on tonight's guest list — but a thick paper book of a hundred thousand names is far too slow and heavy to carry. So instead of a book, he has a board of light switches. When a guest is added to the list, the doorman doesn't write their name anywhere — he runs their name through three little rulebooks (hash functions), each of which points at one switch, and he flips those three switches ON. To check if someone's on the list, he runs their name through the same three rulebooks and looks at the three switches they point to. If even one of those switches is OFF, that person was definitely never added — he waves them away with total confidence. If all three are ON, they're probably on the list… but here's the catch: those switches might have been flipped on by three different other guests. So "all on" means probably yes, not certainly yes. And notice — he can never turn a switch off (someone else might be relying on it), so you can't remove a guest from this kind of list. That switchboard is a Bloom filter.

Now the club owner has a second question: how many different people came tonight? Counting every face is hopeless at 10,000 guests. So the bouncer plays a game: as each guest enters, they flip a coin until it comes up tails, and shout out their streak of heads. Most people get a streak of 1 or 2; getting a streak of 8 heads in a row is rare — it only tends to happen if a huge number of people have played. The bouncer doesn't remember any names or faces; he only remembers the single longest streak he heard all night. A longest streak of 8 whispers "roughly 2⁸ ≈ 256 people came." To make the guess sharper he runs the game at many doors at once and averages. That coin game is a HyperLogLog.

Burn in the image: a nightclub that remembers every guest with a board of switches it can flip on but never off (Bloom filter — "have I seen this one?"), and a bouncer who sizes the whole crowd by the single luckiest streak he overheard (HyperLogLog — "how many different ones?"). Both throw away the actual names. Both answer in a tiny, fixed amount of space. Both are a little bit wrong on purpose — and that's exactly why they're cheap enough to use at a billion-guest scale.

The Story

Otto ran the feed infrastructure for a fast-growing social app. Every time a user opened the app, the service asked a simple question before picking what to show: "has this user already seen this post?" The obvious answer was a set: store every (user, post) pair the user had scrolled past, and check membership on each candidate. It worked beautifully — and then the app hit 50 million users, each with thousands of seen posts, and the "seen set" in Redis swelled past 400 GB of RAM. The finance team circled Otto's desk. The set was correct, but correctness at that size was bankrupting them.

A teammate suggested a Bloom filter per user: a few kilobytes of bits instead of a list of every post ID. Otto balked — "it can be wrong." True, the teammate said, but think about how it's wrong. When the filter says "definitely not seen," it is never lying — so you can confidently treat the post as new. When it says "probably seen," it's right the vast majority of the time, and on the rare false positive you simply skip one post the user might actually have enjoyed. Nobody is harmed; the feed just drops a single candidate now and then. Otto shipped it. Memory per user fell from megabytes to a couple of kilobytes — a 100× cut — and the feed got faster, because most "have I seen this?" checks were now answered in cache instead of hitting the big store.

Emboldened, Otto reached for probabilistic structures again when the product team asked for "unique viewers per video per day" across billions of view events. Storing every viewer ID in a set per video was the 400 GB problem all over again. He used a HyperLogLog instead: ~12 KB per video, counting distinct viewers within ~2%. The part that made the product team gasp was the merge: because two HyperLogLogs combine by taking the max of each register, Otto could keep one HLL per video per hour, then union 24 of them for the daily number and 30 dailies for the monthly number — without ever rescanning the raw events. Dashboards that used to take an overnight batch job now refreshed in milliseconds.

Then came the lesson that scared him straight. Flush with success, Otto used a Bloom filter to answer "has this customer already been charged this month?" to avoid double-billing. It worked in testing. In production, a false positive told the system a customer had been charged when they hadn't — so the charge was skipped, and that customer never got billed. The bug wasn't in the Bloom filter; it did exactly what it promised. The bug was Otto's: he had used a "probably" answer as if it were the truth, in a place where being wrong even once was unacceptable.

His rule, taped to the monitor ever after: a probabilistic data structure is a cheap FILTER you put in front of the truth — never the truth itself. Trust its "no" absolutely; verify its "yes"; and never let an approximate answer make a decision that can't be wrong.

What should stay in your head
An exact set or exact distinct-count is correct but costs O(n) memory — fine until n is billions. Probabilistic data structures answer the same questions in a tiny, fixed amount of space by accepting a small, tunable error. A Bloom filter answers set membership: a bit array of m bits plus k hash functions. To add, hash the item k ways and set those k bits to 1; to query, hash the same way — if any of the k bits is 0 the item is definitely absent, and if all are 1 it is probably present. So it has no false negatives (a "no" is always true) but possible false positives (a "yes" can be wrong, because bits are shared). You can add but not delete (clearing a bit could erase someone else's item — a counting Bloom filter trades space to allow deletes). Accuracy tunes with size: more bits m and the right number of hashes (k ≈ (m/n)·ln2) → fewer false positives. A HyperLogLog answers cardinality — how many distinct items — in ~12 KB for billions, within ~2%: hash each item, look at the run of leading zeros (a run of r zeros is as rare as 1/2^r, so the longest run you've seen hints at ~2^r distinct items), split items across many registers to kill the variance, and take a corrected harmonic mean. HLLs are mergeable (union = element-wise max of registers), so counts roll up without rescanning. One-line test: a Bloom filter tells you "have I seen this one?" (trust the no, verify the yes); a HyperLogLog tells you "how many different ones?" (within a couple of percent) — both by trading a little accuracy for a huge saving in space.

Why This Problem Exists

Both questions — "have I seen this before?" and "how many different things have I seen?" — have a textbook answer: keep a set. Put every item in a hash set; membership is a lookup, and the distinct count is the set's size. For a thousand items, or a million, that's exactly what you should do. The trouble is that a set stores the items themselves, so its memory grows linearly with the number of distinct items — O(n). At internet scale, n is not a million; it's billions, multiplied by millions of users or millions of keys, and "just keep a set" quietly turns into hundreds of gigabytes of RAM per feature.

Worse, you often don't even need a perfect answer. If a recommendation engine occasionally re-shows a post you've already seen, or a "unique visitors" dashboard reads 1,004,210 instead of the true 1,002,998, nobody's day is ruined. We are paying the full price of exactness for answers where approximate is plenty. That mismatch is the opening. If we're willing to be wrong by a controlled, tiny amount, we can stop storing the items at all and instead store a fingerprint of the set in a fixed amount of space — a few kilobytes that doesn't grow whether you've seen a thousand items or a billion.

The cost we're trying to escape
# Exact set: store every item → memory grows with n seen = set() for item in stream: # 1,000,000,000 items... seen.add(item) # ...≈ tens of GB of RAM. Correct, but it does not fit. # "is X present?" → X in seen (exact, but the whole set must be in memory) # "how many unique?" → len(seen) (exact, but same cost) # Probabilistic: store a FINGERPRINT of fixed size → memory does NOT grow with n bloom = BloomFilter(bits=8_000_000) # ~1 MB answers "seen?" for millions of items hll = HyperLogLog() # ~12 KB counts billions of uniques within ~2%

That is the whole game of this lesson: give up exactness you don't need, and the memory bill collapses from "grows forever" to "a fixed, tiny constant." The art is in giving up the right exactness — a Bloom filter gives up certainty on "yes" but keeps certainty on "no"; a HyperLogLog gives up the exact count but keeps it within a couple of percent. Choosing the structure means choosing which small error you can live with.

Bloom Filters — "Have I Seen This One?"

A Bloom filter is just a bit array of length m (all zero to start) and k independent hash functions, each mapping an item to a position in [0, m). Two operations, both tiny:

Bloom filter — the whole algorithm
# bits[0..m) all start at 0; k hash functions h1..hk def add(item): for i in 1..k: bits[ hi(item) % m ] = 1 # flip k bits ON (never OFF) def might_contain(item): for i in 1..k: if bits[ hi(item) % m ] == 0: return False # a 0 bit ⇒ DEFINITELY NOT added. 100% certain. return True # all 1s ⇒ PROBABLY present (might be a false positive)

The asymmetry is the entire point, and it's worth saying slowly:

✔ No false negatives.   If an item was added, its k bits were set to 1 and bits are never cleared — so a later query can never find a 0 for it. A "no" is always the truth.
✘ Possible false positives.   Different items share the array, so an item you never added can have all k of its bits already set by other items. A "yes" means "probably" — go confirm.

Tuning the error — bits, hashes, and the false-positive rate

The false-positive probability depends on three numbers: the array size m, the number of items inserted n, and the number of hashes k. After inserting n items, the chance a query returns a false "yes" is approximately p ≈ (1 − e^(−kn/m))^k. Two knobs fall out of it:

  • More bits per item (m/n larger) always lowers p. A common rule of thumb: about 9.6 bits per item buys ~1% false positives, and every extra ~4.8 bits per item cuts the rate by another 10×.
  • The optimal number of hashes is k ≈ (m/n)·ln2. Too few hashes and single collisions dominate; too many and you set so many bits that the array saturates. There's a sweet spot — which is exactly the "saturation" effect you can feel in the simulator when you crank k on a small board.

You can add, but you can't delete

Because a single 1-bit may be "holding up" several different items at once, you cannot safely flip it back to 0 to remove one item — you'd silently evict the others and create false negatives, breaking the one guarantee that makes the structure useful. The standard fix is a counting Bloom filter: replace each bit with a small counter that you increment on add and decrement on delete. It supports removal, at the cost of several bits per slot instead of one. When even the set of items changes over time and must shrink, people reach for cuckoo filters or scalable/partitioned Bloom filters instead.

HyperLogLog — "How Many Different Ones?"

A Bloom filter answers membership. HyperLogLog answers a different question: cardinality — the number of distinct items in a stream — and it does so in astonishingly little space (about 12 KB for billions of uniques, within ~2%). It never stores the items; it stores a clever statistic about their hashes.

The coin-flip intuition

Hash every item to a random-looking bit string. In a truly random bit string, the chance it starts with exactly r zeros then a one is 1/2^(r+1) — a run of 5 leading zeros is about a 1-in-32 event, a run of 10 is about 1-in-1024. So if, across all the hashes you've seen, the longest run of leading zeros is r, that's evidence you've probably processed on the order of 2^r distinct items (duplicates hash to the same string, so they don't push the record any higher — which is exactly why it counts distinct things). It's the same logic as the bouncer's game: a long lucky streak only tends to show up when a lot of people have played.

One record is a wildly noisy estimate, though — you might get a 10-zero fluke from only a handful of items. HyperLogLog's fix: split items into many registers (say 16,384 of them) using the first few hash bits to pick a register, track the maximum leading-zero run within each register, and then combine all the registers with a bias-corrected harmonic mean. Averaging thousands of independent estimates collapses the noise to a predictable standard error of ≈ 1.04/√(registers) — 16,384 registers → about 0.8% error, in ~12 KB total (each register is only ~6 bits, since it just stores a small zero-count).

HyperLogLog — the shape of it
# M = array of P registers (e.g. 16384), all 0 def add(item): x = hash(item) j = first_p_bits(x) # which register this item belongs to w = rest_of_bits(x) M[j] = max( M[j], leading_zeros(w) + 1 ) # keep the record run for this bucket def count(): return harmonic_mean_estimate(M) # ≈ distinct items, ~2% error def merge(A, B): # union of two HLLs — the superpower return [ max(A[j], B[j]) for j in registers ] # element-wise max; no rescanning

Why "mergeable" changes everything

Because the union of two HyperLogLogs is just the element-wise max of their registers, counts compose. Keep one HLL per video per hour; the day's unique count is the merge of 24 hourly HLLs; the month's is the merge of the dailies — all without ever re-reading a raw event. The same property lets you shard the work across a hundred machines, each counting a slice of the stream, then merge their tiny HLLs at the end. Try doing that with exact sets and you're back to shuffling gigabytes around. (Cousins in the same family: count-min sketch for approximate frequencies, and t-digest / q-digest for approximate percentiles — same spirit, different question.)

They're not rivals — they answer different questions, and both are usually a front end to an exact system, not a replacement for it. The decision is always the same two questions: which fact do I need (membership or count), and how wrong am I allowed to be?

The Hard Parts

Probabilistic structures are a superpower with a very specific blast radius. Know the edges before you deploy one.

A "yes" is never trustworthy on its own

A Bloom filter's false positive is not a bug you can eliminate — it's the price of the space saving. So the safe pattern is always the same: use the "no" to skip work with total confidence, and treat every "yes" as "maybe — now go ask the real store." If being wrong on a "yes" is unacceptable (billing, security allow-lists, correctness invariants), a Bloom filter must sit in front of the source of truth, never instead of it. Otto's billing bug was this rule violated.

You can't read the data back out

Neither structure stores the items, so neither can enumerate them. A Bloom filter can't tell you which items it contains, only "probably/definitely-not this one"; a HyperLogLog can give you a count but never the list of distinct values. If you need to iterate, audit, or delete specific entries, you need the real data too.

Sizing is a commitment you make up front

A Bloom filter's false-positive rate is only as good as the m and k you chose for an expected n. Insert far more items than you planned and the array saturates — bits fill up, and eventually it answers "probably yes" to everything, silently becoming useless (you felt this in the simulator). Either estimate n generously, or use a scalable Bloom filter that adds capacity as it fills. HyperLogLog is friendlier here — its error is roughly constant across a huge range of cardinalities — but very small counts need special handling (real implementations switch to a sparse/exact mode for the first few thousand items to avoid a systematic bias).

Hash quality matters

Both structures assume their hash functions spread items uniformly. A weak or biased hash clusters bits (worse false positives) or skews leading-zero runs (worse count estimates). This is why production code uses strong, fast non-cryptographic hashes (MurmurHash, xxHash) and why a Bloom filter typically derives its k positions from just two base hashes via double hashing rather than running k separate ones.

The through-line: these structures don't make error go away — they make it small, bounded, and one-sided, in exchange for memory that no longer grows. Use them where an approximate answer is genuinely good enough, and as a cheap guard in front of the exact truth — never as the exact truth.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"A Bloom filter can give a wrong 'no'."Never. It has no false negatives — a "no" is always true, because set bits are never cleared. Only "yes" can be wrong (a false positive).
"If it says the item is present, the item is present.""Present" means probably present. Other items can set all of its bits. Always confirm a 'yes' against the real store if being wrong matters.
"I can delete from a Bloom filter by clearing its bits."No — a bit may be shared by other items, so clearing it creates false negatives. Use a counting Bloom filter (or cuckoo filter) if you need deletes.
"More hash functions always means fewer false positives."No — there's an optimum (k ≈ (m/n)·ln2). Too many hashes saturate the array and raise the false-positive rate. You saw this in the simulator.
"HyperLogLog stores the IDs it counted."It stores no items — just a few thousand tiny registers of leading-zero records. It can give you a count, never the list of distinct values.
"HyperLogLog is exact / its error grows with scale."It's an estimate with ~constant relative error (≈1.04/√registers, ~2% for 4096 registers) across a huge range — and it's mergeable, so counts roll up without rescanning.
"Use them to replace the database."They're a cheap filter in front of the source of truth, not a replacement. Trust the "no," verify the "yes," and keep the exact data where correctness is required.

Active Recall

❓ A database keeps 500 on-disk files (SSTables). To answer GET user:42 it must, in the worst case, check every file on disk — expensive. You add a Bloom filter per file. (1) How does that make the common case fast, and which of the filter's two answers are you relying on? (2) What happens on a false positive, and why is it merely a small cost rather than a correctness bug? (click to reveal)
(1) You rely on the "no". Before touching a file on disk, you ask that file's Bloom filter "might you contain user:42?" For the vast majority of files the honest answer is "definitely not" — and because a Bloom filter has no false negatives, you can skip those files with total confidence, without any disk I/O. Only the one (or few) files whose filter says "probably yes" actually get read. Many disk seeks collapse to one. The whole speed-up comes from trusting the certain "no."

(2) A false positive just wastes one lookup. Occasionally a file's filter says "probably yes" for a key that isn't actually in that file. You read the file, don't find the key, and move on — you've paid for one unnecessary disk read, nothing more. The result is still correct, because the file read is the source of truth; the filter only decided whether to bother looking. That's the canonical safe pattern: the Bloom filter is a cheap guard in front of the exact data, so its only failure mode is doing a bit of extra work, never returning a wrong answer. (This is exactly how Cassandra, RocksDB, and Bigtable use Bloom filters.)

Real-World Engineering

🗄️ Cassandra · RocksDB · Bigtable

Every on-disk SSTable carries a Bloom filter of its keys. A read consults the filters first and skips any file that definitely doesn't hold the key, turning a potential fan-out of disk seeks into (usually) a single one. It's the textbook production use of "trust the no."

📊 Redis · BigQuery · Presto

Redis ships HyperLogLog as a native type — PFADD / PFCOUNT / PFMERGE in ~12 KB per key — for unique-visitor style metrics. BigQuery (APPROX_COUNT_DISTINCT) and Presto/Trino (approx_distinct) use HLL so "count distinct over a billion rows" returns in seconds instead of melting the cluster.

🛡️ Chrome Safe Browsing & Medium

Google Chrome historically used a Bloom-filter-style local set of malicious URLs so most sites could be cleared without a network call, only checking the server on a "maybe." Medium famously used Bloom filters to avoid recommending articles you'd already read — a false positive just drops one suggestion.

🌐 CDNs & one-hit-wonders

A huge fraction of CDN requests are for URLs fetched exactly once. Caching those wastes space, so CDNs (e.g. Akamai-style designs) use a Bloom filter to cache an object only on its second sighting — "have I seen this URL before?" — dramatically improving hit rates with a few kilobytes of state.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What does a Bloom filter guarantee, and what can it get wrong?
A: It guarantees no false negatives — a "no" (some probed bit is 0) is always true. It can produce false positives — a "yes" (all k bits set) may be wrong because items share the array. So: trust the no, verify the yes.
Q: How do you add to and query a Bloom filter?
A: Add: hash the item with k functions, set those k bits to 1. Query: hash the same way — if any of the k bits is 0 → definitely absent; if all are 1 → probably present. Bits are never cleared, so you can't delete (without a counting variant).
Q: Why does cranking up k (hashes) on a small array make things worse?
A: Each add sets more bits, so the array saturates faster. Past the optimum k ≈ (m/n)·ln2, extra hashes fill the board and raise the false-positive rate — eventually every query reads "probably yes."
Q: In one line, how does HyperLogLog estimate distinct count?
A: Hash each item; the longest run of leading zeros hints at ~2^r distinct items (a run of r is ~1/2^r rare). Split across many registers, keep each register's max run, and take a bias-corrected harmonic mean → billions of uniques in ~12 KB within ~2%.
Q: What makes HyperLogLog "mergeable," and why does it matter?
A: The union of two HLLs is the element-wise max of their registers. So you can shard counting across machines, or roll hourly → daily → monthly counts, by merging tiny sketches without rescanning the raw events.

If you only remember one thing: when exactness is too expensive and an approximate answer is good enough, trade a sliver of accuracy for a fixed, tiny amount of memory. A Bloom filter answers "have I seen this one?" with no false negatives (trust the "no" — skip the work) but possible false positives (verify the "yes") in a few bits per item, and it can add but not delete. A HyperLogLog answers "how many different ones?" within ~2% using ~12 KB for billions, by remembering the longest run of leading zeros across many registers — and because HLLs merge, counts roll up without rescanning. The golden rule, learned the hard way: these are a cheap filter in front of the truth, never the truth itself.
