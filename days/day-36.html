Bloom Filters & HyperLogLog
Being Approximately Right in Almost No Memory

Yesterday's vector clocks (Day 35) tracked order cheaply. Today we track two other things cheaply: membership — "have I seen this exact item before?" — and cardinality — "how many distinct items have I seen?" At web scale you ask these billions of times a second (every URL a crawler finds, every user ID hitting a dashboard), and the honest answer — keep a set containing every item — is exact but ruinously expensive: a billion items is gigabytes of RAM you don't have. So we cheat, on purpose. A Bloom filter answers membership with a small bit array and a few hash functions: a "no" is always right (no false negatives), a "yes" is "probably" (a tiny, tunable false-positive rate) — a billion items in a few hundred MB instead of tens of GB. HyperLogLog answers cardinality by watching the longest run of leading zeros in hashed values — a rare long streak betrays a huge crowd — counting billions of unique items to within ~2% using about 1.5 KB, and merging across servers with a one-line per-register max. You'll insert into a live Bloom filter and watch it produce a real false positive, stream items into a HyperLogLog sketch and watch 256 bytes track a growing unique count, and see exactly where the accuracy goes — and why trading it is the best deal in systems engineering.

Picture this

It's a huge music festival — a million people pouring through the gates — and the organizers are too cheap to buy a computer. At the entrance stands a bouncer with a wall of light bulbs. When you arrive, she doesn't write your name anywhere. She just runs your name through three little stencils (think of them as three different ways of looking at the name), and each stencil points at one specific bulb on the wall, which she flicks on. Three bulbs lit per guest. Later, someone asks: "did Priya come in?" The bouncer runs "Priya" through the same three stencils and checks those three bulbs. If any of them is dark, she can say with total certainty: "No — Priya definitely never walked through this gate" (if Priya had, those bulbs would be lit). But if all three happen to be lit, she can only say "probably yes" — because those three bulbs might have been lit by three other guests whose stencils happened to overlap with Priya's. That innocent overlap is a false positive. And notice: she can never confidently turn a bulb off (some other guest may be relying on it), so she can't "remove" anyone. That wall of bulbs is a Bloom filter.

Across the field is a second gadget, run by a bouncer who is even lazier: he refuses to remember anything about who came. Instead, every guest flips a coin over and over until they get their first tails, and shouts out their streak of heads ("I got 4 heads then tails!"). The bouncer only ever remembers the single longest streak he has heard all night. At the end he announces the crowd size with a weirdly accurate trick: "the longest streak I heard was about 20 heads in a row — that only tends to happen when roughly a million people are flipping — so there were about a million of you." He counted a million unique people by remembering one number. That is HyperLogLog.

Burn in the two scenes side by side: a wall of light bulbs that can tell you "definitely not here" or "probably here" using almost no space but can never forget (Bloom filter → membership); and a bouncer who remembers only the longest coin-flip streak and reads the crowd size off it (HyperLogLog → count of distinct things). Both throw away the ability to be exactly right in exchange for being nearly right in a space so small it fits in your pocket. That trade is the entire lesson.

The Story

Priya ran the web crawler for a search startup — a swarm of machines that follow links across the internet, fetching pages. The crawler had one humble but relentless need: before fetching a URL, ask "have we already crawled this one?" Miss it and you re-download the same page a thousand times, melting your bandwidth and hammering other people's servers. Easy, she thought — keep a hash set of every URL seen. It worked beautifully… until the set held two billion URLs, each ~70 bytes, and the "just remember what we've seen" structure demanded well over 100 GB of RAM on every crawler. The exact answer was correct and completely unaffordable.

A colleague drew her a Bloom filter on a napkin: forget storing the URLs themselves — keep a long array of bits, all zero to start. To "add" a URL, run it through k hash functions, each pointing at one bit, and set those k bits to 1. To check a URL, hash it the same way and look at those bits: if any is 0, you have never seen it (certainty); if all are 1, you've probably seen it. Priya sized it for a 1% false-positive rate and the whole thing fit in a couple of gigabytes — a 50× cut. Yes, ~1% of genuinely-new URLs would be wrongly skipped as "seen" — but skipping a handful of pages out of billions was a trade she'd take every single day. The crawler stopped choking.

Months later, marketing burst in with a different question: "how many unique visitors did the site have today — across all 50 edge servers?" Priya's first instinct — collect every visitor ID into one giant set and count it — meant shipping hundreds of millions of IDs across the network to a single machine every night. Painful, slow, and it fell over on big days. Then she remembered the crawler trick had a cousin for counting: HyperLogLog. Each edge server kept a tiny fixed-size sketch — about 1.5 KB — updated by hashing each visitor ID and noting the longest run of leading zeros it had ever seen. At midnight the 50 servers shipped their 1.5 KB sketches (not their millions of IDs) to one place, which merged them by taking the per-register max and read off the answer: "≈ 48.6 million unique visitors, ±2%." The nightly report that used to move gigabytes now moved 75 kilobytes.

Her takeaway, taped to the monitor: when a question is asked a billion times, you rarely need the exact answer — you need a good-enough answer that fits in memory. A Bloom filter gives you membership (maybe-yes / definitely-no) in a bit array; HyperLogLog gives you a distinct-count (±a few percent) in a sketch you can merge across a fleet. The magic isn't in being clever about storing data — it's in refusing to store the data at all, and keeping only the faint statistical shadow it casts.

What should stay in your head
Both are probabilistic sketches: they answer a question at massive scale in a tiny, fixed amount of memory, by giving up exactness for a bounded, tunable error. A Bloom filter answers membership — "is X in the set?" — with a bit array of size m and k hash functions: add = set the k bits X hashes to; query = are all k of X's bits set? A "no" is always correct (no false negatives); a "yes" is probably (a tunable false-positive rate that rises as the filter fills). It cannot delete, cannot list its contents, and cannot count — it only ever answers maybe-in / definitely-out. A HyperLogLog answers cardinality — "how many distinct items?" — by hashing each item and remembering, per bucket, the longest run of leading zeros seen; a long run is statistically rare, so its presence implies a large population (estimate ≈ 2^(max run), refined by averaging many buckets). It counts billions to within ~2% using ~1.5 KB, is idempotent (adding the same item twice changes nothing — it counts distinct), and is mergeable (union two sketches by taking the per-bucket max — perfect for combining across servers). One-line test: a Bloom filter tells you whether you've probably seen a specific thing; a HyperLogLog tells you roughly how many different things you've seen — both by keeping a statistical shadow instead of the data itself.

Why This Problem Exists

Every data structure you've met so far — hash tables, B-trees, the sets behind a database index — is built to be exact. Ask "is this key present?" and it tells you yes or no, correctly, every time. That exactness has a price you usually don't notice: to answer perfectly, the structure must store something about every item it has ever seen. A set of n items costs O(n) memory, full stop. For a to-do list that's nothing. For a system seeing billions of items — URLs, user IDs, event keys, cache keys — O(n) is tens or hundreds of gigabytes of RAM per machine, and often it simply won't fit.

Now notice something about the questions we actually ask at that scale. "Have I seen this URL?" doesn't always need to be perfect — if we occasionally re-crawl one page by mistake, the sky doesn't fall. "How many unique visitors today?" almost never needs to be exact — no dashboard is improved by knowing it was 48,623,901 rather than "about 48.6 million." We've been paying for precision we don't need. That gap — between the exact answer we're buying and the approximate answer we'd happily accept — is money left on the table, and probabilistic data structures are how you pick it up.

The trade, in one comparison
# Count 1,000,000,000 distinct user IDs. "Is X present?" and "how many distinct?" EXACT set / hash table ~16–40 GB RAM perfect answers, every query Bloom filter (1% FP) ~1.2 GB membership: "no" exact, "yes" 99% right HyperLogLog ~1.5 KB distinct count within ~2% # From 40 GB to 1.5 KB is a ~25,000,000× memory cut — in exchange for a few % # of error you were never going to act on anyway. THAT is why these exist.

The deep idea is the same one that powered yesterday's logical clocks: stop storing the data; store only the faint statistical shadow it casts. A Bloom filter keeps the shadow of "which hash positions have ever been touched." A HyperLogLog keeps the shadow of "the rarest hash pattern I've ever seen." Neither can reconstruct the original items — that information is gone forever — but each can answer one specific question about them with astonishing economy. Choosing the right shadow for the question is the whole art.

Bloom Filters — Membership for Almost Free

A Bloom filter is a bit array B of m bits (all 0 at the start) plus k independent hash functions, each mapping an item to a position in [0, m). That's the entire structure. The operations are tiny:

Bloom filter — the whole algorithm
# bit array B[0..m-1] all zero; k hash functions h1..hk add(x): for i in 1..k: B[ hi(x) % m ] = 1 # set k bits query(x): for i in 1..k: if B[ hi(x) % m ] == 0: return "DEFINITELY NOT present" return "PROBABLY present" # all k bits were 1

The asymmetry is the whole point, and it comes straight from the logic. If you added x, you set all k of its bits, so a later query for x can never find a 0 — a true member is never reported absent. That gives the headline guarantee:

✔ "Definitely not present" is always true   — no false negatives. If any of x's k bits is 0, x was never added, period.
✘ "Probably present" can be wrong   — a false positive. x's k bits may all have been set by other items that happened to hash there. The more items you add, the more bits are 1, and the higher this chance.

So a Bloom filter is a one-way gate: it reliably tells you what you've never seen, and only probably what you have. That's exactly the right shape for a cheap first check in front of something expensive. "Is this key maybe in the database?" — if the Bloom filter says "definitely not," skip the disk read entirely; if it says "maybe," pay for the real lookup. You spend a few bits to avoid most of the expensive work, and the rare false positive only costs you a lookup that turns up empty — never a wrong answer, because the real store has the final say.

Sizing it: the three knobs

The false-positive rate is not a mystery — it's a dial you set. For n expected items, a target false-positive probability p, bit-array size m, and hash count k, the math is:

# optimal sizing for n items at false-positive rate p m = ceil( -n · ln(p) / (ln 2)² ) # bits needed (p=1% → ~9.6 bits/item) k = round( (m / n) · ln 2 ) # optimal number of hash functions # p = 1% → ~9.6 bits/item, k ≈ 7 p = 0.1% → ~14.4 bits/item, k ≈ 10 # ⇒ a BILLION items at 1% ≈ 1.2 GB, independent of how big each item is.

Two things fall out of this. First, cost is per item, not per byte: a 2 KB URL and a 4-byte integer each cost the same ~9.6 bits, because only the hashes are stored. Second, there's a sweet spot for k: too few hash functions and bits collide too easily; too many and you fill the array too fast. The formula lands you on the minimum false-positive rate for your space budget.

What it cannot do

A plain Bloom filter can't delete (clearing a bit might break some other item that shares it), can't list what's inside, and can't count how many times something was added. Each limitation has a cousin structure that buys the feature back at some cost: counting Bloom filters replace bits with small counters to allow deletion; cuckoo filters support deletion and often beat Bloom on space at low false-positive rates; scalable Bloom filters grow when they fill up. But the plain version — add and maybe-query — is the one you'll reach for 90% of the time.

HyperLogLog — Counting Billions in a Breath of Memory

Bloom filters answer "is this specific thing here?" HyperLogLog answers a completely different question: "how many distinct things are here?" (the "count-distinct" or "cardinality" problem). The exact way — a set that de-duplicates — costs O(n) again. HyperLogLog costs a fixed ~1.5 KB for any cardinality, from ten to ten billion. The trick is one of the most beautiful ideas in all of computer science, and it starts with a coin.

The coin-flip intuition

Flip a fair coin and count how many heads you get before the first tails. Getting 1 head is common (¼ of the time you even get that far after the first flip's logic); a run of 10 heads in a row is rare — it happens about once in 2¹⁰ ≈ 1000 tries. So if you walk into a room and someone tells you "the longest head-streak anyone here flipped was 10," you can reason backwards: a streak that rare probably required about a thousand people flipping. You estimated the crowd size from a single number — the record streak — without counting a single head.

HyperLogLog does exactly this, with hashes standing in for coins. Hash each item to a random-looking bit string; the leading bits of a good hash are like a run of coin flips. Count the position of the first 1-bit (= the run of leading zeros, plus one). Remember only the maximum such run you've ever seen. A long maximum run implies you must have hashed a lot of distinct items to stumble onto something that rare — so cardinality ≈ 2^(max run).

HyperLogLog — the whole algorithm
# m = 2^p registers (buckets), each a small integer, all 0 to start add(x): h = hash(x) # a well-mixed 32/64-bit value j = top p bits of h # which register this item lands in r = leadingZeros(rest of h) + 1 # the "coin-flip streak" for this item R[j] = max( R[j], r ) # keep only the record streak per register estimate(): # harmonic mean of the registers, bias-corrected E = αm · m² / Σj 2^(-R[j]) return E # (with small- and large-range corrections) merge(A, B): R[j] = max( A.R[j], B.R[j] ) # union = per-register max. that's it.

Two refinements turn the toy "2^(max run)" into the real, accurate estimator. First, a single max run is noisy — you might get unlucky. So HyperLogLog splits items into m = 2^p registers using the top p bits of the hash, tracks a separate max-run in each, and averages them (using the harmonic mean, which tames outliers). More registers → more averaging → less error: the standard error is about 1.04 / √m. With m = 2048 registers (~1.5 KB) that's ~2%. Second, small bias corrections patch up the estimate at the very low and very high ends.

The two superpowers that make HyperLogLog a distributed-systems staple: it is idempotent — adding the same item a thousand times only ever updates a register to the same max, so it genuinely counts distinct items and duplicates are free — and it is mergeable — the union of two sketches is just the element-wise max of their registers, with no loss of accuracy. That second property is gold: 50 edge servers can each keep their own sketch locally, and you combine them into a global unique-count by shipping 50 tiny sketches and taking maxes, instead of shuffling hundreds of millions of raw IDs to one machine. (Notice the merge rule is the same element-wise max you saw in yesterday's vector clocks — "keep the bigger number in each slot" is a recurring move for combining partial knowledge.)

The Hard Parts

These sketches are gorgeous, but they are sharp tools. Reach for them knowing exactly what you're giving up.

You are choosing to be wrong — make sure the wrongness is harmless

A Bloom filter's false positive and a HyperLogLog's ±2% are not bugs; they're the deal you signed. The engineering question is always: what happens on the rare wrong answer, and can the system absorb it? A Bloom filter in front of a database is safe — a false positive just causes one needless lookup that returns empty; the database is still the source of truth. A Bloom filter used to decide "this password has never been used, allow it" is dangerous — a false positive would reject a perfectly good password with no recourse. Same structure, opposite wisdom. Always ask whether a wrong "yes" (or a count that's off by 2%) is a shrug or a catastrophe.

No false negatives — unless you break the rules

The "a Bloom filter never has false negatives" guarantee holds only if you never remove anything from a plain Bloom filter. Clearing a bit to "delete" an item will silently also delete every other item that happened to share that bit, and now a true member can report "absent" — a false negative, the one thing Bloom filters promised never to do. If you need deletion, you need a counting Bloom filter or a cuckoo filter, not a hacked bit array.

They answer one question each — and only that one

A Bloom filter cannot tell you how many items it holds, cannot list them, and cannot answer "how many distinct?" A HyperLogLog cannot tell you whether a specific item is present, cannot give you the items, and cannot do set intersection accurately (estimating |A ∩ B| by inclusion-exclusion compounds both sketches' errors badly). Picking the sketch means picking the one question you'll be able to ask. Need several questions? You keep several sketches, or you keep the real data.

Hash quality and saturation are real failure modes

Both structures lean entirely on good, well-distributed hashes. A weak hash clusters bits (Bloom) or corrupts the leading-zero statistics (HLL) and quietly wrecks accuracy. And a Bloom filter has a capacity: size it for n items and then stuff in 10n, and nearly every bit becomes 1, so every query returns "probably present" — the filter degrades into a device that always says yes. Sizing for the real n (Day 30's back-of-envelope estimation, right here in the wild) is not optional.

The through-line: a probabilistic sketch is a precise instrument for imprecise answers. Its error is bounded and tunable, not random sloppiness — which is exactly why you can trust it, once you've decided the error is acceptable for the question you're asking. Use a Bloom filter to cheaply rule things out before expensive work; use HyperLogLog to count distinct things across a fleet; and keep the exact structure for the day you genuinely cannot be wrong.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"A Bloom filter can give a wrong answer either way."No — it is one-sided. A "definitely not present" is always correct (no false negatives); only "probably present" can be wrong (a false positive). That asymmetry is the entire reason it's useful as a cheap pre-filter.
"I'll just clear some bits to delete items from a Bloom filter."Clearing a bit can break every other item that shares it, introducing false negatives and destroying the core guarantee. Use a counting Bloom filter or a cuckoo filter if you need deletion.
"A Bloom filter tells me how many items it holds."It can't count, can't list its contents, and can't tell you cardinality. It only answers maybe-in / definitely-out for a specific item. Counting distinct items is HyperLogLog's job, not a Bloom filter's.
"HyperLogLog gives the exact unique count."It's an estimate (~2% standard error at ~1.5 KB). For counts that must be exact — billing, compliance — you need an exact structure. HLL is for dashboards and scale, where "about 48.6 million" is the right precision.
"Bigger items need a bigger Bloom filter / HLL."No — both store only hashes, so cost depends on the number of items, not their size. A 2 KB URL and a 4-byte int cost the same. Cost scales with n (Bloom) or is fixed regardless of n (HLL).
"I can intersect two HyperLogLogs to get |A ∩ B|."HLLs union perfectly (per-register max), but intersection via inclusion–exclusion compounds both sketches' errors and is often wildly off. Don't use HLL for accurate intersections.

Active Recall

❓ You put a Bloom filter in front of a key-value database so that reads for keys that don't exist can skip the disk. One day a lookup for a key you never inserted returns "probably present," so you do the disk read — and it comes back empty. (1) What just happened, and did your system return a wrong answer to the user? (2) Separately: your "unique active users this month" counter is a HyperLogLog that reports 10.2M; the exact number was 10.0M. Is the counter broken, and roughly what sketch size would give that error? (click to reveal)
(1) That was a false positive — and no, the user got the correct answer. The key's k bits all happened to be set by other keys, so the filter said "probably present." You paid the price of the trade: one needless disk read that returned empty. But the database is the source of truth, so the user still correctly learns the key doesn't exist. This is the safe way to use a Bloom filter — a false positive costs you a little work, never a wrong result. (If you ever got a false negative — "definitely not present" for a key that is there — that would be a real bug, and a plain Bloom filter cannot produce one unless someone "deleted" by clearing bits.)

(2) The counter is working as designed. 10.2M vs 10.0M is a 2% error, exactly the ballpark of HyperLogLog's standard error ≈ 1.04/√m. Solving 1.04/√m ≈ 0.02 gives m ≈ 2700 registers — i.e. the standard ~2 KB (m = 2048) sketch. Nothing is broken: you chose ~2% error in exchange for counting tens of millions of distinct users in two kilobytes that merge trivially across all your servers. If you truly needed exact, you'd pay O(n) memory and give up the free merge.

Real-World Engineering

🗄️ Cassandra, HBase, RocksDB & LSM-trees

Every LSM-tree store keeps a Bloom filter per on-disk SSTable so a read for a missing key can skip the file without touching disk. It's the single most common production Bloom filter on earth — the reason your "key not found" lookups are fast instead of grinding through every table on disk.

📊 Redis, Presto, BigQuery & Druid

Redis ships PFADD/PFCOUNT — a HyperLogLog counting uniques in ~12 KB. Presto/Trino, Google BigQuery (APPROX_COUNT_DISTINCT), and Apache Druid all use HLL so a COUNT(DISTINCT …) over billions of rows returns in moments instead of shuffling every value.

🌐 Chrome, CDNs & crawlers

Google Chrome once used a Bloom filter to check URLs against its malicious-site list locally before asking the server. Web crawlers use them for "already seen this URL?", and CDNs use them to avoid caching one-hit-wonder objects ("only cache on the second request") — a classic, cheap first-line filter.

🧪 The HyperLogLog pedigree

HLL descends from Flajolet & Martin's 1980s work, refined into HyperLogLog (2007) and Google's HyperLogLog++ (better bias correction + sparse representation for small counts). It's a rare case where a named academic algorithm ships, almost verbatim, inside databases you use every day.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What does a Bloom filter answer, and what is its one-sided guarantee?
A: Membership ("is X in the set?"). A "no" is always correct (no false negatives); a "yes" is "probably" (a tunable false-positive rate). It can't delete, list, or count.
Q: How do you add and query a Bloom filter?
A: add(x): set the k bits that x's k hashes point to. query(x): if any of those k bits is 0 → "definitely not present"; if all are 1 → "probably present."
Q: What does HyperLogLog answer, and what's the core intuition?
A: Cardinality ("how many distinct items?"). Intuition: hash each item; the longest run of leading zeros seen is like the longest coin-flip head-streak — a rare long run implies a large population, so count ≈ 2^(max run), averaged over many registers.
Q: Why is HyperLogLog ideal for distributed count-distinct?
A: It's fixed-size (~1.5 KB for any cardinality, ~2% error), idempotent (duplicates are free — counts distinct), and mergeable: union = per-register max, with no loss of accuracy. Each server keeps a tiny sketch; combine by taking maxes.
Q: When is a Bloom filter false positive safe, and when is it dangerous?
A: Safe when a wrong "yes" only costs extra work checked against a source of truth (e.g. a needless DB read that returns empty). Dangerous when the "yes" is itself the final decision (e.g. "this password was never used → allow"), where a false positive causes a wrong outcome.

  • Describe one place a false positive (or a 2% miscount) is harmless, and one place it would be a real bug.
If you only remember one thing: when a question is asked at massive scale, you can usually trade a sliver of accuracy for an enormous amount of memory — by storing the data's statistical shadow instead of the data. A Bloom filter answers membership with a bit array and k hashes: "definitely not present" is always true, "probably present" is a tunable false positive, and a billion items fit in ~1.2 GB instead of tens of GB — perfect as a cheap guard in front of expensive work (the LSM-tree "skip this SSTable" classic). HyperLogLog answers how many distinct? by watching the longest leading-zero run, counting billions to within ~2% in ~1.5 KB, idempotent and mergeable by per-register max — the engine behind every "unique visitors" metric. Choose a sketch by choosing the one question you need, and make sure the inevitable wrong answer is a shrug, not a catastrophe.
