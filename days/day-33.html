Distributed Transactions: 2PC & 3PC
All-or-Nothing Across Many Databases

Consensus (Days 31–32) solved one problem: get many machines to agree on a single ordered log. Today's problem looks the same but is quietly more dangerous — how do you make several separate databases all commit, or all abort, one transaction together, so money is never created in the gap or lost in the crash? On a single database the letter A in ACID (Day 9) was free — one write-ahead log, one disk, atomic. Across two databases there is no shared log, no shared disk, and a network that can drop any message. The classic answer is Two-Phase Commit (2PC): a coordinator, a Prepare phase where everyone locks in and promises, and a Commit phase where one global verdict is applied everywhere. It works beautifully — until the coordinator crashes in exactly the wrong half-second and leaves everyone frozen in-doubt, holding locks, unable to decide alone. We'll build 2PC from scratch, feel that blocking problem in a simulator, meet Three-Phase Commit (3PC) and the one hidden assumption that keeps it in textbooks rather than production, and see how Google Spanner runs 2PC safely by making every participant a Raft/Paxos group.

Picture this

A wedding at the altar. There is one officiant (the coordinator) and two people standing before them (two databases). The whole ceremony has a single, strict rule: the marriage happens only if both people say yes — one "no" and there is no wedding at all. There is no such thing as "half married."

So the officiant runs it in two phases. First the vows: "Do you take this person…?" — and each turns, in turn, and says "I do." Saying "I do" is not a casual nod; it is a binding promise. The moment you say it, you have locked yourself in — you cannot wander off, you cannot say "actually, never mind." You are prepared. Only once the officiant has heard "I do" from both does the second phase happen: "I now pronounce you married." That pronouncement is the commit — now, and only now, is it official and permanent for everyone at once.

Now the dark twist that is the whole reason this lesson has a sequel. Imagine both have said "I do," they are standing there vowed and locked in — and the officiant faints before pronouncing anything. What now? The two can't walk away (they already promised — maybe the officiant was about to say "married"). But they can't declare themselves married either (maybe the officiant fainted because they saw an objection). They can only stand frozen at the altar, waiting — and everyone queued up for the next wedding is stuck behind them. That frozen couple is a database in-doubt, holding its locks, blocking the system. Burn in the image: two phases — vows then pronouncement — and a fainted officiant leaving a couple frozen mid-ceremony. That frozen couple is 2PC's fatal flaw, and the reason 3PC exists.

The Story

Tara owned the money-movement service at a fintech. The rule she could never break was the oldest one in banking: when $100 moves from account A to account B, exactly $100 leaves A and exactly $100 arrives at B — never money created, never money vanished. On a single database this was a solved problem: wrap both the debit and the credit in one transaction, and the database's write-ahead log makes it atomic — both happen or neither does. She had trusted that A in ACID (Day 9) for years without thinking about it.

Then accounts A and B landed in different databases — A in the US ledger, B in the EU ledger, after a migration. Her old single-transaction trick was gone: two databases, two separate logs, two separate disks, a network between them. Her first instinct was the obvious one: debit A, then credit B. It worked a thousand times. The thousand-and-first time, A's debit succeeded and then B's database crashed before the credit landed. $100 left A and simply… evaporated. A customer's money, gone, in a reconciliation report nobody could explain for two days.

A staff engineer walked her through Two-Phase Commit. Instead of doing the two writes directly, a coordinator would orchestrate. Phase 1: ask both databases "can you commit this? Prepare it and tell me." Each would do the write provisionally — durably logged, rows locked — and reply "YES, prepared," which is a promise: whatever you decide, I can carry it out. Phase 2: only if both said YES would the coordinator record "COMMIT" in its own durable log and tell both to finalize. If either said NO, it would tell both to roll back. Tara shipped it. The money stopped evaporating. Atomicity across two databases — it felt like magic.

The magic held for months. Then, at 3 a.m., the coordinator process crashed — in the worst possible instant: right after both databases had voted YES and locked their rows, but before it had sent the commit. Both databases sat there holding their locks, in-doubt. They could not commit (what if the coordinator had actually decided to abort?) and could not abort (what if it had decided to commit?). They could only wait. And behind those locked rows, every other transaction touching those accounts queued up and stalled. The on-call engineer was paged not because money was lost, but because the whole ledger had frozen solid around two stuck rows. Recovery meant waiting for the coordinator to come back and read its log — or a human carefully deciding by hand.

Tara read about Three-Phase Commit, which adds a middle step so a coordinator crash no longer freezes everyone — and then read the fine print: it only works if the network never partitions, an assumption real networks violate constantly (Day 25). Her real fixes were more pragmatic: make the coordinator itself fault-tolerant by backing its decision log with Raft (Day 32), so a crash just fails over instead of blocking; and, for the services that could tolerate it, drop atomic commit entirely in favor of the Saga pattern (Day 34) — a sequence of local commits with compensating undos. Her lesson: 2PC buys you true atomicity across databases, but you pay for it in blocking and locks — so the real engineering question is not "how do I do a distributed transaction?" but "can I avoid needing one at all?"

What should stay in your head
Atomic commitment is the problem of getting N independent databases to agree on a single all-or-nothing outcome for one transaction: every participant commits, or every participant aborts — never a mix. Two-Phase Commit (2PC) solves it with a coordinator and two phases. Phase 1 — Prepare/Vote: the coordinator asks every participant "can you commit?"; each does the work provisionally — writes it durably, locks the rows — and replies YES (a binding promise it can still commit if asked) or NO. A YES means the participant has given up its right to decide alone. Phase 2 — Commit/Abort: if all voted YES, the coordinator durably records COMMIT (its point of no return) and tells everyone to finalize; if any voted NO (or timed out), it records ABORT and everyone rolls back. The guarantee is atomicity. The price is the blocking problem: a participant that has voted YES and then loses contact with the coordinator is stuck in-doubt — it cannot safely commit (maybe the verdict was abort) or abort (maybe the verdict was commit), so it waits, holding its locks, stalling everything behind them. Three-Phase Commit (3PC) inserts a PreCommit step between vote and commit so that, once a participant has heard "pre-commit," it knows the verdict is commit and can safely finish on a timeout if the coordinator vanishes — non-blocking, but only when the sole failure is the coordinator. 3PC's hidden assumption is a network that never partitions and has bounded delays; a real partition (Day 25) can split participants into groups that reach different safe defaults and break atomicity — which is why 3PC is almost never used in practice. One-line test: in 2PC, the instant you vote YES you surrender the right to decide on your own — so if the coordinator disappears, you can only wait.

Why This Problem Exists

On a single database, atomicity (the A in ACID, Day 9) is almost free. The database keeps a write-ahead log on one disk: it records "I'm about to do X," does it, and records "done." If the power fails mid-way, recovery reads that one log and either replays or undoes the change. One log, one disk, one authority — atomic by construction.

Now split the work across two databases — two logs, two disks, a lossy network in between — and that free atomicity evaporates. There is no shared log that both can consult. The naive "just do A, then do B" fails in the gap:

The gap that loses money
# Transfer $100 from A (US ledger) to B (EU ledger) dbA.debit(100) ✓ succeeds — $100 leaves A # ... network blips / dbB crashes HERE ... dbB.credit(100) ✗ never runs # Result: $100 vanished. A is down 100, B is unchanged. # Reverse the order and you can CREATE money instead. There is no safe order.

The problem is that each database can independently succeed or fail, and the network can drop the message that would tell the other what happened. We need a protocol that makes two (or more) separate systems reach one shared decision — commit everywhere or abort everywhere — despite crashes and lost messages. That protocol is atomic commitment, and 2PC is its classic form.

Note the subtle difference from consensus (Days 31–32). Consensus (Paxos/Raft) gets a group to agree on a value that any of them proposed, and a majority is enough — a minority can lag and catch up later. Atomic commitment is stricter: it needs unanimity. A commit requires every single participant to be able to carry it out, because a single participant that can't commit means the whole transaction must abort. "Majority rules" is the wrong model when one dissenter can veto the outcome.

How 2PC Works

There is one coordinator (often the service that received the request, or a dedicated transaction manager) and a set of participants (the databases / resource managers). Every participant and the coordinator keeps its own durable log, because the protocol must survive crashes on either side.

Phase 1 — Prepare (the vote)

1
Coordinator → all: PREPARE. "I want to commit this transaction. Can you?" It also durably logs that this transaction is now in progress.
2
Each participant prepares. It does the work provisionally: writes the change to its own durable log (so it survives a crash), locks the affected rows, and decides whether it can commit (constraints satisfied, disk space, etc.).
3
Participant → coordinator: YES or NO. A YES is a binding promise: "I have durably prepared; whatever you decide, I can carry it out, and I will not change my mind." From this moment the participant has given up the right to decide on its own. A NO means it already rolled back and released its locks.

Phase 2 — Commit or Abort (the decision)

4
Coordinator decides. If all votes are YES, it durably records COMMIT — this write is the point of no return, the single instant the global outcome becomes "commit." If any vote is NO (or a participant timed out), it records ABORT.
5
Coordinator → all: the verdict. It sends COMMIT (or ABORT) to every participant.
6
Participants finalize + ACK. On COMMIT they make the provisional change permanent and release their locks; on ABORT they undo it and release their locks. Each acknowledges, and once all ACKs are in the coordinator can forget the transaction.

Why this is safe against crashes: everything hinges on durable logs. If a participant crashes after voting YES, on restart it sees the "prepared" record in its log, re-acquires the locks, and asks the coordinator "what was the verdict?" If the coordinator crashes, on restart it reads its own log: if it finds COMMIT, it re-sends commit; if it finds no decision yet, it can safely abort (nobody was told to commit). The protocol never forgets a decision it already made. The one scenario it can't handle gracefully is the next section.

The Blocking Problem

Here is 2PC's defining weakness — the thing that makes senior engineers nervous about it. Consider the exact instant a participant has voted YES and is holding its locks, waiting for the verdict, and the coordinator disappears (crashes, or is cut off by a network partition). Ask: what can the participant safely do on its own?

🚫 It can't just commit

Maybe another participant voted NO, and the coordinator decided ABORT and told that participant before dying. If this participant commits on a hunch, the transaction is now half-committed — atomicity is broken, money is created or lost.

🚫 It can't just abort

Maybe everyone voted YES, the coordinator decided COMMIT, and already told another participant who committed and released. If this one aborts, again the transaction is half-done — same broken atomicity, opposite direction.

⏳ So it must block

Unable to safely pick either way, the participant can only wait — holding its locks — until the coordinator comes back and tells it the recorded verdict. This is being in-doubt, and it can last seconds, minutes, or (without intervention) forever.

And blocking isn't just one stuck transaction. Those locked rows are unavailable to everyone else. Every other transaction that needs them queues behind the locks and stalls too. A single coordinator crash at the wrong moment can cascade into a frozen slice of the whole system — not because data was corrupted, but because nobody is allowed to touch it until the in-doubt transactions resolve. This is why the coordinator is a dreaded single point of failure, and why "did the coordinator survive the vote?" is the question 2PC's whole reliability hinges on.

The in-doubt window — the dangerous gap
coordinator dbA dbB | PREPARE → | | | PREPARE ───────────────→ | | ← YES 🔒 | | # dbA locked, promised | ← YES 🔒 ─────────────── | # dbB locked, promised | 💥 CRASH (before writing/sending the verdict) | | ⏳ in-doubt | ⏳ in-doubt # both frozen, holding locks | | (waiting) | (waiting) # The window between "votes collected" and "verdict delivered" # is where a coordinator crash blocks every participant.

Three-Phase Commit — The Fix That Mostly Isn't Used

3PC targets exactly the blocking problem. The insight: participants block because, at the moment the coordinator vanishes, they don't know what the verdict is going to be. 3PC fixes that by splitting the commit into two steps, inserting a PreCommit phase between the vote and the final commit, so that the verdict becomes known to everyone before anyone actually commits.

1
Phase 1 — CanCommit? / Vote. Same as 2PC's prepare: ask everyone, collect YES/NO. (In the strict model, participants don't lock as aggressively yet.)
2
Phase 2 — PreCommit. If all voted YES, the coordinator sends PRE-COMMIT. This carries one crucial piece of information: "everyone voted YES — the decision will be commit, get ready." Each participant acknowledges. Now every participant knows the outcome, even though nothing is finalized yet.
3
Phase 3 — DoCommit. The coordinator sends the final COMMIT, and participants finalize.

Why the extra phase makes it non-blocking

Now re-run the coordinator crash. If a participant has already received PRE-COMMIT and then the coordinator vanishes, it can reason: "I know all votes were YES, so the only possible verdict is commit. On a timeout, I'll commit anyway." Conversely, if a participant times out before ever seeing PRE-COMMIT, it knows the decision couldn't have been commit yet, so the safe default is to abort. Participants that can reach each other can even agree on this default among themselves. Either way, a lone coordinator failure no longer freezes anyone — that's the win.

The hidden assumption that keeps it in textbooks

3PC's safety rests on an assumption real networks violate all the time: no network partitions, and bounded message delays (a synchronous network where a timeout reliably means "dead," not "slow"). But we proved in Day 25 that you cannot tell slow from dead. Picture a partition that splits the participants into two groups right at the PreCommit step: one group received PRE-COMMIT and, on timeout, commits; the other group never saw it and, on timeout, aborts. The result is a split outcome — atomicity shattered, exactly the thing the whole protocol exists to prevent. So 3PC trades 2PC's blocking for a potential safety violation under partitions, adds a full extra round-trip of latency, and is more complex. Most engineers consider that a bad trade. In production, 3PC is rare; the real-world answer to 2PC's blocking is to make the coordinator itself fault-tolerant (below).

The Hard Parts

2PC gives a real, valuable guarantee — true atomic commit across independent systems — but the costs are exactly why distributed systems try hard to avoid needing it.

Blocking & the coordinator as a single point of failure

As we saw: a coordinator crash in the in-doubt window freezes participants and cascades through the locks they hold. The standard production fix isn't 3PC — it's to stop the coordinator from being a single point of failure by making its decision log fault-tolerant with consensus (Raft/Paxos, Day 32). If the coordinator's "COMMIT/ABORT" record lives on a replicated, highly-available log, a crashed coordinator just fails over to a replica that reads the same log and finishes the job — the blocking window collapses from "until a human intervenes" to "until failover completes."

Locks held across the network murder throughput

In single-database transactions, locks are held for microseconds. In 2PC, a participant holds its locks from the moment it votes YES until it receives the verdict — across one or more network round-trips, often milliseconds to seconds. The whole time, those rows are unavailable to everyone else. Under load, these long-held locks become the bottleneck: contention, timeouts, and sharply reduced concurrency. 2PC does not scale the way engineers often hope.

It's a CP protocol — it sacrifices availability

2PC chooses consistency over availability (CAP, Day 10): faced with a failure in the middle of a commit, it would rather pause (block) than risk a wrong, non-atomic answer. That is often the correct choice for money — but it means a distributed transaction is only as available as the least available participant, and any one of them being slow or down stalls the whole thing. More participants = more ways to fail = lower availability.

It doesn't compose well with microservices

2PC needs every participant to speak the same transaction protocol (e.g. XA) and to expose "prepare/commit/abort" hooks. Independent microservices with their own databases, third-party APIs, and message queues generally can't all do this — you can't ask Stripe or an email provider to join your two-phase commit. This is the practical reason the microservices world reaches for the Saga pattern (Day 34) instead: a chain of ordinary local commits, each with a compensating action that undoes it, giving you a weaker (eventually-consistent) but available and loosely-coupled alternative to true atomic commit.

The through-line: 2PC is the price-is-no-object way to get atomicity across databases — correct, but blocking, lock-heavy, and availability-sacrificing. Use it when you genuinely need cross-database atomicity and you can make the coordinator fault-tolerant (Spanner does); avoid it — via Sagas or by redesigning so the transaction lives in one place — whenever you can.

Today extends the atomicity of ACID (Day 9) from one database to many — the same "all-or-nothing" promise, now across a network with no shared log. It is deeply tied to consensus (Day 32) but is not the same problem: consensus needs a majority and tolerates laggards, while atomic commit needs unanimity (one veto aborts everything) — yet the modern cure for 2PC's blocking is precisely to put the coordinator's decision on a Raft-replicated log so it can't be a single point of failure. The blocking problem is a direct instance of the ambiguous-timeout / can't-tell-slow-from-dead lesson from Distributed Failures (Day 25) — an in-doubt participant literally cannot tell whether the silent coordinator committed or aborted. And 2PC's refusal to guess (pausing rather than risking a split outcome) is CAP (Day 10) made concrete: it is a CP protocol. Tomorrow, Sagas (Day 34) take the opposite stance — trade atomicity for availability with local commits and compensating undos.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"2PC is a consensus algorithm like Raft."Different problem. Consensus needs a majority and tolerates laggards; atomic commit needs unanimity — a single NO vetoes the commit. 2PC also blocks on a coordinator failure; Raft keeps going as long as a majority is alive.
"Once a participant votes YES, it can change its mind."No. A YES is a binding promise: the participant has durably prepared and must be able to commit if told to. It has given up the right to decide on its own — that surrender is the whole source of the blocking problem.
"3PC fixed 2PC, so everyone uses it."Almost no one does. 3PC is non-blocking only if the network never partitions and delays are bounded — false in reality. Under a partition it can produce a split, non-atomic outcome. Production fixes 2PC's blocking with a consensus-backed coordinator instead.
"The coordinator crashing just means we retry."If it crashes in the in-doubt window, participants are frozen holding locks until it (or a failover) returns and reads its decision log. Meanwhile everything behind those locks stalls — a crash can freeze a whole slice of the system.
"2PC scales fine — just add participants."The opposite. Locks are held across network round-trips, and more participants = more locks held longer, more failure modes, and availability bounded by the weakest participant. 2PC is a throughput and availability cost, not a free one.
"Microservices should use 2PC for data consistency."Usually a trap. Independent services (and third-party APIs) can't all speak one transaction protocol, and the coupling/blocking is toxic to availability. The idiomatic answer is the Saga pattern (Day 34): local commits + compensating undos.

Active Recall

❓ A bank runs a 2PC transfer across DB-A and DB-B. Both receive PREPARE, both durably prepare, lock their rows, and reply YES. The coordinator receives both YES votes — and then crashes before writing its decision or sending anything. Walk through what each database does, whether the money moved, and how the system eventually recovers. (click to reveal)
Both databases are stuck in-doubt and must block. Each voted YES, so each has a durable "prepared" record and is holding its row locks, waiting for the verdict. Neither can act alone: committing risks a half-commit if the verdict was actually abort; aborting risks a half-commit if the verdict was commit. So both wait, locks held — and any other transaction touching those accounts queues behind the locks and stalls. Did the money move? No — nothing was finalized; the transfer is neither committed nor rolled back, it's frozen. Recovery: when the coordinator restarts, it reads its own durable log. Because it crashed before recording a decision, there is no COMMIT on record, so it may safely abort the transaction (nobody could have committed — the commit record was never written), tell both databases to roll back and release their locks, and the system unfreezes. The production improvement: back the coordinator's decision log with Raft (Day 32), so instead of waiting for the same process to restart, a replica fails over, reads the replicated log, and resolves the in-doubt transaction in seconds. (And note this is the ambiguous timeout of Day 25 in its purest form: the databases cannot tell whether the silent coordinator committed, aborted, or simply hasn't spoken yet.)

Real-World Engineering

🗃️ XA & Classic Databases

The XA standard (and Java's JTA) is 2PC made concrete: a transaction manager coordinates "resource managers" (PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle, IBM MQ) through prepare/commit/rollback. PostgreSQL exposes it as PREPARE TRANSACTION / COMMIT PREPARED. It works, and it's where most engineers first meet in-doubt ("prepared") transactions — and the DBA pain of resolving them by hand after a coordinator dies.

🌲 Google Spanner

Spanner runs 2PC over Paxos: each participant isn't a single database but a Paxos/Raft group (a replicated shard), and the coordinator's state is itself replicated. This is the textbook cure for 2PC's single-point-of-failure — a crashed node just fails over, so the blocking window nearly vanishes. Combined with TrueTime, Spanner offers externally-consistent distributed transactions at global scale. The lesson: 2PC's weakness isn't the two phases, it's the fragile coordinator — make it fault-tolerant and 2PC becomes practical.

📨 Kafka Transactions

Kafka's "exactly-once" producer uses a 2PC-flavored protocol with a transaction coordinator and a durable transaction log: messages are written, then a commit/abort marker is two-phase-committed across the partitions involved, so consumers only ever see fully-committed batches. A distributed-commit protocol hiding inside a message broker.

🧩 Microservices → Sagas

Most microservice architectures deliberately avoid 2PC. You can't enlist Stripe, an email provider, and three internal services into one XA transaction, and the blocking would wreck availability. Instead they use Sagas (Day 34): each service commits locally and publishes an event; a failure later triggers compensating transactions (refund the charge, cancel the reservation) to unwind. Weaker guarantee, far better availability and coupling.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What problem does Two-Phase Commit solve, and how is it different from consensus?
A: Atomic commitment — making N separate databases all commit or all abort one transaction. Unlike consensus (which needs a majority and tolerates laggards), atomic commit needs unanimity: a single NO vote aborts everything.
Q: What are the two phases of 2PC?
A: Phase 1 — Prepare/Vote: the coordinator asks each participant to prepare; each durably logs the change, locks rows, and votes YES (a binding promise) or NO. Phase 2 — Commit/Abort: if all voted YES the coordinator durably records COMMIT and tells everyone to finalize; otherwise it records ABORT and everyone rolls back.
Q: What is the "blocking problem," and when exactly does it strike?
A: If the coordinator crashes after a participant voted YES but before delivering the verdict, the participant is in-doubt: it can't safely commit (maybe the verdict was abort) or abort (maybe it was commit), so it blocks, holding locks and stalling everything behind them until the coordinator recovers.
Q: How does 3PC avoid blocking, and why is it rarely used?
A: It adds a PreCommit phase so every participant knows the verdict is commit before anyone commits — on a coordinator crash they can safely finish on a timeout. But it assumes no network partitions and bounded delays; under a real partition it can produce a split, non-atomic outcome, so production prefers a consensus-backed coordinator instead.
Q: What's the standard production fix for 2PC's single-point-of-failure coordinator?
A: Make the coordinator's decision log fault-tolerant with consensus (Raft/Paxos). Then a crashed coordinator just fails over to a replica that reads the same replicated log and finishes the transaction — Google Spanner runs 2PC over Paxos for exactly this reason.

If you only remember one thing: 2PC makes several databases commit atomically by voting first, then deciding — Phase 1 everyone prepares, locks, and promises YES or NO; Phase 2 the coordinator records one verdict (COMMIT only if all YES) and everyone finalizes. The guarantee is real atomicity across systems; the price is the blocking problem — a coordinator that dies in the gap after the votes leaves participants in-doubt, holding locks, stalling everything behind them. 3PC adds a PreCommit step to be non-blocking on a lone coordinator failure, but its "no partitions" assumption makes it unsafe in practice, so production instead makes the coordinator fault-tolerant with consensus (Spanner's 2PC-over-Paxos). 2PC is a CP, lock-heavy, availability-sacrificing tool: use it when you truly need cross-database atomicity and can harden the coordinator — and avoid it, via the Saga pattern or a single-place redesign, whenever you can.
