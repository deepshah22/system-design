Consensus: The Paxos Algorithm
How Unreliable Machines Agree on One Thing

Welcome to Phase 2. For 30 days we collected the parts of a system — load balancers, caches, databases, queues. Now the parts must act as one. But the machines are unreliable: they crash mid-sentence, freeze for seconds, and lose each other's messages. So here is the question that haunts every distributed system: how can a group of flaky machines agree on a single value — a leader, a lock-holder, the next entry in a log — and be guaranteed never to disagree? That is the consensus problem, and Paxos is the algorithm that cracked it. Today we build it from one beautiful idea — majority quorums — up through its two phases and three roles, and watch, in a simulator you can step through, how it keeps a cluster correct even while nodes die and proposers duel. This is the bedrock under Chubby, Spanner, ZooKeeper, and etcd — and the direct setup for Raft tomorrow.

Picture this

Five friends share a house and want to agree on one thing — say, the name of the new cat. But they're never all awake at once: people nap, step out, and the only way to communicate is by leaving sticky notes on the fridge, which sometimes fall off and get lost. How do they ever reach a decision that can't later turn out to be two different names?

They adopt one rule, and it's the whole secret: nothing counts as decided until a majority — at least three of the five — has signed off on it. Here's the magic hidden in that rule. Suppose three friends (say Ana, Ben, Cass) agree on "Mochi." Later, a different three try to decide — but any group of three out of five must share at least one person with the first three. That overlapping person was there for "Mochi," and when the new group asks "has anything been decided?", that one person says "yes — Mochi," and the new group is forced to go with Mochi too. Two different names can never both win, because every majority bumps into every other majority.

Burn in the image: two circles of three friends, overlapping in the middle by one person — and that one person in the overlap is the memory that makes a decision permanent. That is Paxos. Everything else is just careful bookkeeping around this one picture.

The Story

Split-Brain Sanjay ran a small cluster of three servers that shared one job: exactly one of them, the leader, was allowed to write to the billing database at a time. The other two stood by. To pick the leader, Sanjay did the obvious thing: each node pinged the others, and whoever had been up longest declared itself leader. Simple. It worked in every test.

Then one night a network switch hiccuped for eight seconds. For those eight seconds, node A couldn't hear node B or node C — and they couldn't hear it. Each side looked around, saw the leader had "gone silent," and did the reasonable thing: it elected a new leader. Now there were two leaders, each certain it was the only one, each happily writing to billing. A customer's refund was processed twice. A subscription was both cancelled and renewed. When the network healed, the two halves of the cluster had two different, conflicting stories about reality — and no way to say which was true. This is split-brain, and it is the nightmare that lives under every distributed system.

A principal engineer walked Sanjay through what went wrong. "Your election," she said, "let a minority crown a leader. Each lonely side had just one or two nodes, and it elected itself anyway. The fix is a single rule: no one becomes leader without the votes of a strict majority." Sanjay frowned — "but during the network split, neither side might have a majority." "Exactly," she said. "And that's the point. If you can't get a majority, you don't act — you wait. A majority can only exist on one side of any split, because two majorities of three nodes always share a node, and that node won't vote twice. So at most one leader can ever be elected. The price is that the minority side goes quiet until the network heals — but a system that pauses is infinitely better than a system that corrupts your billing."

That conversation is the seed of Paxos. Leslie Lamport designed it in the 1990s to answer precisely Sanjay's problem — how a set of unreliable processes agree on a single value, with a cast-iron guarantee that they never agree on two — and he built it on exactly that majority rule. Sanjay rebuilt his election on top of it: a node needs a majority's promises before it can lead, and any later would-be leader is forced to honor what the majority already decided. The split-brain never happened again. The cluster sometimes paused during a bad network — but it never again lied.

Sanjay's lesson is the whole lesson: a majority is a referee that can't be in two places at once. Build agreement on majorities, and you trade a little availability for a guarantee you can bet a business on — the cluster will never disagree with itself.

What should stay in your head
Consensus is getting a group of unreliable machines to agree on a single value — and never, under any timing, crash, or lost message, agree on two. Paxos solves it with one load-bearing idea: majority quorums. Because any two majorities of N nodes always overlap in at least one node, that shared node "remembers" the first value chosen and forces every later attempt to adopt it — so only one value is ever chosen (that's safety). The protocol runs in two phases, each needing a majority: Phase 1 — Prepare/Promise (a proposer picks an ever-increasing ballot number, asks acceptors to promise to ignore anything older, and learns any value already accepted); Phase 2 — Accept/Accepted (the proposer asks the majority to accept a value — but it must be the highest-numbered value it heard about in Phase 1, using its own only if none was reported). A value is chosen the instant a majority has accepted it, and from that moment it can never change. Three roles play out: Proposers suggest, Acceptors vote and remember, Learners find out the result (one machine usually plays all three). With 2f+1 nodes you tolerate f failures and still have a majority. The catch is liveness: two proposers can keep out-bidding each other forever (a "duel"), so real systems elect one stable leader — which is exactly Multi-Paxos, and the reason Raft exists (tomorrow). One-line test: could any sequence of crashes and lost messages make two nodes believe two different values were chosen? With majorities, no.

Why Consensus Is Hard

At first glance, agreement sounds trivial — just take a vote, right? The difficulty is that in a distributed system you cannot tell the difference between a machine that has crashed and one that is merely slow or unreachable. A node that hasn't answered in two seconds might be dead forever, or might reply in the next millisecond. Messages arrive late, out of order, or not at all. There is no shared clock. And critically, you can't just wait for everyone: if agreement required all nodes to respond, a single crashed node would freeze the whole system forever.

So we need a scheme that (a) makes progress even when some nodes are down or silent, yet (b) never lets two healthy parts of the system commit to different answers. These two goals pull in opposite directions, and a famous result — the FLP impossibility (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson, 1985) — proved that in a fully asynchronous network with even one possible crash, no algorithm can guarantee it will always reach agreement in bounded time. Paxos lives gracefully inside that limit: it never sacrifices safety (it will not disagree), and it reaches agreement as soon as the network behaves well enough for a majority to talk. It gives up only the promise of a strict deadline — which, as we'll see, is a trade every real system happily makes.

One important boundary: classic Paxos assumes nodes may crash or lose messages, but not lie — they don't send maliciously forged or contradictory data. Tolerating lying nodes is the harder "Byzantine" problem (think blockchains), which needs different, costlier algorithms. For the vast majority of internal infrastructure — your own datacenter's databases and coordinators — crash-fault tolerance is exactly what you want, and it's what Paxos delivers.

The One Trick: Majority Quorums

If you remember one thing from today, remember this. A quorum is simply a group big enough to act on the cluster's behalf. Paxos uses the simplest quorum there is — a strict majority, more than half the nodes. And majorities have a magical property:

Any two majorities of the same set always overlap in at least one member.

Why? If you split N nodes into two groups that don't share anyone, each group has at most half, so neither is a majority. The only way to get two majorities is for them to overlap. That overlapping node is the system's memory: whatever the first majority decided, it was present for — so when a second majority forms, it unavoidably includes someone who knows, and that someone forces the new majority to respect the old decision. Two conflicting decisions are mathematically impossible, no matter how the network splits or which nodes crash.

This also gives you the famous fault-tolerance formula. To tolerate f simultaneous failures and still have a majority alive, you need N = 2f + 1 nodes:

Total nodes (N)Majority neededFailures tolerated (f)Typical use
321Small clusters (etcd, ZooKeeper dev)
532The production sweet spot
743Extra resilience, more latency

Notice why clusters are almost always an odd number. Four nodes need a majority of three and tolerate only one failure — exactly the same resilience as three nodes, but with more machines to coordinate. The extra node buys you nothing and costs you latency. Odd sizes are the efficient choice, and you rarely see more than seven, because every write must wait for a majority to reply — more nodes means more agreement, which means slower.

The Three Roles

Paxos describes three roles. In real deployments one physical server usually plays all three at once, but keeping them separate in your head makes the protocol click.

📢 Proposer

Suggests a value, drives the round.

Receives a request ("make me the leader", "append this log entry") and tries to get a value chosen. It runs the two phases, picks ballot numbers, and must adopt any already-accepted value it learns about. In Multi-Paxos this is the stable leader.

🗳️ Acceptor

Votes and remembers. The quorum.

The heart of safety. Acceptors promise to ignore old ballots, accept values, and — crucially — durably remember the highest ballot they've promised and the last value they accepted. A majority of acceptors agreeing is what makes a value "chosen."

👂 Learner

Finds out the result and acts on it.

Once a value is chosen, learners are informed so the rest of the system can proceed — apply the log entry, recognize the new leader, serve the data. Learners don't vote; they just need to reliably discover the one chosen value.

The separation matters because safety lives entirely in the acceptors' memory. Proposers can be buggy, restart, or duel each other, and learners can lag — but as long as acceptors honor their promises and remember what they accepted, the system can never choose two values. The acceptors are the referee; everyone else is a player.

How Paxos Works — Two Phases

A single "decide one value" run is called Basic Paxos (or a single-decree instance). It is two round-trips, each gated on a majority. The key to the whole thing is the ballot number: a globally unique, ever-increasing number that every proposal carries, so acceptors can always tell newer proposals from older ones (in practice, (counter, node-id) so no two proposers ever pick the same number).

1a
Prepare(n). The proposer picks a ballot number n higher than any it has used, and sends Prepare(n) to the acceptors.
1b
Promise(n). An acceptor that hasn't already promised a higher number replies with a promise: "I will ignore any ballot below n from now on." Critically, it also reports back the highest-numbered value it has already accepted, if any. If it has already promised something higher, it refuses.
2a
Accept(n, v). If the proposer collects promises from a majority, it chooses the value v: if any promise reported an already-accepted value, it must use the one with the highest ballot number (it adopts the in-progress decision); only if nobody reported a value is it free to use its own. It then sends Accept(n, v).
2b
Accepted(n, v). An acceptor accepts (n, v) as long as it hasn't promised a ballot higher than n in the meantime, records it durably, and acknowledges. The moment a majority has accepted, v is chosen — permanently.
3
Learn. The chosen value is propagated to the learners, and the system acts on it.

Step 2a is the soul of Paxos — the part people miss. A proposer does not get to force its own value if the cluster is already mid-decision on another. By being required to adopt the highest already-accepted value it heard, every new proposer either (a) discovers a value is already chosen and re-proposes the same one, or (b) finds none and is free to pick. Either way, once a majority has accepted some v, every future round re-proposes that same v. That's the mechanism behind the magic in the anchor: the overlap guarantees Phase 1 always sees the prior decision, and the adopt-the-highest rule makes it honor it.

A Full Round, Step by Step

Let's trace the hardest, most instructive case: two proposers duel, and we watch safety hold. Five acceptors A1–A5; majority is 3. Proposer X wants "pizza"; Proposer Y wants "tacos".

The trace — read it top to bottom
# X goes first with ballot 2 X → Prepare(2) to A1 A2 A3 A1 A2 A3 → Promise(2, nothing accepted yet) # majority of 3 promised # X got a majority, nobody reported a value → X may use its own X → Accept(2, "pizza") to A1 A2 A3 A1 A2 A3 → Accepted(2, "pizza") # CHOSEN: a majority accepted "pizza" # Now Y shows up with a HIGHER ballot, wanting "tacos" Y → Prepare(4) to A3 A4 A5 A3 → Promise(4, accepted ("pizza" @ 2)) # A3 was in X's majority — it remembers! A4 A5 → Promise(4, nothing) # Y has a majority of promises — BUT one reported ("pizza" @ 2). # Rule 2a: Y MUST adopt the highest accepted value it heard = "pizza". Y → Accept(4, "pizza") # NOT "tacos" — Y is forced to honor the decision A3 A4 A5 → Accepted(4, "pizza") # Result: the value is "pizza". Y's higher ballot changed the # ballot NUMBER, never the VALUE. Two values were never both chosen.

Trace the safety yourself: the only way Y could have pushed "tacos" is if its Phase 1 majority had contained no one who accepted "pizza". But "pizza" was accepted by a majority (A1 A2 A3), and Y's majority (A3 A4 A5) is also a majority — so they must share a node (A3), and that node rats out the decision. The overlap is not luck; it's arithmetic. This is why Paxos is correct by construction, not by hoping the timing works out.

You may also have spotted the dark side: if X now retries with ballot 5, it preempts Y; Y retries with 6 and preempts X; and they can ping-pong forever without either finishing. Nobody ever chooses a wrong value — safety is airtight — but the round may never end. That's the liveness problem, and it's next.

The Hard Parts

Paxos is famous for being correct and for being painful. Here's where the rough edges are.

Liveness vs. safety — the duel

As the trace showed, two proposers can livelock, each preempting the other with an ever-higher ballot. Paxos guarantees it will never choose two values (safety), but it does not guarantee it will choose any value in bounded time (liveness) — exactly the gap FLP predicted. The standard fix is to stop having multiple proposers: elect a single distinguished proposer (a leader) and funnel all proposals through it. One proposer can't duel itself, so rounds finish. This is the jump from Basic Paxos to Multi-Paxos.

One value is useless — you need a log (Multi-Paxos)

Basic Paxos agrees on one value. Real systems need to agree on an endless sequence of values — a replicated log of commands ("set x=1", then "set y=2", …). Multi-Paxos runs one Paxos instance per log slot, but with a clever optimization: once a leader is stable, it can skip Phase 1 for future slots (it already holds the promises) and go straight to Phase 2. That turns the common case into a single round-trip per command — fast enough to be practical. The replicated log you get is the foundation of every consistent distributed database.

It's notoriously hard to implement

Lamport's original paper ("The Part-Time Parliament") was so dense it became a running joke; even his follow-up "Paxos Made Simple" leaves most engineers struggling to build a correct, complete system (leader election, membership changes, log compaction, and recovery are all underspecified). The gap between "I understand single-decree Paxos" and "I shipped a correct Multi-Paxos" is enormous. This pain is precisely why Raft was created in 2014 — same guarantees, designed from the ground up to be understandable — and it's why most new systems reach for Raft. We cover it tomorrow.

The cost of a majority

Every decision waits for a round-trip to a majority of nodes. That's deliberate — it's what buys safety — but it means consensus is not free: writes are slower than hitting one machine, and spreading nodes across distant regions adds the cross-ocean latency from Day 30 to every agreement. This is why you keep consensus clusters small (3 or 5) and close together, and push only the decisions that truly need agreement through them — leadership, configuration, locks — while bulk data takes cheaper paths.

The through-line: Paxos trades availability and speed for an unbreakable safety guarantee. It will pause rather than disagree, and it will cost you a majority round-trip per decision. In exchange you get something priceless — a cluster that can never contradict itself, no matter how the network misbehaves.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"Consensus means every node must agree / be online."It means a majority agrees. Requiring all nodes would make one crash freeze the system forever. A majority is enough — and is exactly what tolerates failures.
"A higher ballot number lets a proposer override the chosen value."A higher ballot wins the right to propose, but the proposer is forced to adopt the highest already-accepted value. The ballot number changes; the chosen value never does.
"Paxos guarantees the cluster always makes a decision."It guarantees safety (never two values), not liveness. Dueling proposers can stall forever. A single leader (Multi-Paxos) restores progress.
"Paxos handles malicious / lying nodes."No — it tolerates crashes and lost messages, not lies. Defeating lying nodes is the harder Byzantine problem (blockchains), with different, costlier algorithms.
"Use an even number of nodes for more safety."Use odd. Four nodes tolerate the same one failure as three but cost more coordination. N = 2f+1 — odd sizes are strictly more efficient.
"Paxos and Raft are fundamentally different."They solve the same problem with the same guarantees. Raft is a re-packaging designed to be understandable (strong leader, clear terms). If you get Paxos, Raft is easy.

Active Recall

❓ A 5-node Paxos cluster has just had a value chosen: acceptors A1, A2, A3 accepted "leader = node-7" at ballot 3. A network partition now isolates A1 and A2 on one side, and A3, A4, A5 on the other. A new proposer on the A3-A4-A5 side starts a round with ballot 6, trying to set "leader = node-9". Walk through what happens — and can it succeed in changing the leader? (click to reveal)
It cannot change the value. The proposer sends Prepare(6) to its side and gets promises from a majority (A3, A4, A5 — that's 3 of 5). But A3 was part of the majority that accepted "leader = node-7" at ballot 3, so its promise reports accepted ("node-7" @ 3). By Rule 2a, the proposer is forced to adopt the highest already-accepted value it heard — which is "node-7". So it sends Accept(6, "node-7"), not "node-9". The ballot advances to 6, but the leader stays node-7. Why it's safe: any majority on this side must include at least one node from the original accepting majority (two majorities of 5 always overlap), and that node carries the decision forward. The A1-A2 side, meanwhile, has only 2 nodes — no majority — so it cannot make any decision at all; it waits. Net result: one chosen value, preserved across the partition, with the minority side safely paused. That is exactly the split-brain Sanjay's naive election failed to prevent.

Real-World Engineering

🔒 Google Chubby & Spanner

Chubby, Google's distributed lock service, is a Paxos implementation at its core — and it's what countless Google systems use for leader election and configuration. Spanner, Google's globally-distributed database, runs Paxos groups (with atomic clocks for ordering) to keep replicas consistent across continents. Paxos is quite literally load-bearing for Google.

🐘 ZooKeeper (ZAB)

Apache ZooKeeper — the coordination service behind Kafka (Day 19), Hadoop, and more — uses ZAB (ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast), a Paxos-family protocol. When a service needs "exactly one leader" or a reliable config store, ZooKeeper's majority-quorum consensus is doing the work underneath.

⚙️ etcd & Consul (Raft)

Kubernetes stores all its cluster state in etcd, which uses Raft — Paxos's more approachable cousin. Every time Kubernetes agrees on what pods should run where, a consensus round decided it. HashiCorp Consul uses Raft too. This is the most common place a working engineer meets consensus today.

💡 Cassandra Lightweight Transactions

Even "AP" databases reach for Paxos when they must. Cassandra (Day 52) is normally eventually-consistent, but its lightweight transactions ("insert if not exists") run a Paxos round to guarantee a single winner under contention — a surgical use of consensus only where correctness demands it.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: In one sentence, what problem does Paxos solve?
A: It lets a group of unreliable machines agree on a single value — and guarantees they never agree on two — despite crashes, delays, and lost messages.
Q: What is the one property of majority quorums that makes Paxos safe?
A: Any two majorities always overlap in at least one node. That shared node remembers the first decision and forces every later round to adopt it, so two different values can never both be chosen.
Q: Name Paxos's two phases and what each achieves.
A: Phase 1 — Prepare/Promise: a proposer claims a ballot number and learns any already-accepted value. Phase 2 — Accept/Accepted: a majority accepts a value (which the proposer must set to the highest value it heard in Phase 1); a majority accepting = chosen.
Q: How many nodes to tolerate f failures, and why odd numbers?
A: N = 2f + 1. So 3 tolerate 1, 5 tolerate 2. Odd is efficient: 4 nodes tolerate the same one failure as 3 but add coordination cost for no benefit.
Q: What does Paxos not guarantee, and how is it fixed in practice?
A: It doesn't guarantee liveness — dueling proposers can stall forever. Fix: elect a single leader (Multi-Paxos), so there's only one proposer and rounds finish. Raft builds this in from the start.

If you only remember one thing: consensus is built on majorities, because any two majorities always overlap — and that overlapping node is the memory that makes a decision permanent. Paxos runs two majority-gated phases (Prepare/Promise, then Accept/Accepted), with one soul-rule: a new proposer must adopt the highest value already accepted, so once a majority accepts some value, every future round re-proposes that same value and two values can never both be chosen. Three roles — Proposers suggest, Acceptors vote and remember, Learners find out — and 2f+1 nodes tolerate f failures (keep clusters small and odd). Paxos buys this cast-iron safety by giving up a little liveness (dueling proposers can stall) and speed (a round-trip to a majority per decision) — it will pause rather than contradict itself.
