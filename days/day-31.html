Consensus: The Paxos Algorithm
Making Unreliable Machines Agree

For 30 days we built the parts of a system. Welcome to Phase 2, where many machines start acting as one. The first question is the hardest: how does a group of servers — some crashed, some unreachable, with messages lost, delayed, and reordered — agree on a single value and never un-agree? That's distributed consensus, and Paxos is the algorithm that solves it correctly. You'll learn the two-phase dance at its heart — Prepare / Promise, then Accept / Accepted — and the one idea that makes it all work: a decision needs a majority, and any two majorities must overlap, so two different values can never both win. This is the bedrock under leader election, replicated databases, and Kubernetes itself.

Picture this

Five friends are trying to pick one restaurant for tonight — but they can only talk through a terrible group chat. Messages arrive hours late, or out of order, or vanish entirely. Two people are asleep and won't answer. Someone's phone dies mid-conversation. And yet, by the end of the night, the group absolutely must converge on exactly one restaurant — and once it's locked in, nobody is allowed to quietly change it, or half the group shows up at the wrong place.

Here's the clever trick they invent. Nobody is allowed to just announce a choice. First you send a numbered note — "Note #7: can I propose something?" — and each friend who sees it writes back "promise: I'll ignore any note numbered below 7", and tells you if they already promised to something earlier. Only once a majority (three of the five) have promised do you send your actual pick — "Note #7: Luigi's" — and only when a majority write back "accepted" is Luigi's truly chosen. The magic is the majority: because any three-person group and any other three-person group must share at least one person, a choice that got three "accepts" can never be forgotten — the shared friend always remembers it and forces the next proposer to keep it. Burn in the image: a round table, numbered notes, and three raised hands. You never need everyone. You need a majority — and majorities always overlap.

The Story

Split-Brain Sofia ran the database behind a fast-growing payments app. She'd done Day 22 well: one primary server took the writes, a standby replica stayed in sync, and a heartbeat let the standby take over if the primary died. "Automatic failover," she told everyone. "If the primary dies, the standby is promoted. Zero downtime."

Then the network hiccuped — not a crash, just thirty seconds where the primary and standby couldn't hear each other. The standby's heartbeat went silent, so the standby did exactly what Sofia built it to do: it declared the primary dead and promoted itself. But the primary hadn't died at all — it was happily still taking writes. For thirty seconds there were two primaries, each accepting payments, each certain it was the only one. Account balances forked. When the network healed, the two histories could not be merged: some customers had been charged twice, others not at all. This is split-brain, and no amount of clever reconciliation code fixes it after the fact — the damage is done the moment two nodes both believe they're in charge.

Sofia's first instinct was to add a lock — "whoever grabs the lock is the real primary." But where does the lock live? On one server? Then that server is a new single point of failure (Day 22), and if it gets partitioned, she's back to two primaries. She'd just moved the problem. A senior engineer drew a circle of five servers on the whiteboard and asked one question: "What if a node can only become primary if a majority of the five agree to it? During your thirty-second blip, the network split the five into a group of three and a group of two. How many primaries can you elect?" Sofia saw it instantly: the side with three can agree; the side with two can never get a majority, so it can't elect anyone. At most one primary, always — because two different majorities can't both exist.

"That's consensus," the engineer said. "And the classic algorithm that does it safely — even when machines crash mid-decision and messages get lost — is called Paxos. It won't make your decision faster, and honestly it's a pain to implement. But it will guarantee you never, ever have two primaries or two conflicting decisions. Safety first; speed you buy later with a leader."

Sofia's lesson is Phase 2's opening lesson: you cannot build a correct distributed system on top of "trust one node." The moment the network can lie to you by going quiet, agreement has to come from a majority vote — and getting that vote right, despite crashes and lost messages, is exactly what Paxos is for.

What should stay in your head
Consensus is getting a group of unreliable machines to agree on a single value with three guarantees: only a value someone actually proposed can be chosen (validity), at most one value is ever chosen (agreement — the safety property), and a value is eventually chosen and learned by all (termination). Paxos achieves this despite crashes and lost/delayed/reordered messages (but not lying — that's the harder Byzantine case). It works in two phases driven by ever-increasing proposal numbers: Phase 1 (Prepare → Promise) reserves a ballot number and surfaces any value already accepted; Phase 2 (Accept → Accepted) commits a value once a majority accepts it. The keystone is the majority quorum: any two majorities of N nodes share at least one node, so a value chosen by one majority is always visible to the next proposer — which is then forced to re-propose it. That single fact is why two different values can never both be chosen. Safety is unconditional; liveness is not — by the FLP result, no async algorithm can guarantee progress under failures, and two "dueling" proposers can livelock, so real systems elect a single stable leader (Multi-Paxos) and skip Phase 1. One-line test: if the network splits in two, can both halves independently decide? With majority quorums — no, because only one side can hold a majority.

What "Consensus" Actually Means

Strip away the jargon and consensus is one sentence: a set of processes must agree on a single value. It sounds trivial — it is anything but, once machines can fail. To be useful, a consensus algorithm must satisfy three properties, and it's worth knowing them by name because interviewers love them and because they explain every design choice in Paxos:

✅ Validity

Only a proposed value can be chosen.

The algorithm can't invent a value out of thin air. If every proposer proposes "A", the answer is "A" — not "B". This keeps the result meaningful.

🤝 Agreement

At most one value is ever chosen.

This is the safety property — the one Paxos never, ever violates. No two nodes may decide different values, and a decision, once made, is permanent. Break this and you get split-brain.

⏭️ Termination

A value is eventually chosen & learned.

The liveness property — the group actually makes progress. This is the one Paxos can't always guarantee (see FLP), which is why we add leaders.

And the fault model matters enormously. Paxos assumes the non-Byzantine (or "crash-fault") model: nodes may crash and recover, and messages may be lost, delayed, duplicated, or reordered — but no node lies, forges messages, or acts maliciously. That "no lying" assumption is a big deal: handling liars needs Byzantine-fault-tolerant protocols (a later lesson) which are far more expensive. For servers inside your own data center, crash-fault is the right model, and Paxos is built precisely for it.

Why It's So Much Harder Than It Looks

"Just take a vote" is the instinct. Here is why every naïve scheme breaks:

TRAP 1

Trust one leader? It crashes.

Let one node decide and broadcast the answer. Fine — until it crashes after telling two nodes and before telling the rest. Now some nodes know the decision and some don't, with no safe way to resolve the disagreement.

TRAP 2

Wait for everyone? Someone's always down.

Require a unanimous vote and your system halts the instant a single node is slow or dead — which, at any real scale, is always. You can't demand everyone; you must decide with the nodes you can reach.

TRAP 3

Two proposers race.

Nothing stops two nodes from proposing different values at the same moment. Without a rule to serialize them, half the cluster accepts "A" and half accepts "B" — a tie with no tiebreaker, and two conflicting decisions.

TRAP 4

The network splits.

A partition cuts the cluster in two. Each half sees the other as "dead." If each half is allowed to decide on its own, you get two answers — the exact split-brain Sofia hit. Something must stop both halves from deciding.

Paxos answers all four with the same two tools: proposal numbers (which serialize racing proposers and let a later attempt supersede a stalled earlier one) and the majority quorum (which guarantees at most one side can ever decide, and that a decision survives any crash of a minority of nodes). Hold those two ideas; everything else is mechanics.

One sobering result frames the whole field: the FLP impossibility theorem (Fischer, Lynch & Paterson, 1985) proves that in a fully asynchronous network where even one node may crash, no algorithm can guarantee both safety and termination in all cases. Paxos's response is principled: it keeps safety absolutely, always, and gives up the guarantee of termination — in practice it almost always terminates, and a leader makes it terminate quickly. Remember that trade; it's the soul of the algorithm.

The Three Roles

Paxos describes three roles, not three kinds of machine — in real systems every server usually plays all three at once. Separating them conceptually is what makes the protocol understandable.

🧑‍💼 Proposer

Wants to get a value chosen. It drives the two phases: picks a proposal number, asks for promises, then asks acceptors to accept a value. A client write usually turns into a proposer's attempt.

the one making the motion

🗳️ Acceptor

The "voters" and the memory of the system. Acceptors promise, accept, and — crucially — remember what they've promised and accepted (on durable storage) so the decision survives a crash. A value is chosen when a majority of acceptors accept it.

the senators who vote & remember

📚 Learner

Finds out which value was chosen and acts on it (applies the write, updates the state machine). Learners don't vote; they just observe the outcome once a majority has accepted, often told by the acceptors or the proposer.

everyone who needs the answer

Keep your eye on the acceptors — they are where safety lives. The whole algorithm is a careful protocol for how acceptors promise and accept so that their collective memory can never represent two different chosen values.

The Two-Phase Dance

Here is single-decree Paxos — agreeing on one value — in full. A proposer runs two phases. Every acceptor keeps two durable facts: the highest proposal number it has promised, and the (number, value) it has most recently accepted (if any).

1a
PREPARE (proposer → acceptors). The proposer picks a proposal number n that is higher than any it has used before (numbers must be unique per proposer and ever increasing), and sends Prepare(n) to the acceptors.
1b
PROMISE (acceptor → proposer). When an acceptor receives Prepare(n): if n is the highest it has seen, it promises never to accept any proposal numbered below n, and it replies with any (number, value) it has already accepted. If it has already promised something higher than n, it ignores the prepare (or says no).
2a
ACCEPT (proposer → acceptors). If the proposer collects promises from a majority, it sends Accept(n, v). The value v is not free to choose if any promise reported an already-accepted value: the proposer must pick the value tied to the highest proposal number it heard about. Only if no promise reported a value may it use its own.
2b
ACCEPTED (acceptor → proposer / learners). An acceptor receiving Accept(n, v) accepts it unless it has since promised to a number higher than n. When a majority accept (n, v), the value v is CHOSEN — permanently. Learners are informed and act on it.

Step 2a is the heart of the whole algorithm. That single rule — "if anyone already accepted a value, you must re-propose that value, not your own" — is what stops a later proposer from overwriting an earlier decision. It means a proposer's job isn't really to push its own value; it's to discover and finish any decision that might already be in progress, and only propose fresh when it's certain none is.

The acceptor's logic — the entire safety-critical core
# Each acceptor keeps two durable values: promised_n = null # highest proposal number I've promised accepted = null # the (n, value) I last accepted, if any def on_prepare(n): if promised_n is null or n > promised_n: promised_n = n # promise to ignore anything below n return PROMISE(n, accepted) # ...and confess what I already accepted return REJECT # I've promised someone higher def on_accept(n, v): if promised_n is null or n >= promised_n: promised_n = n accepted = (n, v) # durably remember it, then ACK return ACCEPTED(n, v) return REJECT # A value is CHOSEN the instant a MAJORITY of acceptors hold it as `accepted`.

Why Majorities = Safety

Everything above rests on one geometric fact. Picture five acceptors. A majority is any three of them. Now take any two majorities — say {1,2,3} and {3,4,5}. They must share at least one acceptor (here, #3). It's impossible to pick two groups of three from five without an overlap. This is the quorum intersection property, and it is the whole ballgame.

Why does overlap guarantee safety? Suppose value X was chosen — that means some majority accepted it. Now a new proposer comes along with a higher number and runs Phase 1. Its Prepare reaches some majority, and that majority must overlap the one that accepted X — so at least one acceptor in its promises will report "I already accepted X". By the rule in step 2a, the new proposer is forced to propose X too. The decision can't be lost and can't be contradicted. Run this argument inductively and you get the full theorem: once a value is chosen, every future proposal — no matter how high its number — proposes the same value. Agreement is preserved forever.

The same fact kills split-brain. During a network partition, the cluster might split into groups of two and three. The group of two can never assemble a majority, so it simply cannot choose anything — it stalls. Only the side that still holds a majority can make progress. There is never a moment when both sides decide, because there can only ever be one majority at a time. This is also why consensus clusters use odd node counts (3, 5, 7): five nodes tolerate two failures (majority = 3), seven tolerate three (majority = 4) — an even count just adds a node without raising the failures you can survive.

Multi-Paxos & the Leader Optimization

Single-decree Paxos agrees on one value. But a real system — a database, a config store, a lock service — needs to agree on a whole sequence of values: command 1, then command 2, then command 3… This is the replicated log that backs a replicated state machine: if every node applies the same commands in the same order, every node ends up in the same state. You get there by running Paxos once per slot in the log — this is Multi-Paxos.

Done naïvely that's two round-trips (Phase 1 + Phase 2) per command — painfully slow. The key optimization: elect one proposer as a stable leader. The leader runs Phase 1 once for a whole range of future log slots, and thereafter only needs Phase 2 — a single round-trip per command — for as long as it stays leader. If the leader crashes, another node notices, bumps the proposal number, runs Phase 1 to recover any in-flight decisions, and becomes the new leader. Notice the beautiful recursion: electing the leader is itself a consensus decision, and Paxos is exactly what makes that election safe.

1
Steady state. One leader, Phase 1 already done; each client write is one Accept round-trip to a majority. Fast and simple.
2
Leader dies. A follower times out, picks a higher proposal number, runs Phase 1 across a majority to learn any half-finished decisions, and takes over — no data lost, no split-brain, because the majority carries the truth forward.
3
Why this matters. This "leader + replicated log + majority commit" shape is exactly what Raft (Day 32) formalizes — and what etcd, ZooKeeper, and Spanner run in production. Paxos is the theory; these are the practice.

If this part felt slippery, you're in excellent company — Paxos is famous for being hard to understand, which is precisely why Raft was invented to teach the same guarantees more clearly. That's tomorrow. Today's job was the idea: majority-quorum, two-phase, proposal-numbered agreement.

The Hard Parts

Paxos is a triumph of correctness, and a notorious source of pain. Know the sharp edges.

Safety always; liveness never guaranteed

By FLP, no async consensus can promise progress under failures. Concretely, dueling proposers can livelock: proposer A runs Phase 1 with n=1; before it finishes Phase 2, proposer B runs Phase 1 with n=2, pre-empting A; A retries with n=3, pre-empting B; forever. No value is ever chosen, yet safety still holds — it's just stuck. The fix is practical, not theoretical: elect one leader so only it proposes. Progress returns; the FLP theorem is sidestepped in practice (not disproven).

It is genuinely hard to implement correctly

The paper is short; a correct, performant implementation is not. Google's "Paxos Made Live" paper catalogs the gap between the one-page algorithm and a production system: disk corruption, handling restarts, log compaction, master leases, group membership changes. Most teams should use a battle-tested library (etcd, ZooKeeper) rather than roll their own — rolling your own consensus is a classic way to introduce subtle, catastrophic bugs.

It costs a round-trip to a majority

Every decision needs at least one round-trip to a majority of nodes, written durably. That's latency you pay for consistency — which is why you reserve consensus for the small, critical state (who's the leader, cluster config, commit order) and keep it off the high-volume data path where you can. Consensus is a scalpel, not a firehose.

CAP, revisited

Remember Day 10: under a partition you must choose consistency or availability. Paxos is the embodiment of choosing consistency (CP). The majority side stays consistent and available; the minority side deliberately refuses to serve decisions rather than risk disagreeing. That refusal isn't a bug — it's the guarantee you're paying for.

The through-line: consensus trades latency and availability-under-partition for iron-clad agreement. You give up "every node can always answer" in exchange for "no two nodes ever disagree." For the parts of a system where being wrong is catastrophic — who holds the lock, what order writes commit, which node is primary — that's exactly the trade you want.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"Consensus means everyone agrees."It means a majority agrees; crashed or unreachable nodes catch up later. Requiring unanimity would halt the system on any single failure — the opposite of fault-tolerant.
"Paxos picks the best value."It picks one proposed value, not the "best" one. Any proposed value is valid; the only guarantee is that there's exactly one. Paxos chooses a winner, not the right winner.
"A network partition can cause two decisions."No — only a side holding a majority can decide, and both sides can't simultaneously be a majority. Quorum overlap makes two conflicting decisions impossible.
"Once I've elected a leader I don't need consensus."The leader can crash. Safely replacing it, and keeping the replicated log consistent across the handoff, is exactly what consensus does. The leader is an optimization on top of Paxos, not a replacement for it.
"Paxos guarantees the cluster makes progress."It guarantees safety always, but not liveness (FLP). Dueling proposers can livelock. A single stable leader restores progress in practice.
"More nodes make it safer."More nodes tolerate more failures but need a bigger majority (slower commits) and don't change the safety guarantee. Use odd counts (3, 5, 7) so majorities are clean and an extra node actually buys fault tolerance.

Active Recall

❓ You run a 5-node consensus cluster (majority = 3). A network partition splits it into a group of 2 and a group of 3, and each group independently tries to choose a configuration value. (a) What happens in each group, and why can the two groups never disagree? (b) The group of 3 chooses value "X". The partition heals, and a node from the formerly-isolated group of 2 — which never saw X — starts a brand-new proposal with a higher proposal number. What value is it forced to choose, and which exact Paxos mechanism forces it? (click to reveal)
(a) The group of 3 has a majority, so it can run both phases and choose a value. The group of 2 can never assemble a majority, so it stalls — it cannot choose anything at all. The two groups can't disagree because there can only ever be one majority at a time: it's impossible for both a 3-group and a 2-group to each be a majority of 5. Stalling is safe; choosing a conflicting value would not be. (b) It is forced to choose X. When it runs Phase 1 (Prepare), it must collect promises from a majority (3 nodes). Any majority it reaches must overlap the majority that accepted X — so at least one acceptor replies "I already accepted X". The rule in step 2a — "propose the value tied to the highest-numbered accepted value you heard about" — then forces it to re-propose X, not its own value. The decision is permanent. This is quorum intersection plus the adopt-the-highest-accepted-value rule doing their job together.

Real-World Engineering

🔒 Google Chubby

Google's lock service — the thing that answers "who is the leader?" for GFS, Bigtable, and more — is built directly on Paxos. Its authors wrote the influential "Paxos Made Live" paper documenting just how much engineering sits between the textbook algorithm and a service that survives disk faults, restarts, and membership changes.

🐘 Apache ZooKeeper

The coordination backbone for Kafka (historically), HBase, and countless clusters. It runs ZAB, a Paxos-family atomic-broadcast protocol, to keep a replicated, strongly-consistent store of small critical state — config, leader election, locks — exactly the "scalpel, not firehose" use of consensus.

☸️ etcd & Kubernetes

Kubernetes stores all of its cluster state in etcd, which uses Raft (Paxos's more-teachable cousin, Day 32). When you kubectl apply, a majority of etcd nodes must agree before the change is committed — consensus is literally the brain of the orchestrator.

🌍 Spanner & CockroachDB

Globally-distributed SQL databases run a Paxos (or Raft) group per shard, replicating each piece of data across regions with majority commit. That's how they offer strong consistency across continents — every write is a tiny consensus decision among the replicas of its shard.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What three guarantees define distributed consensus?
A: Validity (only a proposed value can be chosen), Agreement (at most one value is ever chosen — the safety property), and Termination (a value is eventually chosen & learned — liveness). Paxos guarantees the first two unconditionally; the third only with a stable leader.
Q: What are Paxos's two phases?
A: Phase 1 — Prepare/Promise: a proposer reserves a proposal number n; acceptors promise to ignore anything lower and report any value they've already accepted. Phase 2 — Accept/Accepted: the proposer sends Accept(n, v) to a majority; the value is chosen once a majority accepts.
Q: Why do majority quorums make Paxos safe?
A: Any two majorities of N nodes share at least one node (quorum intersection). That overlapping node remembers an already-chosen value and reports it during Phase 1, forcing every later proposer to re-propose the same value — so two different values can never both be chosen.
Q: In Phase 2, when is a proposer not free to use its own value?
A: If any Phase-1 promise reported an already-accepted value, the proposer must propose the value tied to the highest proposal number it heard about. This single rule is what preserves agreement across competing proposers.
Q: Does Paxos guarantee progress, and what's the fix for when it doesn't?
A: No — by the FLP result, async consensus can't guarantee liveness under failures, and dueling proposers can livelock. Safety still always holds. The practical fix is to elect a single stable leader (Multi-Paxos) so only it proposes — one round-trip per decision.

If you only remember one thing: consensus is how unreliable machines agree on one value and never un-agree, and Paxos does it with proposal-numbered, two-phase voting on a MAJORITY. Phase 1 (Prepare/Promise) reserves a ballot and surfaces any value already in flight; Phase 2 (Accept/Accepted) commits a value once a majority accepts. The magic is quorum overlap — any two majorities share a node, so a chosen value can never be lost or contradicted, and the rule "re-propose the highest value you heard about" forces every future proposer to keep it. Safety is absolute; progress needs a leader (Multi-Paxos), because by FLP no async algorithm can promise both. This is the bedrock under replicated databases, leader election, and Kubernetes' own brain.
