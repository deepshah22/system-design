Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation
Sizing a System Before You Build It

For 29 days we've collected the parts — load balancers, caches, databases, queues, tokens. Today we learn the very first thing a senior engineer actually does when handed a problem: size it. Before you draw a single box, you ask — how many requests per second? How many bytes a day? How much bandwidth? How many servers? The answers decide everything downstream: whether one humble database will do, or whether you need sharding, a cache, and a CDN. You'll learn the estimation pipeline every engineer runs on a whiteboard, the small handful of numbers worth memorizing (including the one trick that makes the per-second math instant), and the latency ladder that explains in one picture why we cache. Then we'll size a real system — a URL shortener — from a blank page to a concrete architecture. The golden rule you'll leave with: a rough number you have in two minutes beats a perfect one you'll never finish. This closes Phase 1 — and opens the door to Phase 2, where every design starts here.

Visual Memory Anchor

🚰 🏙️ 🗼

Picture this

You've just been made the water engineer for a new city. Nobody hands you a blueprint — they hand you one number: "about a million people will live here." From that single number you have to size the entire waterworks, and you do it on the back of a napkin before lunch. Watch how it flows: a million people (your users) × roughly 150 liters each per day (their daily actions) = 150 million liters a day (your daily volume). But pipes aren't sized by the day — they're sized by the second. So you divide by the ~100,000 seconds in a day and get the flow rate the pipes must carry (that's your requests per second). Then the twist every engineer learns the hard way: everybody showers at 7 AM. The morning rush is several times the average, so you size the pipes for the peak, not the average (your peak factor). You size the reservoir for years of supply (your storage), and you build a water tower near the neighborhoods so the most common demand is served from close by without hammering the main reservoir (your cache).

Here's the thing: you never once counted actual humans or measured actual pipes. You multiplied a few round numbers and landed within a factor of two — and that was enough to decide "one pump, or a pumping station?" That is back-of-the-envelope estimation. Burn in the image: one headline number (the population) flows down through a few multiplications into flow rate, reservoir size, and pipe diameter — and the morning rush decides how big the pipes really are. Users → actions → per-second flow → peak → storage & bandwidth. That ladder is the whole lesson.

The Story

Whiteboard Wendy was three minutes into a design review when the staff engineer across the table asked the question that sinks most candidates: "Okay — will one database handle this?" The feature was a link-shortening service for the company's marketing team. Wendy's instinct was to start drawing boxes: an app server here, a database there, maybe a cache. But she stopped, because she realized she had no idea whether this was a one-laptop problem or a thousand-server problem. Drawing boxes before knowing the size is like buying a house before knowing if you have a family of two or a town of a million.

So she did what she'd watched the senior engineers do. She wrote one number at the top of the board: 100 million new links a day. (She didn't know the real figure — she estimated it from "big consumer product.") Then she walked it down. "Links are mostly read, not written — people click them far more than they create them. Call it 100 reads per write. So 100M writes a day, and 10 billion reads a day." She converted to per-second with the trick everyone memorizes — there are about 100,000 seconds in a day — and the board now read ~1,000 writes/sec and ~100,000 reads/sec. She doubled the reads for the morning rush: ~200,000 reads/sec at peak.

Now the question answered itself. "A single database can comfortably do a few thousand simple reads a second. Two hundred thousand? No single box survives that. But look —" she tapped the board — "it's overwhelmingly reads of the same popular links. So I put a cache in front (Day 7); it absorbs the vast majority, and the database only sees the misses. The writes — a thousand a second — one well-indexed database can handle, and when it can't, I shard (Day 13)." Then storage: "100M links a day × ~500 bytes ≈ 50 GB a day; over five years, with replication, a few hundred terabytes. That's a cluster, not a laptop — so object storage or a sharded store, not SQLite on one machine."

The whole thing took four minutes and not a single real measurement — just round numbers multiplied down a ladder. But those four minutes had turned a vague "build a URL shortener" into a concrete architecture with justified components: a load balancer, a cache, a sharded store, a CDN for the redirects. The staff engineer nodded. That nod is the entire point of today's lesson. Wendy left with the rule every senior engineer internalizes: estimate first, draw second. A number you can defend in two minutes is worth more than a diagram you can't.

What should stay in your head
Estimation is a pipeline, not a guess. You start from one headline number — usually Daily Active Users (DAU) — and multiply it down a fixed ladder: DAU → actions per user per day → total actions/day → ÷ ~100,000 seconds → QPS (requests/sec) → × peak factor → × bytes-per-item → storage & bandwidth → ÷ per-server capacity → number of servers. Three ideas make the math fast and honest. (1) Round everything to powers of ten. You are hunting for the order of magnitude — is this 10³ or 10⁶? — because that's what flips an architecture decision; a 2× error almost never does. (2) Memorize a tiny cheat-sheet: a day is ~10⁵ seconds; 2¹⁰≈ a thousand, 2²⁰≈ a million, 2³⁰≈ a billion, 2⁴⁰≈ a trillion (so a KB, MB, GB, TB); and the read:write ratio (reads usually dominate, which is why you cache). (3) Always split average from peak. You size for the morning rush, not the daily mean. Every estimate then answers one architectural question: does one machine do this, or do I need to spread the load (Day 5), cache the reads (Day 7), shard the storage (Day 13), replicate for safety (Day 12), and push bytes to a CDN (Day 8)? The golden rule: a defensible rough number now beats a perfect number later.

Why Estimate At All?

It feels backwards to do arithmetic before architecture — isn't the design the interesting part? But estimation isn't busywork; it's the thing that tells you which design problem you actually have. The same words — "build a link shortener" — describe a weekend project at one scale and a globally-distributed system at another. Numbers are how you tell them apart, and they do it in minutes.

🧭 It chooses your architecture

Numbers decide components.

200,000 reads/sec says "you need a cache and a fleet, not one box." 100 TB says "shard and replicate, not a single disk." The estimate is the justification for every box you'll draw.

⚖️ It finds the bottleneck

What breaks first?

Is this a request-rate problem (QPS), a storage problem (TB/PB), or a bandwidth problem (Gbps)? The dominant number tells you where to spend your effort — and where not to.

💰 It sizes cost & capacity

How many servers? How much will it cost?

Peak QPS ÷ per-server capacity = server count. Multiply by price and you have a budget. This is literally how capacity planning and cloud bills are forecast.

🗣️ It proves you can reason

The interview (and the design doc) demand it.

Every system-design interview opens with "estimate the scale." A candidate who can walk DAU → QPS → storage out loud, calmly, signals senior judgement more than any memorized diagram.

The deeper point is Fermi's insight. The physicist Enrico Fermi was famous for estimating the yield of the first atomic bomb by dropping scraps of paper and watching how far the blast wave pushed them — landing within a factor of two of the real answer with no instruments at all. His trick wasn't magic; it was decomposition. Break an impossible question into a few factors you can roughly guess, multiply them, and trust that your over- and under-estimates largely cancel. "How many piano tuners are in Chicago?" is unanswerable — until you break it into population, pianos per household, tunings per year, and tunings one person can do. That is exactly what we do to "how big is this system?" We never need the exact number. We need the order of magnitude, because that's the resolution at which architecture decisions are actually made.

The Estimation Pipeline — The Ladder You Always Climb Down

Every capacity estimate, for every system, follows the same seven rungs. Memorize the ladder and you never face a blank page again — you just fill in the numbers.

The pipeline in plain arithmetic
# 1. Start from ONE headline number DAU = 100,000,000 # daily active users (estimate it — "big consumer app") # 2. Actions per user per day → total actions writes/day = DAU × 1 = 100,000,000 # ~1 write per user reads/day = writes/day × 100 = 10,000,000,000 # read:write = 100:1 # 3. Per-second — divide by ~100,000 seconds in a day (THE trick) write QPS = 100,000,000 / 100,000 = 1,000 # requests/sec read QPS = 10,000,000,000 / 100,000 = 100,000 # 4. Peak — multiply the average by a rush-hour factor (2–10×) peak read QPS = 100,000 × 2 = 200,000 # 5. Storage — writes/day × bytes, over the retention window × replication per day = 100,000,000 × 500 B = 50 GB/day 5 yr × 3 copies= 50 GB × 365 × 5 × 3 ≈ 270 TB # 6. Bandwidth — QPS × bytes-per-item read egress = 100,000 × 500 B = 50 MB/s # 7. Servers — peak QPS ÷ what one box can do (assume ~1,000 QPS) servers = 200,000 / 1,000 = ~200 (so: cache + fleet)

Read it top to bottom once and the structure clicks: one number enters at the top, and every architectural question falls out of a rung. Rung 3 (the ÷ 100,000) is the only step people fumble — so make it a reflex. Rung 4 (peak) is the one people forget — and forgetting it means your system falls over every morning at 9 AM. Rungs 5–7 turn the request rate into the three things you actually provision: disks, pipes, and boxes.

The Latency Ladder — Why We Cache, in One Picture

There's a second cheat-sheet every engineer carries: the rough time each kind of operation takes. Popularized by Google's Jeff Dean as "latency numbers every programmer should know," it spans ten orders of magnitude — from reading a byte already in the CPU's cache to a network round trip across an ocean. You don't memorize the exact nanoseconds; you memorize the ladder of tiers, because the gaps between tiers are what justify caching, co-location, and CDNs.

CPU & memory — nanoseconds SSD & local disk — micro/milliseconds network — milliseconds

Stare at the jumps. Reading data from memory is around 100 nanoseconds. Reading it from an SSD is thousands of times slower; from a spinning disk, tens of thousands of times slower; and a round trip across the world is roughly a million times slower than memory. Each rung down is a cliff, not a step. That single fact is the entire economic case for the patterns you've learned: a cache (Day 7) exists to turn a slow disk-or-network read into a fast memory read; a CDN (Day 8) exists to turn a slow cross-ocean round trip into a fast nearby one; keeping services in the same datacenter exists because a 0.5 ms local hop beats a 150 ms intercontinental one by 300×. When you estimate and find a hot path crossing a slow rung millions of times a second, the ladder tells you exactly which pattern buys the biggest win.

Worked Example — Sizing a URL Shortener From Scratch

Let's run the whole pipeline end to end, the way you would on a whiteboard, and watch a concrete architecture fall out of the numbers. The ask: "Design a service like TinyURL."

① Headline number. Assume a big consumer product: 100 million new URLs/day. (We estimated this — "popular app" ≈ 10⁸ daily actions.)

② Reads vs writes. Links are clicked far more than created. Assume 100:1 → 10 billion reads/day. This ratio is the whole story: the system is overwhelmingly a read system.

③ Per second. Drop five zeros (÷100,000): write ~1,000 QPS, read ~100,000 QPS.

④ Peak. ×2 for the daily rush → ~200,000 reads/sec at peak.

⑤ Storage. Each record (short code, long URL, owner, timestamp) ≈ 500 bytes. 100M/day × 500 B = 50 GB/day. Over 5 years, ×3 for replication ≈ ~270 TB.

⑥ Bandwidth. A redirect is tiny. 100,000 reads/sec × ~500 B ≈ ~50 MB/s out — nowhere near a 1 Gbps (125 MB/s) link's limit per edge. Not a bandwidth problem.

⑦ Servers. 200,000 peak QPS ÷ ~1,000 QPS/box = you'd need hundreds of boxes if every read hit the database — which is the signal to not let them.

The architecture the numbers demand

Now the design writes itself — each component is justified by a number, not by habit:

⚖️ Load balancer (Day 5)

200,000 peak QPS can't land on one app server. Spread it across a fleet behind a load balancer. Justified by: peak QPS ≫ one box.

⚡ Cache (Day 7)

100:1 read-heavy, and the same popular links dominate (80/20). A cache absorbs the vast majority of the 100k reads/sec; the DB sees only misses. Justified by: read:write ratio + skew.

🗄️ Sharded + replicated store (Days 12–13)

~270 TB is far past one node, and we can't lose data. Partition the keyspace across shards, replicate each ×3. Justified by: total storage.

🌍 CDN for redirects (Day 8)

Reads are global and cacheable (a short code → a fixed URL rarely changes). Serve them from the edge to cut latency and origin load. Justified by: global read volume + cacheability.

That is the magic of estimating first. We didn't reach for a cache because caches are cool; we reached for it because 100:1 reads at 100k/sec left no other choice. Every box on the board now has a number behind it — and that is what "designing a system" actually means. In Phase 2, every single design (URL shortener, chat, feed, YouTube) opens with exactly these seven rungs.

When Rough Beats Precise — The Tradeoffs

Estimation has its own craft, and most of it is knowing when to stop. The goal is a decision, not a spreadsheet.

TensionHow to play it
Speed vs precisionOptimize for speed. You want the order of magnitude in two minutes. Three significant figures is almost always wasted effort — the inputs themselves are guesses.
Average vs peakSize for peak, but know the average too (it drives storage and cost). Confusing the two is the most common estimation bug — and the one that pages you at 9 AM.
Powers of 2 vs powers of 10For estimation, treat 2¹⁰ ≈ 10³. The ~2.4% error per step never changes a decision. Be precise only when billing or hard limits are involved.
Round numbers vs real dataStart with round guesses to get unblocked; replace them with real metrics once the system is live. Early estimates choose the shape; production data tunes it.
When estimation doesn't matterIf every plausible number lands on "one box is fine," stop — you've answered the question. Don't over-engineer a system that will never be big.

The master move is matching effort to the decision. "Do we need sharding?" needs one multiplication. "What's our exact AWS bill next quarter?" needs real data and spreadsheets. Back-of-the-envelope is for the first kind — the fork in the road where the order of magnitude is the whole answer. Spending an hour to turn "about 200 TB" into "214.6 TB" changes nothing about the architecture, and that hour is the tell of a junior engineer. Senior engineers estimate fast, decide, and move on — then let production metrics do the precise work later.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The mistakeThe reality
"I need the exact number."You need the order of magnitude. A 2× error almost never flips an architecture decision; 10³ vs 10⁶ always does. Chasing precision is the classic junior tell — estimate fast and move on.
"Average load is what I size for."Size for peak. Traffic clusters (mornings, launches, Black Friday). A system sized for the mean falls over at the rush. Always apply a peak factor.
"Bits and bytes, close enough."1 byte = 8 bits. Network links are quoted in bits per second, storage in bytes. A 1 Gbps link is ~125 MB/s, not 1 GB/s — an 8× mistake that ruins a bandwidth estimate.
"Reads and writes are about equal."Most systems are read-heavy (often 100:1 or more). Missing this means missing the cache — the single most impactful component in read-dominated designs.
"Forgetting replication and overhead."Real storage is raw data × replication factor (usually ×3) plus indexes and overhead. Estimate the raw number, then multiply — or you'll under-provision by 3× or more.
"86,400 is the number to divide by."It is — but use ~100,000 for mental speed. Rounding to 10⁵ lets you just drop five zeros. Precision here costs seconds and buys nothing.
"More detail is always better."Stop the moment the number answers the question. If every guess says "one box is fine," you're done — extra arithmetic is wasted effort, the opposite of the skill.

Active Recall

❓ An interviewer says: "Design a photo-sharing app — start by estimating the scale." You assume 500 million daily active users, each uploading ~1 photo/day at ~200 KB, read 50× more than written, kept 5 years with 3× replication. Walk the full pipeline out loud: write QPS, read QPS, peak, storage/day, 5-year storage, and read bandwidth. Then say which single number dominates the design and why. (click to reveal)
Writes: 500M uploads/day ÷ 100,000 s ≈ 5,000 writes/sec. Reads: 50× → 25 billion/day ÷ 100,000 ≈ 250,000 reads/sec; ×2 peak ≈ 500,000 reads/sec. Storage/day: 500M × 200 KB = ~100 TB/day (!). 5-year × 3 replicas: 100 TB × 365 × 5 × 3 ≈ ~550 PB. Read bandwidth: 250,000 reads/sec × 200 KB ≈ 50 GB/s — enormous. What dominates: unlike the URL shortener (a request-rate problem), this is a storage and bandwidth problem. Hundreds of petabytes screams object storage (Day 8), not a database; 50 GB/s of image egress screams CDN to serve bytes from the edge. The metadata (who, when, which album) is small and goes in a sharded database; the photos themselves go in blob/object storage fronted by a CDN. Same seven rungs as the URL shortener — but because bytes-per-item jumped from 500 B to 200 KB, the bottleneck moved entirely. That's the lesson: the pipeline is fixed, but which number is biggest tells you what kind of system you're really building.

Real-World Engineering

📝 Jeff Dean's latency numbers

Google's Jeff Dean popularized a short table of "numbers every programmer should know" — memory ~100 ns, SSD read ~150 µs, disk seek ~10 ms, cross-continent round trip ~150 ms. It became industry folklore precisely because those tiers justify nearly every caching and co-location decision engineers make.

🧪 The Fermi interview question

"How many piano tuners in Chicago?" and "how many golf balls fit in a 747?" are classic interview prompts. They aren't trivia — they test whether you can decompose an unknown into guessable factors and multiply. The exact same skill sizes a distributed system.

📊 Capacity planning & cloud bills

Every infra team forecasts capacity by estimating peak QPS, storage growth, and bandwidth, then dividing by per-unit capacity and multiplying by price. Back-of-the-envelope math is the first draft of the AWS/GCP bill — before any autoscaler or spreadsheet refines it.

🎯 The interview opener

At nearly every FAANG-style system-design interview, the first five minutes are "estimate the scale." Interviewers use it to separate candidates who reason from numbers from those who pattern-match diagrams. Calmly walking DAU → QPS → storage out loud is a senior-signal on its own.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What is the estimation pipeline, in order?
A: DAU → actions/user/day → total actions/day → ÷ ~100,000 seconds → QPS → × peak factor → × bytes-per-item → storage & bandwidth → ÷ per-server capacity → number of servers. One headline number enters the top; every architectural quantity falls out of a rung.
Q: How do you convert "actions per day" to "per second" fast?
A: Divide by ~100,000 — a day is ≈ 10⁵ seconds (86,400 rounded). In practice: just drop five zeros. 10 billion/day → 100,000/sec instantly.
Q: Powers of 2 as round decimals (and data sizes)?
A: 2¹⁰ ≈ thousand (KB), 2²⁰ ≈ million (MB), 2³⁰ ≈ billion (GB), 2⁴⁰ ≈ trillion (TB), 2⁵⁰ ≈ quadrillion (PB). For estimation, treat 2¹⁰ ≈ 10³ — the small error never flips a decision.
Q: Why always separate average from peak?
A: Traffic clusters (rush hours, launches). You size for peak (average × a peak factor of 2–10×) or the system falls over at the busiest moment. The average still matters — it drives storage and cost.
Q: Why does the latency ladder justify caching and CDNs?
A: The tiers are cliffs: memory ~100 ns, SSD ~µs, disk ~ms, cross-ocean network ~150 ms (a million× memory). A cache turns a disk/network read into a memory read; a CDN turns a cross-ocean round trip into a nearby one. The gaps are the payoff.

If you only remember one thing: estimate before you architect — size the system before you draw a single box. Run the pipeline: one headline number (usually DAU) → multiply by actions → divide by ~100,000 seconds to get QPS → multiply by a peak factor → multiply by bytes to get storage and bandwidth → divide by per-server capacity to get machine count. Keep the tiny cheat-sheet in your head (a day ≈ 10⁵ seconds; 2¹⁰/2²⁰/2³⁰/2⁴⁰ ≈ thousand/million/billion/trillion; reads usually dominate writes) and remember the latency ladder — memory to SSD to disk to a cross-ocean hop spans a million× — which is the physics behind every cache and CDN.
