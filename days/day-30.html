Back-of-Envelope Estimation
Turning "Build Twitter" Into Real Numbers

For 29 days we've collected the parts — load balancers, caches, databases, queues, replicas, gateways. Today we learn the one skill that tells you how many of each you actually need. Before you draw a single box, a good engineer does ninety seconds of quick math: how many requests per second? how many bytes stored? how much bandwidth? how many servers? That math is back-of-envelope estimation, and it's the difference between an architecture you guessed and one the numbers demanded. You'll learn the estimation pipeline (a few assumptions flow into QPS, storage, bandwidth and server count), the tiny set of numbers worth memorizing, the art of rounding so aggressively that the answer takes seconds — and why these numbers, not taste, decide whether your system fits on one laptop or forces sharding, caching and a CDN. This closes Phase 1; tomorrow we open Phase 2 with the hard distributed algorithms that big numbers force on us.

Visual Memory Anchor

💒 🧮 🍽️

Picture this

Picture a wedding caterer who has just been handed a job. Before she buys a single ingredient, she flips over a napkin and does thirty seconds of math. "500 guests. Each eats about 3 plates over the evening — call it 1,500 plates. But they don't eat evenly: everyone hits the buffet in the same 30-minute dinner rush, so I must cook for the peak, not the average — size the kitchen for maybe 3× the steady rate. Each plate needs a fridge shelf's worth of ingredients, held for the three days before the event — that's my storage. The serving line can only push so many plates per minute through one doorway — that's my throughput. And one waiter can carry maybe 20 plates an hour, so for the rush I need this many waiters."

She never computes anything exactly. 500 guests might be 480 or 530 — she doesn't care, because her answer ("rent the big kitchen, hire eight waiters, book two fridges") doesn't change. That's the whole art: a few rough assumptions, multiplied together, rounded hard, give you the size of the thing before you build it. Now map it onto a software system and it's exactly the same picture: guests = users, plates per guest = requests per user, the dinner rush = peak QPS, the fridges = storage, the serving-line doorway = bandwidth, and the waiters = servers. Burn in the image of the caterer and her napkin: you size the kitchen before the wedding — never during the dinner rush.

The Story

Guessing Gaurav was in his first serious system-design interview. "Design a photo- sharing service," the interviewer said. Gaurav dove straight for the fun part — microservices here, a message queue there, a sprinkle of Kafka — drawing boxes and arrows with real confidence. Ten minutes in, the interviewer asked a quiet question that stopped him cold: "How much storage will this actually need in a year?" Gaurav had no idea. He'd designed an elaborate machine without knowing if it was handling a thousand users or a billion. The whole design was floating in the air, attached to nothing.

He tried again a week later, and this time he started differently. Before a single box, he said: "Let me size this first." He wrote down three guesses on the whiteboard. 100 million daily users. Each uploads about 2 photos a day and scrolls a feed 20 times a day. That's it — three numbers. Then he turned the crank. 100M × 2 = 200M uploads/day; divide by the ~100,000 seconds in a day and that's ~2,000 uploads/second on average, maybe ~6,000 at peak. Reads: 100M × 20 = 2 billion/day ≈ 20,000 reads/second, peaking near 60,000. A 100:1 read-to-write ratio jumped off the napkin — "this is a read-heavy system, so caching and CDNs will matter enormously." Each photo ~1 MB: 200M × 1 MB = 200 TB of new photos every day, ~73 PB/year. He didn't even finish the sentence before the realization landed: "there's no way this fits on one database — the storage number alone forces sharding and object storage."

The interviewer leaned in. Gaurav hadn't drawn a single box yet, but he'd already justified three of the biggest architectural decisions — a CDN, aggressive caching, and sharded object storage — purely from the numbers. Every box he drew after that had a reason. "We need roughly 60 app servers at peak if each handles ~1,000 requests a second; let's round to a fleet behind a load balancer." The design was no longer floating. It was anchored to reality, and he could defend every part of it.

Gaurav walked out with the lesson every senior engineer eventually internalizes: don't design first and size later — size first, and let the numbers tell you what to design. The math took ninety seconds and was never more precise than "about." That roughness wasn't sloppiness; it was the point. He only needed to know the answer to the nearest factor of ten — and that was enough to make every real decision.

What should stay in your head
Estimation is a pipeline: a handful of assumptions flow, by multiplication, into the four numbers that actually drive a design — QPS, storage, bandwidth, and server count. Start from users × actions-per-user to get requests per day; divide by the ~100,000 seconds in a day to get average QPS; multiply by a peak factor (2–3×) because traffic clumps. Split reads from writes — most systems are read-heavy (often 100:1), and that single ratio tells you how much caching and how many replicas you'll want. Storage = writes/day × object size × retention. Bandwidth = QPS × object size. Servers = peak QPS ÷ per-server capacity. The craft is in rounding ruthlessly — round every input to a nice power of ten, keep the answer accurate only to the nearest order of magnitude, and you'll finish in under two minutes. The purpose is never a precise bill; it's a sanity check that picks the architecture: these numbers tell you whether the thing fits on one box or demands sharding, caching, and a CDN. One-line test for any design: before you draw a box, can you say — to the nearest factor of ten — how many requests per second and how many terabytes this handles?

Why Estimate At All?

It's tempting to skip the math and jump to the diagram — the boxes are the fun part. But estimation isn't busywork; it's the step that makes every later decision defensible. Three reasons it earns its ninety seconds:

🧭 It picks the architecture

Numbers choose, not taste.

"2,000 writes/sec and 73 PB/year" instantly rules out a single database and demands sharding + object storage. "50 QPS and 10 GB" rules out all that complexity — one box is correct. You can't choose the design until you know the size.

🚦 It catches the impossible early

Fail on the napkin, not in production.

If your estimate says a single component must handle 10 million writes/second to one disk, you've found a wall before building. Estimation surfaces the bottleneck while it still costs nothing to move.

🗣️ It makes you credible

Reason out loud, in numbers.

In interviews and design reviews alike, "we need ~60 servers because peak is ~60k QPS at ~1k each" signals a senior engineer. Hand-waving "it'll scale" signals the opposite. The math is how you justify every box you draw.

Notice what estimation is not: it is not capacity planning for a real budget (that's Day 82, and it uses measured data, not guesses), and it is not an exact answer. It's a fast feasibility and sizing sketch whose only job is to be right to the nearest order of magnitude — because that's all you need to make the big calls.

The Estimation Pipeline

Every estimate follows the same assembly line. You feed in a few assumptions at the top, and each stage multiplies them into one of the numbers that drives a real decision. Learn the five stations and you can size anything.

①
Assumptions. State them out loud and write them down: daily active users, actions per user per day, the read:write ratio, the average object size, and a peak factor. These are guesses — pick round numbers. Everything downstream is just these, multiplied.
②
QPS (requests/second). users × actions = requests/day, then ÷ 86,400 (the seconds in a day, which we round to ~100,000). Multiply by the peak factor for peak QPS. Split off writes using the ratio: writes = requests ÷ (ratio + 1). This single number — QPS — sizes your compute.
③
Storage. writes/day × object size × retention. Only writes add data; reads don't. Multiply by days of retention (a year ≈ 365 days; five years ≈ 1,800). This tells you whether one disk, one database, or a sharded cluster is in your future.
④
Bandwidth. QPS × object size = bytes/second on the wire. Reads usually dominate egress (data flowing out); writes dominate ingress. Big objects (video, images) make bandwidth — and therefore a CDN — the headline concern.
⑤
Servers & memory. peak QPS ÷ per-server capacity (assume a round number like ~1,000 QPS/server) = how many app servers behind the load balancer. For cache memory, size the hot working set — often the 80/20 rule: ~20% of the data serves ~80% of the reads, so cache that 20%.

That's the entire method. The beauty is that it's compositional: change one assumption at the top (say, objects are 1 MB not 1 KB) and you re-run the line in seconds to see every downstream number move. That is exactly what the calculator above is doing.

Numbers Worth Memorizing

You can't do fast math if you have to derive every constant. There's a small, famous set of numbers that senior engineers keep in their heads. Memorize these three little tables and estimation becomes almost free.

① Powers of ten — the data-size ladder

Data sizes climb by factors of 1,000. Attaching a feeling to each rung — "does it fit in RAM? on one disk? only in a cluster?" — is what makes the storage number meaningful.

② Time — the numbers that make QPS instant

The single most useful trick in all of estimation: 1 million requests/day ÷ 100,000 s ≈ 10 requests/second. So 1 request/sec ≈ 100,000/day ≈ ~3 million/month. Once that mapping is reflexive, you convert between "per day" and "per second" in your head without pausing.

③ Latency — "numbers every engineer should know" (orders of magnitude)

You won't use these for QPS math, but they tell you where time goes, which shapes caching and placement decisions. The exact figures don't matter; the ratios do — memory is ~100× faster than SSD, which is ~100× faster than a disk seek, and crossing the planet dwarfs them all.

The takeaway you carry forward: serve from memory when you can, from the same datacenter when you must, and from across the planet only when you have no choice — the exact reasoning behind caches (Day 6), replicas (Day 12) and CDNs (Day 4).

The Art of Rounding

Beginners think estimation is about getting a precise number. It's the opposite. The skill is throwing away precision aggressively so the math stays doable in your head, while the answer stays trustworthy to the nearest factor of ten. A few habits make this automatic:

🔟 Round inputs to powers of ten

87 million users becomes "100 million." 2.3 KB becomes "a couple KB." You're going to be off by less than 2×, and your final answer only needs to be right to ~10×, so this is free accuracy you don't need — spend it on speed.

➗ Cancel zeros, don't multiply them

Work in powers of ten: 108 users × 101 actions = 109/day; ÷ 105 s = 104 = 10,000 QPS. You're adding and subtracting exponents, never doing long multiplication.

🎯 Stop at one significant figure

"About 20,000 QPS," not "19,340 QPS." "Tens of terabytes," not "47.2 TB." One significant figure is all a sizing decision can use; more digits are a false sense of precision that slows you down and fools you.

🧪 Sanity-check the magnitude

Glance at the answer: does it feel right? If "a personal blog" came out needing 500 servers, you slipped a factor of 1,000 somewhere. The rough answer's job is to be obviously in the right ballpark.

The same estimate, done in exponents (≈90 seconds, in your head)
# Assumptions (rounded hard) users = 100,000,000 # 10^8 daily active users reads/user = 20 # ~10^1.3, call it 2 × 10^1 photo size = 1 MB # 10^6 bytes # QPS: add the exponents, divide by seconds/day (~10^5) reads/day = 10^8 × (2×10^1) = 2 × 10^9 # 2 billion/day avg read QPS = 2×10^9 ÷ 10^5 = 2 × 10^4 # ~20,000 /s peak (×3) ≈ 6 × 10^4 # ~60,000 /s # Storage of a day of uploads (writes), photos at 10^6 B uploads/day ≈ 2 × 10^8 # 100M users × ~2 each bytes/day = 2×10^8 × 10^6 = 2 × 10^14 # ~200 TB / DAY per year ≈ 2×10^14 × 365 ≈ 7 × 10^16 # ~73 PB → must shard + CDN

No calculator, no long multiplication — just exponents added and a couple of roundings. The answer ("tens of thousands of QPS, petabytes a year") is everything you need to start choosing boxes.

A Worked Example — Sizing a Twitter-like Feed

Let's run the whole pipeline end to end on a system we'll actually design later (Day 46). The point isn't the specific numbers — it's watching assumptions turn into architectural decisions, one stage at a time.

①
Assumptions. 300M daily active users. Each reads their feed 10×/day and posts ~0.3 tweets/day (most people lurk). A tweet is text + metadata ≈ 300 bytes. Peak factor 2×.
②
QPS. Reads: 300M × 10 = 3 billion/day ÷ 100,000 ≈ 30,000 reads/s, peak ~60,000. Writes: 300M × 0.3 ≈ 90M/day ≈ ~1,000 writes/s, peak ~2,000. The read:write ratio is ~30:1 — read-heavy, so caching and read replicas are going to matter.
③
Storage. 90M tweets/day × 300 B ≈ 27 GB/day of text. Over 5 years: 27 GB × 1,800 days ≈ ~50 TB. Text is small! That fits in one sharded database comfortably — but add images/video and the number would jump by 1,000× into petabytes, which is why media lives in separate object storage behind a CDN.
④
Bandwidth. The expensive part is building each feed (dozens of tweets per read). Even at a modest few KB per feed response, 60,000 peak reads/s × a few KB ≈ hundreds of MB/s of egress — enough that you cache assembled feeds rather than rebuild them every time.
⑤
Servers & cache. 60,000 peak QPS ÷ ~1,000 QPS/server ≈ ~60 app servers behind a load balancer. For cache: active users' recent feeds are the hot set — cache a few days of tweets (tens of GB) in Redis and most reads never touch the database. The 30:1 read ratio just paid for itself.

Look at what fell out of three guesses: a load-balanced fleet of ~60 servers, read replicas, a heavy Redis cache, a sharded database for text, and object storage + CDN for media. Every one of those is a Phase-1 concept you now own — and the estimate is what told you which to reach for. That is the entire reason estimation closes this phase: it's the glue that turns a pile of components into a sized system.

Rules of Thumb

A field guide of defaults to reach for when you have no better information. Each is a "good enough" starting assumption you can state and then refine.

⏱ Seconds in a day ≈ 100,000

The master conversion. 1 req/s ≈ 100k/day ≈ ~3M/month.

86,400 rounded to 105.

📖 Assume read-heavy

Most consumer systems read far more than they write — 10:1 to 1000:1. Drives caching + replicas.

Feeds, timelines, product pages.

📈 Peak ≈ 2–3× average

Traffic clumps around waking hours and events. Size compute for the peak, not the mean.

More for spiky events (sales, launches).

🖥️ ~1,000 QPS per server

A rough stand-in for a commodity app server. Divide peak QPS by it to count servers.

Real number varies 10×; good for a sketch.

🎯 80/20 for caching

~20% of the data serves ~80% of the reads. Cache that hot slice; it's usually small enough for RAM.

Sizes your Redis/Memcached tier.

📦 Object-size anchors

Text/metadata ~hundreds of bytes; a photo ~1 MB; a minute of video ~10 MB. Media dwarfs text by 1,000×.

Media → object storage + CDN.

➕ Headroom & growth

Size for a few years and leave slack. Doubling your estimate for safety is cheaper than an outage.

Plan for 2–3× current load.

🔟 One significant figure

Round everything to a power of ten; keep one digit. Precision beyond that is fiction at this stage.

"~20k QPS," not "19,340."

The Hard Parts

Estimation looks trivial until you lean on it too hard or too little. The senior skill is knowing exactly how much weight it can bear.

Garbage in, garbage out — the assumptions are everything

The whole estimate is only as good as the guesses at the top. If you assume 2 photos/day but users really post 20, every downstream number is 10× wrong. So state your assumptions out loud, make them reasonable, and — crucially — know which one your answer is most sensitive to. Usually one assumption dominates (object size for a media app, actions/user for a social app); get that one roughly right and the rest can be sloppy.

Average hides the peak — and the peak is what breaks you

"20,000 QPS average" sounds survivable; the 3 a.m. batch job or the Super Bowl ad that briefly drives 200,000 QPS is what actually takes you down. Estimation must always carry a peak factor, and for spiky, event-driven systems that factor can be 10× or more, not 2–3×. Sizing for the average is one of the most common and most dangerous mistakes.

Don't confuse a sketch with a budget

Back-of-envelope math is for choosing an architecture, not for provisioning a real cluster or forecasting a cloud bill. Those need measured numbers from load tests and production telemetry — real capacity planning, which is Day 82. Treating a 90-second guess as a procurement plan is how teams end up wildly over- or under-provisioned.

False precision is a red flag

If someone presents "47,293 QPS and 182.4 TB" from assumptions that were themselves guesses, the extra digits are theater — they imply a certainty the inputs can't support. A confident "tens of thousands of QPS, tens of terabytes" is both more honest and more useful. The goal is the right order of magnitude, stated plainly.

The through-line: estimation trades precision for speed and insight. It will never tell you the exact size of anything — and that's fine, because its real job is to make the big decisions (shard or not? cache or not? one box or a fleet?) obvious in the time it takes to write six numbers on a napkin.

🗣️ The system-design interview staple

Nearly every big-tech design interview opens with "estimate the scale." Interviewers use it to see whether you reason in numbers or hand-wave. The structured flow — assumptions → QPS → storage → bandwidth → servers — is exactly what they're scoring.

📉 Capacity planning at scale

Real teams start every new service with a back-of-envelope sketch, then validate it with load tests and production metrics. The envelope picks the architecture; the measurements (Day 82) size the actual fleet and budget. Both matter — in that order.

🎬 Why Netflix & YouTube are CDN-first

Run the video math (as above) and bandwidth, measured in hundreds of GB/s, dominates everything. That single estimate is why streaming giants push content to thousands of edge locations — the envelope literally dictates the company's core architecture.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What are the five stages of the estimation pipeline?
A: Assumptions → QPS → Storage → Bandwidth → Servers. A few guesses (users, actions/user, read:write, object size, peak factor) multiply into requests/second, then storage (writes × size × retention), bandwidth (QPS × size), and server count (peak QPS ÷ ~1,000 QPS/server).
Q: How many seconds are in a day, and why does that number matter?
A: 86,400 — rounded to ~100,000 (105). It's the master conversion between "per day" and "per second": 1 million/day ≈ 10/s, so 1/s ≈ 100k/day ≈ 3M/month.
Q: Why size for peak instead of average, and what's a typical peak factor?
A: Because the peak is what overwhelms a system, not the mean — traffic clumps. A common default is 2–3× average, but spiky/event-driven systems can peak 10×+. Always carry a peak factor through the compute estimate.
Q: What adds to storage — reads or writes? Give the formula.
A: Only writes add data. Storage = writes/day × object size × retention. Reads consume bandwidth (QPS × object size), not storage. (A year ≈ 365 days; 5 years ≈ 1,800.)
Q: Why round so aggressively, and to what accuracy?
A: Because the goal is the right order of magnitude, fast — not a precise number. Round inputs to powers of ten, work by adding/subtracting exponents, and keep one significant figure. The answer only needs to be right to ~10× to make the big decisions.
