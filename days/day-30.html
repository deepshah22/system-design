Back-of-Envelope Estimation
Sizing a System Before You Build It

For 29 days we've collected building blocks — load balancers, caches, databases, queues, tokens. Today we learn the skill that tells you which of them you actually need, and how many: back-of-envelope estimation, the quick mental math every engineer scrawls on a whiteboard to turn a vague "build a URL shortener" into concrete numbers — requests per second, bytes stored, servers required. You'll memorize three small sets of numbers (the 86,400 ≈ 10⁵ seconds-in-a-day trick, a data-size ladder from a byte to a petabyte, and a latency ladder from memory to disk to a cross-ocean hop), then learn a repeatable seven-step recipe — scale → traffic → read:write ratio → peak → storage → bandwidth → memory. We'll run a full worked example end to end and watch each number force a design decision you already know how to make: when to cache, when to shard, when to add replicas. This is the finale of Phase 1 — and the bridge into designing real systems.

Visual Memory Anchor

✉️ ✏️ 🧮

Picture this

Picture two engineers at a bar. One says, "Let's build a photo app — like Instagram." The other doesn't open a laptop or sketch a database schema. They grab the nearest thing with a blank side — the back of an envelope — and start scribbling. "Okay, say a hundred million people, each posts twice a day… that's two hundred million photos a day… divide by about a hundred thousand seconds in a day… so roughly two thousand uploads a second. Each photo maybe a megabyte… that's two hundred gigabytes a day… call it seventy terabytes a year." Thirty seconds of scribbling, and they already know three things: it won't fit on one machine (must shard), reads will dwarf writes (must cache), and the bill is real.

That envelope is doing something almost magical: with no data, no benchmark, and numbers rounded brutally, it turns a fuzzy idea into a shape. It's not trying to be right to three decimals — it's trying to be right to the nearest power of ten, which is exactly enough to tell "runs on a laptop" apart from "needs a thousand servers." Burn in the image: a crumpled envelope covered in arrows and round numbers, divided by that magic ≈10⁵ seconds in a day, feeding a little funnel — users → requests per second → bytes → servers. Whenever someone asks "how big is this?", reach for the envelope before you reach for the keyboard.

The Story

Guesswork Gina was a brilliant coder who hated "hand-wavy" math. When her team was asked to design a notification service — "push alerts to our users" — she skipped the napkin and went straight to building. She picked a single PostgreSQL instance (Day 8), wrote beautiful code, and shipped. It worked great in the demo with a hundred test users.

Then it met reality: forty million users, each getting a handful of notifications a day. The "handful × forty million" she never did on paper turned out to be hundreds of millions of writes a day — thousands per second, spiking far higher at the 8 AM "good morning" push. Her single database fell over within the hour. Worse, she'd sized a disk for "a few gigabytes"; the real working set was terabytes. Every architectural choice she'd made — one database, no cache, no queue — was wrong, and it was wrong for a reason she could have discovered in thirty seconds with a pen, before writing a line of code.

A staff engineer sat her down and asked one question: "Before you build anything — how many requests per second, and how many bytes? Just order of magnitude." Gina protested that she couldn't know that precisely. "You don't need precise," he said. "You need to know whether it's ten per second or ten thousand per second — because those are completely different systems." He showed her the trick: forty million users × say five notifications is two hundred million a day; a day is about a hundred thousand seconds; two hundred million divided by a hundred thousand is two thousand per second on average — and maybe ten thousand at the morning peak. "That number," he said, "tells you everything: you need a queue to absorb the spike (Day 18), a cache for the reads (Day 7), and the data sharded across machines (Day 13). You knew the whole architecture before lunch."

Gina rebuilt it — this time the envelope came first. She sized the peak, put a message queue in front to flatten it, fronted reads with a cache, and partitioned the store. It held. And she noticed something career-changing: in every design interview and every planning meeting after that, the engineers who seemed "senior" weren't the ones who knew the most frameworks — they were the ones who, within a minute of hearing a problem, could say "that's about ten thousand QPS and a hundred terabytes, so here's the shape."

Gina's lesson is the whole lesson: estimate first. Round hard. The goal isn't the exact number — it's the order of magnitude, because the order of magnitude is what chooses your architecture.

What should stay in your head
Back-of-envelope estimation is deliberately rough arithmetic you do before designing, to find a system's order of magnitude — because "10 requests/sec" and "100,000 requests/sec" are not the same system, and the number decides the architecture. The single most useful fact is that a day has 86,400 ≈ 10⁵ seconds, so average QPS = (actions per day) ÷ 100,000. Then you keep pulling the same thread: peak traffic is the average times a peak factor (≈2–10×) and is what you must provision for; reads usually dwarf writes (often 10:1 to 1000:1), which is why caching and read replicas exist; storage compounds over years (bytes/day × 365 × retention), which is what forces sharding; bandwidth is just QPS × payload size; and cache memory follows the 80/20 rule — keep the hot ~20% in RAM. You hold three small ladders in your head: time (10⁵ s/day), data sizes (byte → KB → MB → GB → TB → PB, each ×1000), and latency (memory ~100 ns, SSD ~0.1 ms, datacenter round-trip ~0.5 ms, disk seek ~10 ms, cross-ocean ~150 ms). The craft is rounding without guilt — one or two significant figures, powers of ten — and then reading the answer: a number tells you which building blocks from the last 29 days you need and how many. One-line test: before I draw a single box, do I know this system's QPS, its bytes, and its peak — to the nearest power of ten?

Try this: start at the defaults (100M users, 2 writes each, 1 KB, 100:1 reads, 5× peak). That's ~2,000 writes/sec but ~200,000 reads/sec — and a peak near a million QPS, which screams "cache everything and spread it across many machines." Why Estimate At All?

It feels unscientific to multiply rounded guesses — so why do senior engineers do it constantly? Because the purpose of the estimate is not to predict the future accurately; it's to rule out whole categories of design cheaply, before you've spent weeks building the wrong one. The decisions that matter most in system design are coarse: one database or many? cache or no cache? synchronous or queued? one region or many? Each of those flips at a threshold, and you only need to know which side of the threshold you're on.

Think of it like estimating whether you can afford a house. You don't need to know the price to the dollar — you need to know if it's "roughly my annual salary" or "roughly ten times my salary," because those are completely different conversations. System design is the same: being off by 2× almost never changes the architecture, but being off by 100× changes everything. Rounding to the nearest power of ten is not sloppiness — it's choosing the right precision for the decision at hand. And in an interview, a quick, confident estimate is the single clearest signal that you think like an engineer and not just a coder.

Numbers to Memorize — Three Small Ladders

Estimation is only fast if you aren't computing from scratch. You carry three cheap ladders in your head. None of them needs to be exact — each is rounded to be memorable.

⏱ Time

A day ≈ 10⁵ seconds.

86,400 rounds to 100,000 ≈ 10⁵. So QPS ≈ requests-per-day ÷ 100,000. A month ≈ 2.5 million seconds. These two turn "per day/month" into "per second" instantly.

📦 Data sizes

Each rung is ×1000.

byte → KB (10³) → MB (10⁶) → GB (10⁹) → TB (10¹²) → PB (10¹⁵). Rules of thumb: a char ≈ 1 B, an ID/UUID ≈ 16 B, a tweet/metadata row ≈ 1 KB, a photo ≈ 1 MB, a minute of video ≈ 50 MB.

⚡ Latency

Memory is ~100,000× faster than a disk seek.

Memory read ≈ 100 ns; SSD read ≈ 0.1 ms; same-datacenter round-trip ≈ 0.5 ms; disk seek ≈ 10 ms; round-trip across the planet ≈ 150 ms. This is why caching (Day 7) and avoiding cross-region hops matter so much.

The powers-of-two footnote

Sometimes you'll see 2¹⁰ ≈ 1 thousand, 2²⁰ ≈ 1 million, 2³⁰ ≈ 1 billion, and 2³² ≈ 4 billion (the number of IPv4 addresses, and the limit of a 32-bit integer). These show up when you reason about how many bits an ID needs — e.g. "how long must a short URL be?" We'll use exactly this on Day 38. For most estimates, though, you can treat a KB as 1000 bytes, not 1024 — the whole point is that a 2.4% error never changes an architecture.

# "40 million users, 5 writes each per day" → writes per second? writes_per_day = 40,000,000 × 5 = 200,000,000 # 2 × 10^8 seconds_per_day ≈ 100,000 # 10^5 (the magic number) write_QPS ≈ 2×10^8 / 10^5 = 2,000 / sec # done — in your head

The Seven-Step Recipe

Every capacity estimate — in an interview or a design doc — follows the same pipeline. Pull each thread in order; each step's output is the next step's input. Say the numbers out loud as round figures.

1
Clarify the scale. Ask for (or assume) daily active users (DAU) and actions per user per day. This is the one place you're allowed to make up a number — state your assumption clearly ("let's say 100M DAU, 2 posts each") and everything downstream follows.
2
Traffic → average QPS. Multiply DAU × actions to get actions per day, then divide by 100,000 (≈ seconds in a day). That's your average writes per second. Reads come next.
3
Read : write ratio. Most systems read far more than they write (a tweet is written once, read thousands of times). Pick a ratio — 10:1, 100:1, 1000:1 — and multiply to get read QPS. This single number is why caches and read replicas exist.
4
Peak factor. Traffic isn't flat — it spikes (mornings, launches, Black Friday). Multiply average QPS by a peak factor of ~2–10×. Peak is the number you provision for; average is only for cost. Getting this wrong is how systems fall over at 8 AM.
5
Storage over time. Bytes per day = writes/day × object size. Then multiply by retention: × 365 × N years. Storage compounds, and the multi-year total is what tells you whether it fits on one machine or must be sharded (Day 13).
6
Bandwidth. Read bandwidth = read QPS × object size; write bandwidth = write QPS × object size. Big objects (photos, video) make bandwidth — not QPS — the bottleneck, and point you at a CDN and object storage.
7
Memory / cache. Apply the 80/20 rule: ~20% of data serves ~80% of requests. Estimate the hot working set (e.g. 20% of a day's objects) to size your cache RAM (Day 7). If the hot set fits in memory, your reads are cheap; if not, you need more cache nodes.

Then: sanity-check and round. Collapse every answer to one or two significant figures and a power of ten, and ask "does this feel right?" If you got "0.3 QPS" for a global app or "40 billion servers," you dropped a zero somewhere — redo it. Estimation's final step is always a gut-check.

Worked Example — Design a URL Shortener

Let's run the whole recipe on the classic interview warm-up: a URL shortener (TinyURL/bit.ly). Someone creates a short link; millions of people click it. We'll preview the exact system you'll design in full on Day 38 — today we just size it. Watch how four assumptions produce an architecture.

Step by step, out loud
# 1 & 2 — SCALE → WRITE QPS new_links_per_day ≈ 100,000,000 # assume 100M new short URLs/day write_QPS ≈ 10^8 / 10^5 = 1,000 / sec # 3 — READ : WRITE (links are clicked far more than created) ratio ≈ 100 : 1 read_QPS ≈ 1,000 × 100 = 100,000 / sec # 4 — PEAK (a viral link spikes traffic) peak_read_QPS ≈ 100,000 × 2 = 200,000 / sec # provision for THIS # 5 — STORAGE (keep links ~5 years; each row ≈ 500 bytes) storage_per_day ≈ 10^8 × 500 B = 50 GB / day storage_5yr ≈ 50 GB × 365 × 5 ≈ ~90 TB # 6 — BANDWIDTH (tiny objects → QPS, not bytes, is the pressure) read_bandwidth ≈ 100,000 × 500 B ≈ 50 MB / sec # 7 — CACHE (hot 20% of a day's links serve most clicks) cache_RAM ≈ 20% × 50 GB ≈ ~10 GB (hot set)

Now read the numbers and the architecture writes itself:

200,000 reads/sec at peak

Far too much for one server or one database to serve from disk. You need a cache (Day 7) in front and load balancing (Day 5) across many read nodes.

number → caching + horizontal scale

~90 TB over 5 years

Won't fit on a single machine. The data must be partitioned/sharded (Day 13) across many nodes — and consistent hashing (Day 14) decides which key lives where.

number → sharding

100:1 read-heavy

Reads dominate, so add read replicas (Day 8) and lean on the cache. Writes are only ~1,000/sec — a single primary can handle those comfortably.

number → replicas

Tiny 500-byte objects

Bandwidth (50 MB/s) is trivial; the pressure is request rate, not bytes. A key-value store (Day 39) fits perfectly — no need for a CDN of large blobs here.

In under two minutes, with nothing but rounded multiplication, you've justified a cache, a load balancer, read replicas, and a sharded key-value store — the entire high-level design — and you can defend every box with a number. That is what estimation buys you.

The Hard Parts

Estimation looks trivial until you do it under pressure. The senior skill is knowing where the rough edges are.

Average vs. peak — the mistake that takes systems down

The number that matters is almost never the average; it's the peak. A system sized for average traffic falls over the instant everyone shows up at once — the 8 AM push, the Super Bowl ad, the celebrity retweet. Always multiply by a peak factor and provision for that. (The flip side: peak is for capacity; average is for cost. You pay for peak capacity even when it's idle, which is why queues and autoscaling exist — to flatten the spike instead of over-provisioning for it.)

Round early, round hard — but keep track of your zeros

Aggressive rounding is the whole technique, but the one thing you must not fumble is the power of ten. "200 million ÷ 100 thousand" is "2×10⁸ ÷ 10⁵ = 2×10³"; do the exponents separately from the digits and you'll stop dropping zeros. A good estimate carried to the wrong power of ten is worse than no estimate — it's confidently wrong.

Garbage in, garbage out — state your assumptions

Every estimate rests on inputs you assumed (DAU, actions, object size, ratio). If those are nonsense, so is the answer. The fix isn't to agonize over them — it's to say them out loud: "assuming 100M DAU and a 100:1 read ratio…" Now your interviewer (or future self) can correct one input and watch the whole estimate update, instead of distrusting the conclusion. Transparency about assumptions is what separates an estimate from a guess.

Know when the envelope stops

Back-of-envelope math is for ruling out designs and picking a shape, not for final capacity planning. Once you've chosen the architecture and need to actually buy hardware or set autoscaling limits, you load-test and measure — real numbers beat estimates. The envelope gets you to the right neighborhood fast and cheaply; benchmarks find the exact house. Don't confuse the two, and don't over-engineer precision the decision doesn't need.

The through-line: estimation trades precision for speed and clarity. You give up being exactly right in exchange for being approximately right in thirty seconds — early enough to change the design while it's still free to change. There is no "best" estimate, only one precise enough to make the decision in front of you.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"I can't estimate without real data, so I shouldn't guess."The goal is the order of magnitude, not the exact figure. "Roughly 10K QPS" is enough to choose an architecture. State your assumptions and go — a rough estimate beats no estimate every time.
"Size the system for average traffic."Size for peak. Average is for cost; peak is for capacity. A system built for the average collapses the moment everyone arrives at once (the 8 AM push).
"Reads and writes are about the same."Most systems are heavily read-skewed (often 10:1 to 1000:1). That skew is the whole reason caches and read replicas exist — assuming 1:1 hides your real bottleneck.
"A day is 86,400 seconds — let me use that exactly."Use ≈10⁵. The 15% error is irrelevant to an order-of-magnitude answer, and the round number keeps the math in your head instead of on a calculator.
"Storage is a one-day number."Storage compounds — multiply by 365 and the retention years. "50 GB/day" sounds fine until it's "90 TB over five years," which is what actually forces sharding.
"Estimation replaces load testing."It precedes it. The envelope rules out wrong designs cheaply; once you've picked one, you still measure real numbers before provisioning. Different tools for different stages.

Active Recall

❓ An interviewer says: "Design a Twitter-like feed. We have 200 million daily active users; assume each user posts twice a day and reads their feed 20 times a day; a tweet is about 300 bytes." On the spot, and out loud, estimate: write QPS, read QPS, the peak you'd provision for, and the 5-year storage — then say which two architectural decisions those numbers force. (click to reveal)
Writes: 200M × 2 = 400M posts/day. ÷ 10⁵ ≈ 4,000 writes/sec. Reads: 200M × 20 = 4 billion feed reads/day. ÷ 10⁵ ≈ 40,000 reads/sec (a ~10:1 read-skew — reads dominate, as expected). Peak: apply ~3–5×, so provision for roughly 120,000–200,000 reads/sec — that's the number that sizes your fleet, not the average. Storage: 400M posts/day × 300 B ≈ 120 GB/day of tweet text; × 365 × 5 ≈ ~220 TB over five years (and that's text only — media would dwarf it). What it forces: (1) the 40K+ read QPS at a 10:1 skew means you must put a cache (Day 7) in front and use read replicas (Day 8) — you can't serve that from disk; (2) ~220 TB won't fit on one machine, so the data must be sharded (Day 13). Bonus senior move: a spiky peak suggests pre-computing/fan-out feeds via a queue (Day 18) rather than building every feed on read. Notice you justified the entire high-level design from four assumptions and a pen.

Real-World Engineering

📄 Jeff Dean's "Numbers Everyone Should Know"

Google's legendary engineer popularized a latency cheat-sheet — memory reads in nanoseconds, disk seeks in milliseconds, cross-continent round-trips in hundreds of ms. It became required knowledge precisely because those orders of magnitude silently decide whether a design is fast or hopeless. Our latency ladder is its descendant.

🧑‍💻 The system design interview

At nearly every big-tech company, a capacity estimate is an explicit, scored part of the design round. Interviewers aren't checking your arithmetic — they're checking whether you reach for the envelope at all, state assumptions, and let the numbers drive the design. It's the clearest "thinks like an engineer" signal they have.

📊 Capacity planning & SRE

Before any launch, SRE teams estimate expected and peak load, then provision (and set autoscaling) with headroom. The back-of-envelope number sets the starting point; load tests refine it. Under-estimate the peak and you page the on-call at launch; over-estimate and you burn money on idle machines.

💸 Cloud cost sizing

"How many instances, how much storage, how much egress?" is a monthly estimation exercise at every company running on AWS/GCP/Azure. Egress bandwidth especially is estimated up front (QPS × object size) because it's a line item that can dwarf compute — exactly the "big objects → bandwidth is the bottleneck" insight from today.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What is the single most useful number in back-of-envelope estimation, and how do you use it?
A: A day ≈ 10⁵ (100,000) seconds. So average QPS ≈ requests-per-day ÷ 100,000. It converts any "per day" figure into "per second" instantly, in your head.
Q: Average QPS or peak QPS — which do you provision for, and why?
A: Peak. Multiply average by a peak factor (~2–10×). Average is for cost; peak is for capacity. A system sized for the average falls over when everyone arrives at once.
Q: Why does the read:write ratio matter so much?
A: Most systems are heavily read-skewed (10:1 to 1000:1). Read QPS = write QPS × ratio, and a high read load is the entire reason caches (Day 7) and read replicas (Day 8) exist.
Q: How do you estimate multi-year storage, and what decision does it drive?
A: bytes/day = writes/day × object size, then × 365 × N years. Storage compounds; when the total exceeds one machine (TB–PB), it forces partitioning / sharding (Day 13).
Q: Roughly how much faster is a memory read than a disk seek — and why do you care?
A: Memory ≈ 100 ns, disk seek ≈ 10 ms — about 100,000× faster. That gap is the whole reason caching changes a system's behavior: serving from RAM vs. disk isn't a tweak, it's a different order of magnitude.

  • List the seven steps of the estimation recipe in order, saying what each one's output feeds into.

  • For a URL shortener at 100M new links/day and 100:1 reads, estimate peak read QPS and 5-year storage — and name the architecture it forces.
  • Why does object size (1 KB vs 1 MB) change which bottleneck you hit, even when QPS is identical?
  • When does back-of-envelope math stop being the right tool, and what replaces it?
If you only remember one thing: estimate before you design, round hard, and chase the order of magnitude — because the order of magnitude is what chooses the architecture. Carry three ladders: a day ≈ 10⁵ seconds (so QPS = actions/day ÷ 100,000); data sizes byte→KB→MB→GB→TB→PB, each ×1000; and latency from memory (~100 ns) to disk seek (~10 ms) to cross-ocean (~150 ms).
