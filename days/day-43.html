Design a Pastebin
Your First Complete Little Product

For five days you've been building components — a shortener, a key-value store, a cache, a crawler, a rate limiter. Today you assemble your first whole product, end to end: a pastebin. Someone drops in a chunk of text or code, you store it, and you hand back a short URL anyone can open to read it back — think Pastebin.com or a GitHub Gist. It looks almost identical to Day 38's URL shortener, and that's the point: it's the write-heavy sibling — the shortener saved a tiny string and returned a tiny key; the pastebin saves a big blob and returns a tiny key. That one difference drives the whole design. You'll generate a short unguessable key (and see exactly why auto-increment IDs leak every paste you've got), make the single most important move — splitting the tiny metadata from the large content so your database never bloats — serve a brutally read-heavy workload from a cache and CDN because the content never changes, and expire old pastes with a TTL. You'll fire a live key generator and watch keyspace and collision odds move, then warm up a cache-vs-origin read simulator and watch the hit ratio climb.

Picture this

Picture the wall of lockers at a train station, hundreds of numbered metal doors, and next to them a little ticket machine. You've scribbled something on a sheet of paper — a long message, a page of notes — and you want a friend to be able to read it later without you being there. So you find an empty locker, put your sheet inside, and the machine prints you a tiny ticket with a short code on it: 7h3k2q. That's it. You text your friend just those six characters.

Your friend walks up to the wall, taps 7h3k2q into the machine, and that exact locker pops open with your sheet inside. The short code is the whole address — nobody browses the wall, nobody opens lockers at random; you either have the code or you don't. Notice the quiet split that makes this work: the lockers are big and hold the bulky paper (that's your content), but the machine's little logbook only records one tiny line — "ticket 7h3k2q → locker 4412, clear it at midnight, padlocked." The logbook is small and quick to flip through; the lockers are big and dumb and just hold stuff. Keep those two things separate and everything stays fast.

Two more details complete the picture. Some lockers have a padlock — a private paste that needs a password, or one only you can open. And a janitor walks the wall clearing out lockers whose time is up — that's expiry, the paste that vanishes after ten minutes or a week. Hold the image: a wall of big lockers (the content, in object storage), a tiny logbook of ticket-codes pointing at lockers (the metadata, in a database), a six-character ticket you can text to anyone (the short key), a padlock (access control), and a janitor (TTL expiry). That picture is a pastebin — and the entire rest of today is just making that wall work for a billion lockers and ten thousand people a second.

The Story

Sofia was tired of watching her team paste enormous stack traces into chat, where they'd get truncated, lose their formatting, and become un-searchable an hour later. So one weekend she built a tiny internal pastebin: a text box, a "Create" button, and a page that showed the text back. On the backend she did the obvious thing — stored each paste as a row in Postgres with the text in a TEXT column, auto-increment integer primary key, and returned a URL like /p/1, /p/2, /p/3. It worked on Monday and everyone loved it.

On Tuesday a security-minded colleague sent her a screenshot. He'd simply changed the number in the URL — /p/1, /p/2, /p/3 — and walked the entire database, reading every paste anyone had ever made, including one with a database password in it. Her sequential IDs weren't just ugly, they were an enumeration hole: a predictable ID means anyone can guess every other ID by counting. Sofia swapped the integer for a random short key — six base62 characters, like the shortener from Day 38 — generated at creation and stored as the lookup key. Now a paste's URL was /7h3k2q, and the only way to reach it was to already have the link. Unguessable by default.

By Thursday the database was in trouble. Someone had pasted a 4 MB log file; others followed. The Postgres table ballooned, nightly backups crawled, and — worse — every single read dragged the whole fat blob through the database and over the wire, even for a one-line snippet. Sofia realized the mistake: she was storing a big, opaque content blob in a system built for small, structured rows. She split them. The content — the actual bytes of the paste — went into object storage (an S3 bucket), which is built to hold huge blobs cheaply. The metadata — the key, who made it, when, when it expires, whether it's private — stayed in the database as a tiny row that just held a pointer (the object-storage key) to where the content lived. The database got small and fast again; the blobs lived somewhere built for blobs.

Finally, she noticed the shape of the traffic. One paste — an incident postmortem link dropped in the company Slack — got read thousands of times while most pastes were read once or twice. Pastebins are wildly read-heavy, and the content is immutable: once written, a paste never changes. That's the dream case for caching. Sofia put a cache in front (Redis for metadata, a CDN for the content bytes) with a long TTL, because immutable content can be cached forever without fear of staleness. Popular pastes were served straight from the edge; the origin barely noticed. She added expiry (10 minutes / 1 day / never) checked lazily on read plus a nightly sweeper, and visibility (unlisted / private / password). Without meaning to, Sofia had rebuilt exactly what Pastebin.com and GitHub Gist are — a short unguessable key, a tiny metadata row pointing at a big immutable blob, served read-heavy through a cache.

What should stay in your head
A pastebin is a write-once, read-many key-value store for text — the write-heavy twin of the URL shortener. The whole product is two operations: create (take text → return a short key) and read (take a key → return the text). Three decisions carry the design. (1) The key: generate a short, random, unguessable base62 key — never an auto-increment integer, which lets anyone enumerate every paste by counting. (2) The split: store the big content blob in object storage (S3), built for large cheap blobs, and keep only tiny metadata (key, owner, timestamps, expiry, visibility, and a pointer to the blob) in a database — so the DB never bloats and stays fast. (3) The reads: the workload is brutally read-heavy and the content is immutable, so cache it hard — a CDN for the bytes, Redis for metadata — with long TTLs, because content that never changes can never go stale in a cache. Everything else is tuning: expire pastes with a TTL (lazy-delete on read plus a background sweeper), and control access through unlisted unguessable links, private pastes, and passwords. One-line test: a pastebin is an unguessable short key mapping to an immutable blob in object storage, with tiny metadata in a database, served read-heavy through a cache.

With caching on, the hit ratio climbs fast because a few hot pastes dominate the reads — the cache quickly holds exactly the pastes everyone wants, and the origin only sees cold misses. The reason this works so cleanly is immutability: unlike a cache of changing data (where you fight invalidation), a paste's bytes never change, so a hit is always correct.

Why This Problem Exists

People constantly need to share a block of text that's too big, too ugly, or too formatted to drop into a chat message or an email — a stack trace, a config file, a SQL query, a page of logs, a code snippet for a colleague. Chat apps mangle it, email buries it, and neither gives you a stable link you can point other people at. A pastebin solves exactly that: paste once, get a short URL, share the URL. The content lives at a stable address, keeps its formatting, and anyone with the link can read it — no accounts, no attachments, no truncation. It's one of the most-used utilities on the internet precisely because it does one humble thing reliably.

Under the hood it's deceptively close to the URL shortener you built on Day 38 — both map a short key to something you stored — but the direction of the data flips the whole design. The shortener receives a big thing (a long URL) and stores a tiny thing (just that string); a pastebin receives a big thing (kilobytes or megabytes of text) and must store that big thing, then serve it back many times. That's why a pastebin forces two lessons the shortener didn't: where do you put a large blob (not in your metadata database), and how do you serve it cheaply when it's read far more than it's written (cache the immutable content). The core logic is trivial — two operations, create and read — but, as always, the difficulty is in the details: an unguessable key, the metadata/blob split, read-side caching, expiry, and access control.

The entire pastebin, in pseudocode — two operations; everything else is where you put the bytes
def create(text, visibility, ttl): key = gen_random_key(length=7) # short, UNGUESSABLE base62 — not an auto-increment id blob_id = object_store.put(text) # the BIG content goes to S3 / object storage db.insert({ # the DB holds only a tiny row... "key": key, "blob_id": blob_id, # ...with a POINTER to the blob "visibility": visibility, "expires_at": now() + ttl }) # expiry = a timestamp + a sweeper return "https://pb.io/" + key # the short URL you share def read(key): meta = cache.get(key) or db.lookup(key) # read-heavy → serve metadata from cache if meta is None or expired(meta): return 404 # lazy-delete on read return cdn_or_object_store.get(meta.blob_id) # immutable content → cache / CDN forever

The Design, Step by Step

The same discipline as the last five designs: scope it, size it, sketch the interface, then draw the boxes. The twist today is that there are two kinds of data with wildly different sizes and access patterns — tiny metadata and big content — and almost every decision follows from keeping them in the right homes.

Step 1 — Scope the requirements

✅ Functional

Create a paste from text → return a short URL.

Read a paste by its key (with syntax highlighting, optionally).

Support expiry (TTL) and visibility (unlisted / private / password).

🎯 Non-functional

Read-heavy — reads vastly outnumber writes; reads must be fast and cheap.

Durable — a paste shouldn't vanish before its expiry.

Cheap at scale — storage grows forever; it must cost little per paste.

🤔 Clarify early

How big can a paste be? (caps change storage + upload design.)

Edit/delete? Usually immutable — simplifies caching enormously.

Anonymous or accounts? Who may read a private paste?

Step 2 — Size it (back-of-the-envelope, Day 30)

Say 1,000,000 new pastes/day, a read:write ratio of about 10:1 (10M reads/day), and an average paste of 10 KB.

# throughput — note how lopsided reads vs writes are writes/sec = 1,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ ~12 / s # avg; peak ~5× ≈ 60/s reads/sec = 10,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ ~115 / s # avg; peak ~600/s → READ-heavy # storage — the content is the whole cost, and it only grows content/day = 1,000,000 × 10 KB = ~10 GB / day # ≈ 3.6 TB/yr → OBJECT STORAGE (cheap), not the DB metadata/day= 1,000,000 × 100 B = ~100 MB / day # ≈ 36 GB/yr → a tiny DB, easily indexed # key length — how many characters before clashes bite? 5 yrs of pastes ≈ 1.8e9 # base62: 62^6 ≈ 5.7e10, 62^7 ≈ 3.5e12 → 7 chars: keyspace ≫ pastes, so random keys rarely clash (still check on insert)

The sizing screams the shape of the system. The content is ~100× the size of the metadata and grows forever — it belongs in cheap, elastic object storage, never swelling your database. The metadata is tiny and heavily indexed — a natural database job. And the traffic is read-heavy by 10×, which points straight at a cache and a CDN. Two data types, two homes, one cache: that's the architecture before you've drawn a single box.

Step 3 — The core interface

POST /pastes { text, visibility, ttl } → { key, url } GET /{key} → the paste content (or 404 if missing/expired) # key = short random base62 string, generated at create time # create writes the blob to object storage + a metadata row to the DB # read is the hot path: cache → metadata → (CDN / object storage) for the bytes

Two endpoints — that's the entire product surface. The asymmetry is the whole story: POST is rare and does the heavy lifting (write a blob, write a row); GET is constant and must be as close to free as possible, which is why the read path leans on caching while the write path doesn't need to.

Step 4 — Draw the architecture

Writes fan the data into its two homes; reads come back mostly from the cache and CDN, barely touching the origin. The metadata DB and the blob store are deliberately different systems, each doing what it's best at.

1
Create (write path). The request hits the app through a load balancer. The app generates a short random key, writes the content blob to object storage, and writes a tiny metadata row (key → blob pointer, owner, expiry, visibility) to the database.
2
Return the short URL. The caller gets back https://pb.io/{key} — the only thing they need to share. Nothing about the URL hints at how many pastes exist or how to find another.
3
Read (hot path). A GET /{key} first checks the cache for the metadata; on a miss it reads the one small row from the DB and caches it. The content bytes are served from a CDN / object storage — and because content is immutable, the CDN can cache it with a very long TTL.
4
Check expiry & access. If the paste is past expires_at, return 404 and lazily delete it. If it's private or password-protected, enforce that before serving the bytes.
5
Reclaim space. A background sweeper periodically deletes expired pastes' blobs and rows so storage doesn't grow without bound — the janitor clearing out old lockers.

The Heart: Metadata vs Blob (and the Key)

Two decisions make or break a pastebin, and both are about where things go. First, how you generate the key. Second — and this is the one that separates a toy from a real system — keeping the small metadata and the large content in separate stores.

Generating the key — four approaches

StrategyHow it makes a keyVerdict
Auto-increment IDUse the database's sequential primary key: 1, 2, 3, … (optionally base62-encoded). ❌ Enumerable. Anyone can read every paste by counting. A security hole and an information leak (reveals your total count).
Random base62 keyGenerate N random characters from [a-zA-Z0-9] at create time. ✅ The default. Unguessable and short. Needs a uniqueness check on insert (rare clash → retry) via a DB unique constraint.
Hash of contentKey = first N chars of hash(content).✅ for dedup. Identical pastes collapse to one blob automatically. But it's deterministic — two people pasting the same text share a URL, which may leak that a paste exists.
Pre-generated keys (KGS)A Key Generation Service makes random unique keys offline and hands them out from a pool.✅ at huge scale. No collision check on the hot path — the key is already known-unique. The same trick used by big URL shorteners (Day 38).

For almost everyone, a random base62 key with a uniqueness check is right. Why not auto-increment? Because a predictable key isn't a key at all — it's an invitation to walk your whole dataset, exactly Sofia's Tuesday. The unguessable key is doing double duty here: it's both the address of a paste and the access control for an "unlisted" one — you can't read what you can't guess.

The split that keeps the database fast

Here's the move that matters most. A naïve pastebin shoves the whole paste text into a column in the metadata database. It works for a week, then the table bloats with megabytes of opaque text, backups slow to a crawl, the cache of "hot rows" the DB keeps in memory gets polluted with giant blobs, and every read drags the full content through a system optimized for small, structured, indexed rows. The fix is to recognize that you have two completely different kinds of data:

MetadataContent blob
Size~100 bytesKB to MB (100× bigger, and it's most of your bytes)
ShapeStructured: key, owner, timestamps, expiry, visibility, blob pointerOpaque bytes — never queried by its contents
AccessLooked up, filtered, indexed, checked for expiryFetched whole by key; never searched or partially read
Right homeDatabase (SQL or a KV store) — fast, indexed, cheap for small rowsObject storage (S3/GCS) — built for large blobs, cheap per GB, served via CDN

The metadata row holds a pointer (the object-storage key) to where the content lives. The database stays tiny and quick no matter how big the pastes get; the blobs live in a store designed for exactly that, and can be fronted by a CDN for free, fast reads. This "small structured record points at a big opaque blob" pattern is everywhere in system design — user profiles pointing at profile photos, messages pointing at attachments, products pointing at images. Learn it here and you'll reuse it for the rest of the course.

✔ Split tiny metadata (in a DB) from the large content blob (in object storage), joined by a pointer — the DB stays small, fast, and cheap to back up; the blobs live where blobs belong and ride a CDN. Generate a random, unguessable key.
✘ Don't store big pastes in a column of your metadata database, and never use auto-increment IDs as the public key — the first bloats and slows the DB; the second lets anyone enumerate every paste you have.

Scaling the Reads — Immutable Content Is a Cache's Dream

A pastebin is read-heavy by an order of magnitude, and a handful of pastes (the one in the viral Slack thread, the popular tutorial snippet) soak up most of the reads while the long tail is read once and forgotten. That's a Zipf distribution — exactly the shape caching loves. And there's a gift that makes caching here even easier than usual: paste content is immutable. Once written, a paste never changes, so a cached copy can never be stale. You get to skip the single hardest part of caching — invalidation (Day 22) — entirely.

Three layers of read acceleration

CDN for the content. The blob bytes are static and immutable, so a CDN (Day 23) can cache them at the edge, close to readers, with a very long TTL. A popular paste gets served from a city near the reader without the origin ever being touched — fast for them, nearly free for you. Redis for the metadata. The small metadata row (is it expired? private? where's the blob?) is looked up on every read; cache it in Redis (Day 40) so the hot path rarely hits the database. The database last. Only cold reads — a paste's first view, or one that aged out of cache — fall through to the metadata DB, which then fetches the blob.

Why immutability changes everything

In most systems, caching is a constant battle against staleness: the moment data changes, every cached copy is wrong, and you need an invalidation strategy (TTLs, write-through, explicit purges). A pastebin sidesteps all of it. Because the content for a given key is fixed forever, you can set Cache-Control headers to cache the bytes for a year, let the CDN keep them as long as it likes, and never worry about a reader getting an old version — there is no old version. The only cache subtlety is expiry: when a paste's TTL passes it should stop resolving, which you handle at the metadata layer (short-TTL the metadata, or check expires_at on the lookup) rather than by invalidating the immutable content.

✔ Cache the immutable content aggressively — CDN for the bytes, Redis for the metadata, long TTLs — read-heavy + never-changing content is the ideal caching case, and it lets you serve most reads without touching the origin.
✘ Don't serve every read from origin object storage, and don't over-think invalidation — immutable content never goes stale, so the hard part of caching simply isn't here; the one thing to honor is expiry, handled at the metadata layer.

The Hard Parts

Create-and-read is trivial. These four edges are what separate a weekend hack from a pastebin that survives real traffic, real abuse, and real storage bills.

Expiry — lazy delete plus a sweeper

Pastes that live forever make storage grow without bound, so most pastebins support a TTL (10 minutes, a day, a week, never). Storing expires_at is easy; actually reclaiming the space is the subtle part. The standard pattern is two-pronged: lazy delete on read (if someone fetches an expired paste, return 404 and delete it then) plus a background sweeper that periodically scans for expired rows and deletes their blobs — because a paste that's never read again would otherwise never get cleaned up. It's the janitor walking the locker wall. This is the same TTL instinct you've met in caches (Day 22) and the rate limiter (Day 42), applied to durable storage.

Access control — the unguessable link is the lock

Most pastebins offer three visibilities. Public pastes are listed and searchable. Unlisted — the common default — aren't listed anywhere; the only way to reach one is the link, and its security rests entirely on the key being unguessable (this is why random keys matter so much, and why a short 4-character key is risky — it's brute-forceable). Private pastes require being the owner or knowing a password. For genuinely sensitive content, some tools (PrivateBin) go further and encrypt the paste in the browser so the server never even sees the plaintext — the decryption key lives in the URL fragment after the #, which browsers never send to the server.

Abuse — you're hosting other people's content

The moment you let anonymous strangers store arbitrary bytes and share links, you've built a tool people will abuse: malware payloads, phishing pages, leaked credentials, spam. Real pastebins need rate limiting (Day 42, to stop automated flooding), content scanning and abuse-reporting, and a takedown process. It's the unglamorous but unavoidable cost of being a public content host — the system design isn't just boxes and arrows, it's also "what happens when someone uses this for evil?"

Big pastes & deduplication

What's the maximum paste size? A cap (say a few MB) keeps the system sane; truly large uploads might stream directly to object storage rather than through your app servers. And if you key by content hash, identical pastes automatically deduplicate — a million people pasting the same viral snippet store exactly one blob — at the cost of making URLs deterministic (which can leak that a given paste exists). Whether that trade is worth it depends on whether you value storage savings or unguessability more.

The through-line: create → store → read is easy; doing it with an unguessable key, the metadata/blob split, immutable-content caching, honest expiry, and real abuse handling is the craft. Get those right and two endpoints become a product used by millions.

Real-World Engineering

📋 Pastebin.com

The original, serving billions of pastes since 2008. Short random keys, public/unlisted/private visibility, and configurable expiry (10 minutes to never) — the canonical feature set this whole lesson reconstructs, built around short-key lookup and heavy read caching.

🐙 GitHub Gist

A pastebin with git underneath: every gist is a tiny repository, so you get version history, comments, and forking for free. It shows the metadata/content split taken to an extreme — the content lives in git objects, the metadata (visibility, owner) in GitHub's databases.

🔐 PrivateBin

A privacy-first pastebin that encrypts content in the browser before it's ever sent — the server stores only ciphertext and never holds the key, which travels in the URL fragment after # (never sent to the server). The purest expression of "unguessable link = the lock."

☁️ S3 + CloudFront

The textbook production stack: put the immutable blobs in S3 (cheap, durable object storage), front them with CloudFront (a CDN) using long cache headers, and keep metadata in DynamoDB or Postgres. Most of your reads never touch your servers at all.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: In one sentence, what is a pastebin?
A: A write-once, read-many store for text that maps a short unguessable key to an immutable content blob — the content lives in object storage, a tiny metadata row in a database points at it, and reads are served read-heavy through a cache/CDN.
Q: Why is an auto-increment integer a bad choice for a paste's public key?
A: It's enumerable — anyone can read every paste (including private ones) by counting 1, 2, 3…, and it leaks your total count. Use a short random base62 key that's unguessable; it also acts as the access control for unlisted pastes.
Q: What is the metadata/blob split and why does it matter?
A: Store the large content blob in object storage (built for cheap big blobs) and keep only a tiny metadata row (key, expiry, visibility, and a pointer to the blob) in the database. The DB stays small and fast; the blobs ride a CDN. Shoving big text into a DB column bloats it and slows every read and backup.
Q: Why is a pastebin unusually easy to cache?
A: Paste content is immutable — once written it never changes — so a cached copy can never be stale. You skip cache invalidation (the usual hard part) entirely and can cache the bytes on a CDN for a year. The only subtlety is honoring expiry, handled at the metadata layer.
Q: How do you handle paste expiry properly?
A: Store expires_at, then reclaim space two ways: lazy-delete on read (expired fetch → 404 + delete) and a background sweeper that scans for and deletes expired pastes that are never read again. Setting the timestamp alone doesn't free the storage.

If you only remember one thing: a pastebin is an unguessable short key mapping to an immutable blob in object storage, with tiny metadata in a database, served read-heavy through a cache. It's the write-heavy twin of the URL shortener — same short-key idea, but now you're storing a big thing, which forces two moves: put the content blob in object storage (never in a database column) with only a tiny metadata row pointing at it, and serve the brutally read-heavy traffic from a CDN and cache — easy, because immutable content never goes stale. Generate a random, unguessable key (auto-increment IDs let anyone enumerate every paste), expire old pastes with lazy-delete plus a sweeper, and gate access with unlisted links, private pastes, and passwords. Two endpoints, one reusable pattern — small record points at big blob — that you'll meet again and again.
