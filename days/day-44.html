Design a Type-Ahead Search
The Box That Finishes Your Sentence

You start typing cho into a search box and a little dropdown instantly offers chocolate cake, chocolate chip cookies, chicken curry — ranked by what people actually search. It feels like magic, and it fires on every single keystroke, for millions of people at once, in under a tenth of a second. Yesterday's pastebin handed back one exact paste for an exact key; today we flip the lookup around: given just the first few letters, return the best completions. The naïve answer — run a database query on every keystroke — melts under its own traffic, so you'll meet the data structure built for exactly this, the trie, and the single move that makes it instant at scale: precomputing the top completions at every node. Then the twist nobody expects — rankings change all the time, so you'll decouple serving from building, aggregate popularity offline, and hot-swap a fresh trie, accepting that suggestions can lag by an hour. You'll type into a live in-memory trie and watch it crush a naïve scan, then run a real debounce + prefix-cache simulator and see how few backend requests you truly need.

Picture this

Picture a tree made of letters, growing sideways. At the very base is an empty trunk. From it sprout branches, one per letter: a branch for a, a branch for b, a branch for c. Walk out along the c branch and it forks again — c→a, c→h, c→o. Keep walking the c→h fork and you're spelling out words one letter at a time: c-h-o-c-o-l-a-t-e. Every word your users have ever searched is a path from the trunk out to a leaf. To find all the words that start with ch, you don't search the whole forest — you just walk two steps, to the c→h fork, and everything beyond it begins with ch. That tree-of-letters is a trie.

Now add the trick that makes it fast. Nailed to every fork is a little leaderboard sign: the top handful of complete words down that branch, ranked by how often people search them. Stand at the c→h fork and the sign already reads "1. chocolate cake · 2. chocolate chip cookies · 3. chicken curry." You didn't go count every leaf out past the fork — someone wrote the leaderboard ahead of time, and you just read it. That's the whole secret of type-ahead: a query isn't a search, it's a short walk to a fork plus a glance at a pre-written sign.

Two last touches complete the picture. The leaderboards don't write themselves — a nighttime groundskeeper tallies up the day's searches and repaints every sign so the rankings stay current (that's the offline job that rebuilds the trie). And because a sign is only repainted now and then, a brand-new trending word might take a little while to climb onto it — nobody minds; the signs are allowed to be slightly out of date. Hold the image: a sideways tree of letters (the trie), a leaderboard nailed to every fork (precomputed top-k completions), a reader who just walks a few steps and reads the sign (an O(prefix-length) query), and a groundskeeper repainting the signs each night (the offline rebuild). That picture is a type-ahead search — and the rest of today is making that tree work for millions of words and a flood of keystrokes a second.

The Story

Lena was building the search box for a recipe site, and she wanted it to feel alive — suggestions appearing the instant you typed. The first version was the obvious one. On every keystroke, the frontend hit the backend, which ran a database query: SELECT title FROM recipes WHERE title LIKE 'cho%' ORDER BY searches DESC LIMIT 10. With a few thousand recipes it was flawless — you'd type c, ch, cho and the dropdown kept pace. She shipped it, proud.

Then the site got popular, and the database started groaning. Lena did the arithmetic and went pale. A single person typing the word chocolate fired nine queries — one per letter. With tens of thousands of people searching at once, each hammering out a word letter by letter, the box was generating an order of magnitude more database queries than every other feature of the site combined. The LIKE 'cho%' query could at least use an index (a B-tree range scan on the title column, from Day 11), but it didn't matter — the problem wasn't any one query being slow, it was the sheer number of them. The search box was a QPS firehose pointed straight at her database.

She needed prefix lookups that never touched the database. A colleague drew her a trie — a tree of letters where each path spells a word — and loaded every recipe title into it, in memory. Now cho was just three pointer-hops down the tree; the completions hung right there as the sub-tree below the cho node. No database, no range scan, pure RAM. It was fast — until someone typed a single letter like c, and the server had to walk thousands of descendants under that node, collect them all, and sort them by popularity, on every keystroke. The trie had fixed the database problem and handed her a CPU problem.

The fix was the move that turns a clever data structure into a real system: precompute the answer. Instead of collecting-and-sorting on every query, Lena walked the trie once, offline, and at every node stored the top-k completions for that prefix, already ranked. Now a query was devastatingly simple: walk to the prefix node (a handful of hops) and read its cached list — no scan, no sort, O(length of what you typed). The last question was how the rankings stayed fresh, since what's popular changes hourly. She refused to rebuild the trie on every search. Instead she decoupled: the live service only ever served from the trie, while a separate offline job collected the query logs, aggregated popularity, rebuilt a fresh trie, and hot-swapped it in every so often. Suggestions could lag reality by an hour — and nobody cared. Without meaning to, Lena had rebuilt the architecture behind Google's search box and Elasticsearch's completion suggester: a trie with precomputed top-k, served from memory, rebuilt offline.

What should stay in your head
Type-ahead search answers one question, billions of times a day: given a prefix, return the top-k completions, ranked by popularity, in under ~100 ms — and it fires on every keystroke, so the real enemy is request volume, not any single slow query. Three decisions carry the design. (1) The data structure: a trie (prefix tree) held in memory — a word is a path of letters, so finding everything that starts with a prefix is an O(prefix-length) walk, not a database scan. (2) The precompute: store the top-k completions at every node, already ranked, so a query is a walk plus a read — never a collect-and-sort of a hot prefix's millions of descendants. (3) The update path: popularity changes constantly, so decouple serving from building — the live service only serves from the trie, while an offline job aggregates query logs, rebuilds the trie, and hot-swaps it periodically. Suggestions are eventually consistent, and an hour of lag is fine. Everything else is leverage on request volume: debounce on the client (don't fire mid-burst), cache popular prefixes at the edge, and shard the trie by prefix to scale out. One-line test: type-ahead is a prefix walk on an in-memory trie whose every node caches its top-k, served read-heavy and rebuilt offline.

Why This Problem Exists

Humans are slow, imprecise typists, and they don't know exactly what they want to type until they see it. Type-ahead — also called autocomplete, typeahead, or search suggestions — bridges that gap: it reads your mind a few letters at a time, surfacing the most likely completions so you can pick one instead of finishing the word. It reduces typing, fixes spelling, guides people toward queries that actually return results, and makes a product feel fast and intelligent. The search box on Google, the product bar on Amazon, the @-mention menu in Slack, the destination field in a maps app — they're all the same feature.

It looks trivial — "just find words that start with what they typed" — and the naïve version really is a one-liner: a database LIKE 'prefix%' query. The reason type-ahead is a genuine system-design problem is the brutal combination of three constraints that collide. It fires on every keystroke, so one user typing one word is many requests, and the whole system sees keystrokes per second, not searches per second — a volume an order of magnitude beyond normal search. It must feel instant — below ~100 ms, or the suggestions arrive after you've already moved on, which is useless. And it must be ranked — not any ten matches, but the best ten, by popularity (and sometimes personalization), because a dropdown only shows a handful. Fast and ranked and at keystroke volume is what rules out the database query and forces the trie-with-precomputed-top-k design.

The naïve version vs. the shape we're building toward
# ❌ the naïve version — one DB query per keystroke def suggest(prefix): # called on EVERY keystroke, by everyone, at once return db.query( "SELECT title FROM items WHERE title LIKE ? ORDER BY searches DESC LIMIT 10", prefix + "%") # range-scans + sorts, on a firehose of requests → DB melts # ✅ the shape we want — a walk + a read, from memory, no DB on the hot path def suggest(prefix): node = trie.walk(prefix) # O(len(prefix)) pointer hops, in RAM if node is None: return [] # no words start with this prefix return node.top_k # PRECOMPUTED, already ranked — just read it

The Design, Step by Step

Same discipline as the last six designs: scope it, size it, sketch the interface, then draw the boxes. The thing to hold onto today is that there are really two systems glued together — a fast online path that serves suggestions from memory, and a slow offline path that builds the thing it serves from. Almost every decision is about keeping those two apart.

Step 1 — Scope the requirements

✅ Functional

Given a prefix, return the top-k completions (say 5–10).

Completions ranked by popularity (what people actually search).

Suggestions update over time as search trends shift.

🎯 Non-functional

Fast — well under ~100 ms, or it's useless.

Huge read volume — fires per keystroke; built for QPS.

Highly available — the box can't hang the whole page.

🤔 Clarify early

Prefix only, or match words in the middle too? (changes everything.)

How fresh must rankings be? (seconds, or is an hour fine?)

Personalized or one global ranking? (adds a whole dimension.)

Step 2 — Size it (back-of-the-envelope, Day 30)

Say a big search box: 5 billion searches/day, average query ~20 characters, and suppose the client debounces so you get roughly one request per ~4 characters typed rather than one per keystroke.

# request volume — this is the whole reason the design is hard searches/day = 5e9 reqs/search ≈ 20 chars / 4 per request = ~5 requests # debounced; raw per-keystroke ≈ 20 suggest reqs/day = 5e9 × 5 = 2.5e10 (25 billion/day) avg QPS = 2.5e10 / 86,400 ≈ ~290,000 / s # peak several× → ~1M/s. All READS. # storage — the trie itself is small enough to hold in memory distinct queries ≈ 1e8 (100M) worth keeping # the long tail is dropped, not stored trie size ≈ a few GB in RAM # fits on one big box; shard by prefix past that per node : children map + a cached top-k list (~k×(word+score))

The sizing screams the shape of the system. It's all reads, at a volume (hundreds of thousands to a million QPS) that no database survives if each request is a query — so the serving path must be an in-memory lookup, replicated many times for QPS and sharded when the trie outgrows one box. The data itself is modest and changes slowly, so building it is a background job, not something on the request path. Reads enormous, writes rare and offline: that's the architecture before you've drawn a box.

Step 3 — The core interface

GET /suggest?q=cho&limit=10 → [ "chocolate cake", "chocolate chip cookies", "chicken curry", … ] # q = the prefix typed so far; limit = how many completions to return # served entirely from an in-memory trie — never touches a database # cacheable at the edge (a CDN / Day 4): a given prefix returns the same list for everyone

One read endpoint — that's the entire online surface. There is no "write" endpoint a user calls: the writes to this system are the search logs flowing in the background, consumed by the offline builder. That split between a trivial read API and an invisible write pipeline is the heart of the design.

Step 4 — Draw the architecture (two lanes)

The top lane is the online serving path: a keystroke, thinned by debouncing and often answered from an edge cache, reaching an in-memory trie. The bottom lane is the offline building path: search logs aggregated into popularity counts, used to rebuild the trie and hot-swap it in. The two lanes meet only at the trie — one reads it, the other replaces it.

1
Keystroke → debounce. The browser waits for a brief pause (and often a minimum of 2–3 characters) before sending anything, so a fast burst of typing becomes one request, not one per letter — the first and cheapest cut at the firehose.
2
Edge cache / CDN. A given prefix returns the same global list for everyone, so popular prefixes (a, ch, new) are served straight from a CDN / cache (Day 4) — most requests never reach your service at all.
3
Suggestion service (in-memory trie). A cache miss hits a stateless suggestion server that holds the trie in RAM, walks the prefix, and reads the node's precomputed top-k — O(prefix length), no database. These servers are replicated for QPS and sharded by prefix when the trie is too big for one box (Day 13).
4
Logs → aggregation (offline). Every real search is logged to a stream (Kafka, Day 19). A background job aggregates popularity — counts, time-decayed so trending beats stale — over all those logs.
5
Rebuild & hot-swap. The job builds a fresh trie with updated top-k lists and swaps it into the suggestion servers (every few minutes to hourly). Suggestions are eventually consistent — a brand-new trend takes a little while to appear, which is fine.

The Heart: The Trie + Precomputed Top-K

Two ideas do almost all the work, and they stack. First, the trie makes finding the prefix fast. Second, precomputed top-k at each node makes ranking the completions free. Miss either one and type-ahead falls over.

Why a trie, and four ways to answer a prefix query

ApproachHow it answers "prefix → completions"Verdict
DB LIKE 'p%'A SQL range query per keystroke against an indexed column. ❌ Melts. Works for a toy dataset, but at keystroke volume it's a QPS firehose into your database. LIKE '%mid%' can't even use the index.
Plain trieWalk the prefix (O(len)), then DFS the sub-tree to collect all completions and sort by score. ➖ Fast walk, slow finish. Great for the prefix match, but collecting + sorting a hot prefix's millions of descendants on every keystroke burns CPU.
Trie + precomputed top-kStore the ranked top-k completions on every node; a query walks to the node and reads the list.✅ The default. A query is O(prefix length): a few hops plus one read. The sort happened once, offline. Costs a little memory per node.
Search-engine suggesterA completion suggester built on a finite-state transducer (FST) — Elasticsearch, Lucene.✅ At scale / with extras. Compact, handles weights, fuzzy typos, and middle-of-string matches. More machinery; Day 55.

For almost everyone, a trie with precomputed top-k is the answer. The plain trie is the crucial stepping stone — it's why prefix lookup is cheap — but on its own it trades a database bottleneck for a CPU one. Moving the collect-and-sort off the request path and into an offline build is the move that makes it a system: the expensive work happens once, in the background, and every one of the millions of live queries just reads the result.

What actually lives on a node

A trie node is tiny: a map from the next character to a child node, and a short, pre-ranked list of the best completions for the prefix ending at that node. That's it. The walk is pure pointer-chasing; the answer is sitting on the node you land on.

class TrieNode: children: dict[char → TrieNode] # one branch per next letter top_k: list[(word, score)] # PRECOMPUTED, already sorted desc by score def suggest(prefix): node = root for ch in prefix: # O(len(prefix)) — a few pointer hops node = node.children.get(ch) if node is None: return [] # nothing starts with this prefix return node.top_k # one read — the answer is already ranked
✔ Use a trie for the prefix walk, and precompute the ranked top-k on every node so a query is walk + read (O(prefix length)) — the expensive collect-and-sort happens once, offline, not on every keystroke.
✘ Don't run a LIKE 'prefix%' query per keystroke, and don't DFS-and-sort the sub-tree on the hot path — the first turns your database into a firehose target; the second trades that for a CPU meltdown on popular short prefixes.

Serving, Updating & Scaling

With the trie + top-k in hand, the remaining work is all about volume and freshness: serving a flood of reads, and keeping the rankings current without ever rebuilding on the request path.

Serving the flood — debounce, cache, replicate, shard

The read path is attacked in layers, cheapest first. Debounce on the client (Day's first widget below): wait for a short pause before sending, so a burst of typing is one request — this alone removes most keystroke traffic for free. Cache at the edge: a prefix maps to the same global list for everyone, so popular prefixes are served from a CDN / cache (Day 4) and never reach you. Replicate the suggestion servers: they're stateless readers of an in-memory trie, so you can run hundreds of identical copies behind a load balancer to absorb QPS. Shard by prefix when the trie outgrows one machine: terms starting a–f on one shard, g–m on another (Day 13) — a request is routed by its first letters to the right shard.

Updating the rankings — decouple, aggregate, hot-swap

Here's the part that separates a toy from a system. Rankings change constantly — a term goes viral, a product trends — but you can never rebuild or re-sort on the live request path. So you decouple: the online service only ever reads the trie; a separate offline pipeline builds it. Real searches are logged to a stream (Kafka, Day 19). A background job aggregates popularity — typically with time decay so recent searches count more than last month's — builds a fresh trie with new top-k lists, and hot-swaps it into the servers every few minutes to hourly. The cost is that suggestions are eventually consistent (Day 10): a brand-new trend takes a little while to climb onto the leaderboards. For autocomplete that's a perfectly good trade — nobody notices or minds a few minutes of lag on a suggestion.

✔ Serve reads from replicated in-memory tries behind a cache; build the trie in an offline pipeline and hot-swap it periodically — debounce + edge cache kill most traffic, replication absorbs the rest, and offline rebuilds keep rankings fresh enough.
✘ Don't rebuild, re-sort, or recompute rankings on the live request path, and don't insist suggestions be perfectly fresh — eventual consistency (an hour of lag) is the right trade for autocomplete; chasing real-time rankings buys you nothing and costs you everything.

The Hard Parts

Prefix-walk-and-read is the easy 80%. These edges are what separate a clever demo from an autocomplete that handles real users, real typos, and real scale.

Ranking is the product — and it's more than raw counts

Which completions you show is the feature, and popularity alone isn't enough. Good ranking blends frequency (how often a query is searched) with recency / time-decay (trending now beats popular last year), and often context — your location, your recent searches, the current season. Some systems add light personalization (your own history nudges your suggestions), which breaks the "same list for everyone" assumption that made edge-caching so easy — a classic tension between a better result and a cheaper system.

Typos and fuzzy matching

A pure trie is unforgiving: type choclate (missing an o) and the walk falls off a cliff — no node, no suggestions, even though you clearly meant chocolate. Real systems add fuzzy matching, tolerating an edit or two (an insertion, deletion, or swap) so near-misses still resolve. That's expensive on a plain trie but comes more naturally to an FST-based suggester (Elasticsearch, Day 55), which is a big reason teams reach for one as requirements grow.

Memory footprint & the long tail

A trie of every query ever typed would be enormous, and most of it is a long tail searched once and never again — pure noise. You prune: keep only queries above a frequency threshold, drop the rest. Storing a full top-k list on every node also multiplies memory, so implementations compress — share lists, store only at branch points, or use a compact FST. The guiding instinct: a suggestion that almost nobody wants isn't worth a byte of RAM on the hot path.

Multi-word and middle-of-string matching

A plain prefix trie only completes from the start: type cake and it will never suggest chocolate cake, because cake isn't a prefix of it. If you need to match words in the middle, you index every suffix (or every word) as its own entry, or move to an inverted-index / search-engine suggester. It's the same prefix-vs-substring gap Lena hit with LIKE 'p%' (indexable) versus LIKE '%p%' (not) — the data structure has to match the kind of matching you promise.

The through-line: walk → read top-k is easy; doing it with good ranking, typo tolerance, a bounded memory footprint, and an offline rebuild that keeps it fresh is the craft. Get those right and one read endpoint becomes a feature that quietly shapes how billions of people search.

Real-World Engineering

🔎 Google Autocomplete

The canonical example: predictions appear as you type, ranked by how often queries are searched, time-decayed so trending terms surface, and shaded by language, location, and (optionally) your own history. The entire feature is a ranked prefix lookup served in tens of milliseconds, globally.

🟣 Elasticsearch Completion Suggester

A production-grade suggester built on a finite-state transducer (FST) — a compressed cousin of a trie — held in memory for speed. It supports weights (popularity) and fuzzy matching for typos out of the box, which is why teams reach for it as autocomplete requirements grow (Day 55).

🧱 Redis Sorted Sets

A lightweight, popular build-it-yourself approach: for each prefix, keep a sorted set (ZADD/ZREVRANGE) of completions scored by popularity, so the top-k is a single range read. It's the "precomputed top-k per prefix" idea mapped straight onto a data store you already run (Day 40).

⚙️ The offline log pipeline

Behind every one of these sits the unglamorous half: search logs streamed through Kafka (Day 19), aggregated into time-decayed popularity counts by a batch or stream job, used to rebuild the trie/FST/sorted-sets and hot-swap them in. The serving layer is only as good as the pipeline that feeds it.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: In one sentence, what is a type-ahead search?
A: Given a prefix, return the top-k completions ranked by popularity in under ~100 ms, on every keystroke — served by walking an in-memory trie whose every node caches its precomputed top-k, with the trie rebuilt offline.
Q: Why can't you just run a LIKE 'prefix%' database query per keystroke?

Without precomputed top-k, answering requires DFS-collecting and sorting the sub-tree per keystroke — a CPU meltdown on short prefixes. Storing the ranked top-k on every node turns a query into a walk + a single read.
Q: How do you keep rankings fresh without rebuilding on the request path?
A: Decouple serving from building. The live service only reads the trie; an offline pipeline aggregates search logs (time-decayed popularity), rebuilds a fresh trie, and hot-swaps it in periodically. Suggestions become eventually consistent — an hour of lag is an acceptable trade.
Q: Name two cheap ways to cut the keystroke request volume before it reaches your service.
A: Debounce on the client (wait for a typing pause, often requiring 2–3 chars, so a burst becomes one request) and cache popular prefixes at the edge/CDN (a prefix returns the same global list for everyone, so hot prefixes never reach the origin).

If you only remember one thing: type-ahead is a prefix walk on an in-memory trie whose every node caches its top-k, served read-heavy and rebuilt offline. It fires on every keystroke, so the enemy is request volume — which rules out a per-keystroke database query no matter how well indexed. A trie makes finding the prefix O(length); precomputing the ranked top-k on each node makes returning the best completions a single read, by moving the collect-and-sort off the request path and into an offline build. Keep rankings fresh by decoupling — aggregate query logs, rebuild the trie, hot-swap it, and accept eventually-consistent suggestions — and tame the firehose with debounce, edge caching, replication, and sharding by prefix.
