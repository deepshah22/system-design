Design a Social Media Feed
The Timeline That Builds Itself

You open the app and — instantly — a ranked stream of posts from the hundreds of people you follow is already there, scrolled and ready, for a billion people at once. Yesterday type-ahead returned the best completions of a word; today we assemble, for every single person, the best posts. The obvious design — when you open your feed, go ask everyone you follow for their latest posts and merge them — collapses the instant you follow a few hundred accounts. The clever design — when you post, immediately copy it into every follower's ready-made feed — collapses the instant a celebrity with 50 million followers hits send. So the entire system turns on one decision: fan-out on write (push) versus fan-out on read (pull) — and the answer every real feed lands on is the hybrid: push for normal accounts, pull for the handful of celebrities. You'll run a live cost explorer and watch a celebrity's single post explode into millions of writes under push, then watch a busy follower's feed-open explode into hundreds of reads under pull — then see the hybrid defuse both. Finally you'll assemble a real feed step by step: read your push inbox, pull the celebrities, merge, rank, and render.

Picture this

Picture a giant mailroom. Along one wall is a row of mailboxes, one per person — and your mailbox is your feed. When your friend posts a photo, a mailroom clerk grabs a stack of copies and walks the wall, dropping one copy into the mailbox of every single person who follows your friend. By the time you walk up and open your box, the photo is already sitting there, pre-sorted, waiting. Reading your feed is just opening one box — no running around, no asking anyone. That pre-delivery is fan-out on write, the push model: do the work when the post is made, so reading is instant.

Now a celebrity with fifty million followers posts a single selfie. The clerk picks up the stack… and has to make fifty million trips down the wall for that one post. The mailroom seizes. That's the fan-out storm — the one scenario that breaks pure push. So for the famous, the clerk gives up on pre-delivery and instead pins their posts to a public noticeboard by the door. Nothing gets copied into anyone's box. Instead, when you come to read your feed, you open your own box (the normal folks, already delivered) and take a two-second detour past the noticeboards of the handful of celebrities you follow, grabbing their latest. Gathering posts at read time is fan-out on read, the pull model.

Hold the whole picture: a wall of mailboxes (each person's precomputed feed), a clerk pre-delivering normal posts into every follower's box (push — cheap reads, expensive writes), a noticeboard for celebrities whose posts are grabbed only when you show up (pull — cheap writes, expensive reads), and you, at read time, opening your box and detouring past a few noticeboards, then merging the two piles into one ranked stack (the hybrid). That mailroom is a social media feed — and the rest of today is deciding, post by post, which deliveries happen when.

The mailroom: normal posts are pre-delivered into every follower's box (push); celebrity posts wait on a noticeboard to be grabbed at read time (pull).

The Story

Maya was building the home feed for a new social app. The first version was the obvious one: when you open the app, find everyone you follow, fetch their recent posts, merge them, sort by time, and show the result. With a few hundred beta users each following a dozen friends, it was flawless — a feed appeared instantly. She shipped it, proud.

Then people started following things. A power user followed 800 accounts. Now every time she opened the app, the server ran a query against 800 different authors, pulled their latest posts, merged hundreds of candidates, and sorted them — on every single pull-to-refresh. Multiply that by millions of opens an hour and Maya's database was doing fan-in work on a scale that dwarfed everything else. Reading a feed had become the most expensive operation in the system. This was fan-out on read (pull), and it was melting under read volume.

A mentor flipped it around. "Stop doing the work when people read. Do it when they post." So Maya rebuilt it: when you publish a post, a fan-out service looks up all your followers and writes the post's ID into each of their feeds — a precomputed list (in Redis) she called an inbox. Now reading a feed was devastatingly simple: open your own inbox, read the top IDs, hydrate the content, done. One list lookup, no matter how many people you follow. Reads went instant. This was fan-out on write (push), and it felt like magic — until the day a musician with 20 million followers posted.

That one post turned into 20 million inserts. The fan-out service fell hours behind; some followers saw the post at noon, some at dinner. Worse, most of those 20 million people wouldn't open the app that day — Maya had done millions of writes nobody would read. The fan-out storm had arrived, and it's the thing pure push can never survive. The fix wasn't to pick push or pull — it was to use both. For normal accounts, keep pushing: pre-deliver into follower inboxes, cheap reads. For the tiny set of celebrity accounts, stop pushing entirely — store their posts once and pull them at read time, merging them into each viewer's feed on the fly. Push the many, pull the few, merge at read. Without meaning to, Maya had rebuilt the exact architecture behind Twitter's timeline and Instagram's feed: a hybrid fan-out, precomputed inboxes for the masses and read-time merges for the famous.

What should stay in your head
A social feed answers one question, billions of times a day: for this specific person, what are the best recent posts from the accounts they follow — assembled in under a second? The whole design turns on when you do the work of gathering posts. Fan-out on write (push): when someone posts, copy its ID into every follower's precomputed feed (an "inbox", usually a Redis list) — reads become a single list lookup, but a post by someone with N followers costs N writes, which explodes for celebrities (the fan-out storm). Fan-out on read (pull): store each post once; at read time, query every account you follow, merge and rank — writes are trivial, but a feed-open for someone following M accounts costs M reads plus a merge, which explodes for active users. Neither pure model survives, so real systems use the hybrid: push for normal accounts (cheap reads for the common case) and pull for the handful of celebrities each person follows (no write storms), merged at read time. On top of that sit three more decisions: the feed is a list of IDs, hydrated with content separately (so you store IDs, not duplicated posts); ranking turns "newest first" into "best first" (an ML relevance score); and the feed is eventually consistent — a post taking seconds to appear everywhere is fine. One-line test: a feed is a per-person precomputed inbox, filled by pushing normal posts and pulling celebrity posts, merged and ranked at read time.

Why This Problem Exists

Every other feature on a social app is small compared to the feed. A profile page loads one person's posts. A notification is one event. But the home feed is the opposite shape: it gathers posts from everyone you follow, ranks them, and does it on every app open, for everyone, constantly. The read-to-write ratio is extreme — people scroll their feed hundreds of times for every post they make — so the feed is the most read-heavy surface in the whole product.

That shape creates a brutal asymmetry you can't optimize your way out of with a single trick:

📥 Reads are constant & huge

Hundreds of millions of people open the app dozens of times a day. If building a feed is expensive at read time, you pay that cost billions of times a day.

✍️ Writes are spiky & uneven

Most posts reach a few hundred people. A tiny number of posts — from celebrities — reach tens of millions. The follower distribution is a brutal power law.

⚖️ You must pick when to do the work

Gather posts when someone posts (push) or when someone reads (pull)? One makes reads cheap and writes explode; the other does the reverse. There's no free lunch.

🕐 Perfect freshness isn't required

Nobody notices if a post takes a few seconds — even a minute — to appear in their feed. That slack is the budget you spend to make the hard parts tractable.

So "design a feed" is really the question when do I pay the cost of assembling it, and for whom? Push pays at write time, pull pays at read time, and because the follower graph is a power law, the only answer that survives both the celebrity and the power-follower is to split the decision per account. That split is the heart of the design.

The Design, Step by Step

Let's build the hybrid feed from the outside in. Two paths matter: the write path (someone posts) and the read path (someone opens their feed). The hybrid does cheap work on each, pushing the normal case and pulling the extreme case.

The write path — when someone posts

1
Post service stores the post once in the post store (and ships any media to object storage + CDN, Day 4), returning a post_id. The post is now durable and addressable — exactly once, never copied.
2
It emits a “new post” event onto Kafka (Day 19). Posting returns to the user immediately; fan-out happens asynchronously — the author doesn't wait for millions of deliveries.
3
The fan-out service consumes the event and checks the author. Normal account? Look up its followers and push the post_id into each follower's feed inbox (a capped Redis list). Celebrity? Skip the fan-out entirely — the post stays only in the post store, to be pulled later.
4
Each inbox is capped (say the latest ~800 IDs) so it never grows unbounded — old entries fall off the end. The inbox stores IDs, not content, so it stays tiny.

The read path — when someone opens their feed

1
The timeline service reads the viewer's inbox from Redis — one list lookup returns the pushed post IDs from all the normal accounts they follow. Cheap, O(1)-ish.
2
It then pulls recent posts from the few celebrity accounts the viewer follows (their posts were never pushed) — a small, bounded fan-in because almost nobody follows thousands of celebrities.
3
Merge the pushed IDs and the pulled celebrity IDs into one candidate set, then rank — newest-first for a chronological feed, or an ML relevance score for a “For You” feed.
4
Hydrate the top ~N IDs into full posts (author, text, media URLs, like counts) from caches and the post store, and return them. Media loads from the CDN. Scroll down → fetch the next page.
The hybrid feed: posts fan out via Kafka to follower inboxes (push) — except celebrity posts, which are pulled and merged at read time.
The one invariant: the feed inbox stores post_ids, never post bodies. A post is written once to the post store; the inbox is a cheap list of pointers into it. That's what lets you push an ID into a million inboxes without duplicating a million copies of the photo — and it's why hydration (ID → content) is a separate, cacheable step.

The Heart: Push vs Pull vs Hybrid

Everything above is scaffolding around one decision. Here are the three models side by side — what each makes cheap, what each makes explode, and why production systems land on the hybrid.

Push does the work at write time (cheap reads, write storms). Pull does it at read time (cheap writes, read storms). Hybrid splits by account type.

Push — fan-out on write

When you post, write the ID into every follower's inbox. Reading a feed is then a single list lookup, no matter how many people you follow — reads are O(1) and blazingly cheap, which is exactly what you want for the most-read surface in the app. The cost: a post by someone with N followers is N writes. For normal accounts that's fine. For a celebrity it's a fan-out storm — millions of writes for one post, most of them into inboxes nobody will read that day. Push optimizes the common case (reading) and punishes the rare case (a famous person posting).

Pull — fan-out on read

Store each post once; when someone opens their feed, query all the accounts they follow, merge, and rank. Writes are O(1) and trivial — posting a celebrity selfie is just one insert. The cost lands on the read: a feed-open for someone following M accounts is M queries plus a merge, paid on every refresh. For the most-read surface in the product, that's backwards — pull optimizes the rare case (posting) and punishes the common case (reading).

Hybrid — push the many, pull the few

Use push for normal accounts (so the common read stays a cheap inbox lookup) and pull for celebrity accounts (so one post never triggers a write storm). At read time, merge the viewer's pushed inbox with a small pull of the celebrities they follow. The pull side is bounded because almost nobody follows thousands of celebrities, and the push side is bounded because normal accounts have manageable follower counts. The hybrid keeps both the write path and the read path cheap by refusing to treat a 20-million-follower account and a 300-follower account the same way.

Ranking, Freshness & Scaling

The push/pull/hybrid decision gets a feed built. Three more concerns make it good and make it scale.

Chronological vs ranked ("For You")

The simplest feed is reverse-chronological: merge candidates, sort by time, done. Modern feeds are ranked: a relevance model scores each candidate (predicted engagement, recency, affinity to the author, media type, how often you've interacted) and sorts by score. Ranking turns "newest first" into "best first" — but it adds a scoring step on the read path and makes the feed non-deterministic (two people following identical accounts see different orders). It's the single biggest lever on engagement, and the single biggest source of complexity.

Freshness is eventual, and that's the budget

Because fan-out is asynchronous (through Kafka), a post doesn't appear in every follower's feed at the same instant — it ripples out over seconds. That's eventual consistency (Day 10), and it's not a bug; it's the slack that makes async fan-out possible. Nobody refreshing their feed can tell whether a post arrived 200 ms or 3 seconds after it was published. You spend that tolerance to avoid doing fan-out synchronously on the posting user's request.

Scaling the pieces

🧱 Inboxes in Redis, sharded

Each user's feed inbox is a capped list in Redis (Day 40), sharded by user ID (Day 13). Capping to the latest ~hundreds of IDs keeps memory bounded — the feed is a sliding window, not an archive.

🌀 Fan-out workers off Kafka

Fan-out is a fleet of consumers reading the “new post” topic. They parallelize the writes and absorb spikes; a slow celebrity fan-out never blocks normal posts because celebrities are pulled, not pushed.

🗄️ Hydration cache

Turning IDs into full posts hits a cache first (hot posts live in memory); only misses touch the post store. Media is served from the CDN (Day 4), never the app servers.

📈 Estimate before you build

Back-of-envelope (Day 30): 500M daily users × ~20 opens = 10B feed reads/day ≈ 115k reads/sec average, multiples higher at peak. That read volume is why push exists.

The Hard Parts

Push-the-many-pull-the-few is the easy 80%. These edges are what separate a clever diagram from a feed that survives real users, real celebrities, and real scale.

Where exactly is the celebrity line?

The hybrid needs a threshold: above X followers, pull instead of push. Too low and you pull too many accounts (read path bloats); too high and a near-celebrity's post still causes a mini-storm. Real systems tune this (often tens of thousands to low millions of followers) and sometimes decide per follower-activity, not just per follower-count — push to your active followers, skip the dormant ones, so you don't write into millions of inboxes nobody will open.

Fan-out to inactive users is wasted work

Most followers won't open the app today. Pushing a post into tens of millions of inboxes when only a fraction get read is enormous wasted write amplification. A common refinement: don't fan-out to dormant users at all — mark accounts inactive after N days and pull their feed on the rare occasion they return, reserving push for people who actually show up.

Ranking makes the feed non-deterministic and hard to cache

A chronological feed can be heavily cached — the same inbox gives the same order. A ranked feed is personalized and time-sensitive, so the final order can't be cached as aggressively, and the scoring model becomes a service of its own on the read path. Better results, more moving parts — the recurring tension between a smarter feed and a cheaper one.

Deletes, edits, blocks, and privacy

Once you've pushed an ID into a million inboxes, what happens when the author deletes the post, gets blocked by a viewer, or the viewer unfollows? You generally don't chase down a million inboxes — you filter at hydration/read time: the inbox holds candidate IDs, and the read path drops anything deleted, blocked, or no-longer-visible before rendering. The inbox is a hint, not a promise.

The through-line: push many → pull few → merge → rank is the skeleton; doing it with a well-placed celebrity threshold, no wasted fan-out to dormant users, a ranking model, and read-time filtering for deletes and privacy is the craft. Get those right and one feed endpoint quietly shapes what billions of people see every day.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"On feed-open, just query everyone I follow and merge."That's pull, and it melts the most-read surface in the app — an active user following hundreds of accounts pays a huge fan-in on every refresh. Precompute an inbox instead.
"Push is clearly best — reads are O(1)!"Until a celebrity posts and one write becomes millions of inserts (the fan-out storm), most into inboxes nobody reads. Pure push can't survive the power-law follower graph.
"Store the full post in every follower's feed."Never duplicate content. The inbox stores post IDs; the post is written once and hydrated separately. IDs are cheap to copy a million times; photos are not.
"Fan-out should finish before the post is confirmed."No — fan-out is asynchronous (via Kafka). Posting returns immediately; delivery ripples out over seconds. The feed is eventually consistent.

Real-World Engineering

🐦 Twitter / X Timelines

The canonical hybrid. Twitter precomputes each user's home timeline by fanning out tweets on write into per-user Redis-backed timelines, but treats high-follower accounts specially — their tweets are merged in at read time rather than fanned out, precisely to avoid the fan-out storm. Their internal caching layer for this was famously built on Redis.

📸 Instagram Feed

Instagram's feed and “stories” rely on precomputed, cached timelines with a heavy ranking model layered on top — the order you see isn't chronological but scored by predicted engagement and affinity. Media is served from CDNs; the feed service deals in IDs and hydrates content separately.

👍 Facebook News Feed

Facebook leans toward pull + aggressive ranking: candidates are gathered and then scored by one of the most complex relevance models in the industry (thousands of signals), with multi-layer caching to keep the read path fast despite the ranking cost. The feed is a ranking problem as much as a fan-out problem.

🌀 The async fan-out backbone

Behind all of them sits the unglamorous half: a Kafka-style event log (Day 19) carrying “new post” events to a fleet of fan-out workers, writing capped Redis inboxes sharded by user, with eventual consistency as the accepted trade. The feed is only as good as the pipeline that fills the inboxes.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: In one sentence, how does a social media feed work at scale?
A: It's a per-person precomputed inbox of post IDs, filled by pushing normal accounts' posts to every follower and pulling celebrity posts at read time, then merged and ranked when you open the app and hydrated from a content cache/CDN.
Q: What is fan-out on write (push), and what's its failure mode?
A: When someone posts, copy the post ID into every follower's inbox, so reads are an O(1) list lookup. Failure mode: a post by an account with N followers costs N writes — for a celebrity that's a fan-out storm of millions of inserts, most into inboxes nobody reads.
Q: What is fan-out on read (pull), and what's its failure mode?
A: Store each post once; at read time, query every account the viewer follows, merge, and rank. Writes are O(1). Failure mode: a feed-open for someone following M accounts costs M reads + a merge, paid on every refresh — a read storm on the most-read surface.
Q: Why does the hybrid use push for most accounts but pull for celebrities?
A: The follower graph is a power law. Push keeps the common read cheap but explodes on high-follower posts; pull avoids write storms but is expensive per read. Hybrid pushes normal accounts (cheap reads) and pulls the few celebrities each viewer follows (no storms), merging both at read time — keeping both paths cheap.
Q: Why does the feed inbox store IDs instead of full posts, and when is content fetched?
A: Copying an ID into a million inboxes is cheap; duplicating a photo a million times is not. The post is written once to the post store; the read path hydrates the top-N IDs into full posts from a cache/CDN — which also lets you filter deleted/blocked items at read time.

If you only remember one thing: a feed is a per-person precomputed inbox, filled by pushing normal posts and pulling celebrity posts, merged and ranked at read time. The feed is the most read-heavy surface in the app, so you want reads cheap — which is why you push (fan-out on write) a post's ID into every follower's inbox and make reading a single list lookup. But the follower graph is a power law, so a celebrity's single post would explode into millions of writes — the fan-out storm — which is why you pull (fan-out on read) celebrity posts at read time instead. The hybrid keeps both paths cheap by refusing to treat a 20-million-follower account like a 300-follower one. Store IDs, not posts; fan out asynchronously through Kafka and accept eventual consistency; rank candidates for a “For You” feed; and filter deletes and blocks at read time. One inbox per person, one reusable pattern — precompute for the many, gather for the few — that powers the next lesson too.
