Observability
Learning to See Through the Fog

Yesterday's lesson ended with a warning: the worst failures are the gray ones — a service that's up for its health check but down for real users, a request that vanished, money that moved while the client thinks it failed. You cannot survive what you cannot see. Today you build the instruments that let you see. Observability is the discipline of wiring a system so you can ask any question about what's happening inside it — from the outside, after the fact, including questions you never thought to ask in advance. You'll meet the three signals — metrics, logs, and traces — and, more importantly, the three questions they answer: is it broken? where? and why? You'll see how a single trace ID stitches one user's request across twenty services into one readable waterfall, learn the four golden signals worth watching, understand why observability is not the same as monitoring, and face the real tradeoffs — cardinality, sampling, and cost — that make this genuinely hard.

Visual Memory Anchor

🏥📈📋🩻

Picture this

A patient (your system) is in the ICU, and you're the doctor trying to keep them alive. You can't open them up and watch every cell — you have to infer what's happening inside from instruments on the outside. You have exactly three. The first is the vital-signs monitor 📈 — heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen — a few numbers, updated every second, always on, that beep the moment something leaves the safe range. It's cheap and it never sleeps, and it tells you that the patient is crashing and when — but a heart rate of 160 doesn't tell you why. The second is the nurse's chart 📋 — a detailed, timestamped diary of every individual event: "14:03 gave 5mg morphine, patient winced." Rich, specific, the record of what exactly happened — but there are thousands of entries and you can't watch them all in real time. The third is the contrast dye 🩻 — you inject a tracer into one patient and watch it flow through organ after organ on the scan, lighting up the exact spot where the blood slows or stops. It follows one journey through the whole body and shows you where the problem is. No single instrument is enough: the monitor says "something's wrong," the dye says "it's the left kidney," and only then does the chart tell you "because of the drug given at 14:03." Burn in the image: metrics are the vital-signs monitor (is it broken? when?), traces are the contrast dye following one request (where?), and logs are the detailed chart (why?). You need all three — and a way to jump between them.

The Story

In-the-Dark Devi got the page at 2:14 a.m.: checkout error rate is up. That was the entire message — because that was everything her systems could tell her. Her company had grown from one app into a dozen services — gateway, orders, payments, inventory, pricing, email — and tonight, somewhere in that web, one in twenty checkouts was failing. She had no idea which service, or why.

She started the only way she could: SSHing into machines and running grep over log files, one server at a time. But there were forty servers, the logs were unstructured text her teammates had written in six different styles, and — worst of all — nothing tied them together. She could see the gateway logged "received order #8842," and she could see a payment error somewhere in the payments logs, but she couldn't prove they were the same request. It was like reading twelve people's diaries and trying to reconstruct one conversation with no names and no timestamps that lined up. Two hours in, she still didn't know where the failure lived.

Her dashboard hadn't helped either. It showed one big graph — total error rate — a single angry red line climbing. It told her that something was wrong (she already knew; the customers told her). It could not tell her which service, or which customers, or why. When she tried to ask "is it only failing for orders over $100?" the dashboard just stared back: it had never been built to answer a question nobody predicted. The aggregate had averaged away exactly the detail she needed.

The outage lasted four hours. The root cause, once found, was almost insulting: the pricing service had started returning a null tax field for one product category, and payments rejected the malformed total. A one-line fix. The next week Devi's team did the real work — the work that would have turned those four hours into four minutes. They gave every request a trace ID at the front door and passed it through every service. They made every log line structured — JSON with that trace ID attached. They broke the one giant error graph into per-service metrics. And they wired it so that clicking a spike on a graph opened the exact trace of a failing request, and clicking a slow step in that trace opened the exact log lines for it. They hadn't made their system fail less. They'd made it possible to see — and that, Devi learned, is a different and prior discipline. Its name is observability.

What should stay in your head
Observability is the property of a system that lets you understand its internal state purely from the signals it emits — enough to answer questions you did not anticipate, without shipping new code. It rests on three signals, and the clearest way to remember them is by the question each one answers: Metrics are cheap, always-on numbers aggregated over time (requests/sec, error %, p99 latency) — they tell you IS it broken, and WHEN, and they're what you alert on. Traces follow one request across every service it touches, as a waterfall of timed spans — they tell you WHERE it broke (which service, which hop ate the time). Logs are detailed, timestamped records of individual events — they tell you WHY (the exact error, the bad value). The workflow ties them together: a metric alert fires → you open the trace to localize the guilty span → you read that span's logs to find the cause. The glue that makes this possible is a single trace ID / correlation ID threaded through all three, so you can jump between them for one specific request. And the deeper point (Day 25's ghosts): observability is what turns gray, ambiguous, partial failures from unsolvable mysteries into things you can actually find. You can't build reliable systems you can't see.

The Three Signals (and the Question Each Answers)

People call these the "three pillars of observability." Don't memorize them as a trivia list — memorize them by the job each one does. They overlap in what they can show, but each has a natural home:

📈

Metrics

Is it broken? & When?

Numbers measured and aggregated over time — a counter (requests served), a gauge (current memory), a histogram (latency distribution). Tiny to store, always on, cheap to query for months. This is what dashboards and alerts are built from.

Strength: cheap, real-time, long retention, perfect for trends & alerting.
Weakness: aggregate — it averages detail away and can't hold high-cardinality labels (one number per user_id would explode). Tells you that, rarely why.
🩻

Traces

Where is it broken?

The end-to-end journey of one request across every service, drawn as a waterfall of spans (each span = one operation, with a start, a duration, and a parent). A shared trace ID stitches the hops together so you can see exactly which service ate the latency or threw the error.

Strength: pinpoints the guilty service/hop in a request that crosses many services.
Weakness: needs instrumentation in every service and context propagation on every hop; usually sampled, so a specific rare request may not be captured.
📋

Logs

Why is it broken?

Discrete, timestamped records of individual events — ideally structured (JSON with fields), not free-form prose. The richest, most specific signal: the exact exception, the bad input, the stack trace. The place you land to read the actual cause.

Strength: maximum detail per event; can carry any field, including high-cardinality ones.
Weakness: expensive and noisy at volume; hard to aggregate; useless across services without a shared correlation ID (Devi's 2 a.m. nightmare).

The one habit that upgrades all three at once: emit structured data and attach a trace/correlation ID to everything. Unstructured print("something broke") is a dead end; a structured event — { level: "error", service: "payments", trace_id: "abc-123", order_id: 8842, err: "null tax" } — can be searched, aggregated, and jumped to from a trace. Modern practice (Honeycomb, OpenTelemetry) even argues the "three pillars" framing is a little artificial: emit one wide, structured event per unit of work with dozens of fields, and you can derive metrics, filter it like logs, and connect it like a trace from the same data.

How a Trace Works — One ID to Rule Them All

Tracing is the piece most beginners have never seen, so let's make it concrete. When a request hits your front door, the first service mints a unique trace ID (say abc-123). Every service that touches this request from then on does two things: it records a span (its own little "I started at T, worked for 42 ms, here's my parent") and it passes the trace ID onward in the outgoing request's headers. Do that faithfully and you can later collect every span carrying abc-123 and lay them out on one timeline — a waterfall that shows, at a glance, where the time went and where it broke.

Two ideas make or break tracing:

Context propagation.The trace ID (and the current span ID, so children know their parent) must ride along on every hop — usually in HTTP headers like traceparent (the W3C standard). Miss it on one hop and the trace breaks in two; you get orphaned spans and a blind spot exactly where you need to see. This is why tracing is all-or-nothing across a system.
Sampling.At scale you can't keep a trace for every one of billions of requests — the storage and cost would dwarf the app itself. So you sample: keep, say, 1%. Head-based sampling decides at the start (cheap, but may miss the rare failure); tail-based sampling waits until the request finishes and keeps it if it was slow or errored (smarter, but you must buffer every trace briefly). The art is keeping the interesting traces without keeping them all.

Notice how tracing directly answers a Day-25 ghost: distributed systems have "no single stack trace." A trace is the distributed stack trace — reassembled after the fact from spans scattered across a dozen machines, held together by one ID.

The Debugging Workflow — From Alarm to Cause in Three Hops

Signals aren't valuable in isolation; they're valuable as a path. The reason to have all three — and to link them by a shared ID — is so an on-call engineer can travel this route at 2 a.m. without heroics:

1 · Detect — the metric.An alert fires because a metric crossed a threshold: p99 latency > 800 ms, or error rate > 2%. You now know that something is wrong and roughly when it started. You do not yet know where or why.
2 · Localize — the trace.You pivot from the spiking metric to example traces from that exact window (an "exemplar" links a metric bucket to a representative trace). The waterfall shows one service's span ballooning or turning red — say payments. Now you know where.
3 · Root-cause — the log.You click the guilty span and jump to the log lines that share its trace ID: { trace_id:"abc-123", err:"tax field was null for category 'gift-card'" }. Now you know why — and you can ship the one-line fix.

Metric → trace → log. That is the loop, and every piece of the tooling exists to make each hop instant. Without the shared trace ID, each hop becomes an hour of grepping (Devi's outage). With it, the whole journey is three clicks.

What to Actually Measure — Don't Drown, Watch the Vital Signs

You could emit a million metrics and still miss the fire. Two famous checklists cut through the noise by telling you which few signals actually matter. Google's SRE book calls these the Four Golden Signals — watch these on every user-facing service and you'll catch the vast majority of incidents:

⏱ Latency

How long requests take — and always the tail (p95/p99), not the average. Averages hide the slow requests that anger users.

🚦 Traffic

How much demand — requests/sec, transactions/min. Context for everything else; a latency spike means one thing at 10 rps and another at 10,000.

🔥 Errors

The rate of failing requests — 500s, timeouts, and the sneaky "successful" responses with wrong content. Often the first thing users feel.

💾 Saturation

How full your resources are — CPU, memory, queue depth. The leading indicator: saturation climbs before latency and errors do.

Two sibling checklists sharpen the aim depending on what you're watching. RED (Rate, Errors, Duration) is the per-service view — perfect for a microservice's request handling. USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) is the per-resource view — perfect for a machine, disk, or connection pool. And the numbers you promise to hit are SLOs (e.g. "99.9% of checkouts succeed in <300 ms"), measured by SLIs (the actual metric) — your metrics exist, above all, to tell you whether you're keeping those promises and how much error budget you have left.

Observability vs Monitoring — Known-Unknowns vs Unknown-Unknowns

These words get used interchangeably, but the distinction is the whole point of today. Monitoring is watching for failure modes you already predicted: you built a dashboard for CPU, an alert for disk-full, a graph for the login endpoint. It answers known-unknowns — "I know CPU might spike; is it spiking?" It's essential, but it can only ever tell you about the questions you thought to ask in advance.

Observability is the deeper capability: having emitted rich enough data that you can ask brand-new questions after the fact — questions no dashboard anticipated — and get answers, without shipping new instrumentation. It answers unknown-unknowns — "wait, is this only failing for Android users on the checkout endpoint paying with gift cards over $100?" This is the borrowed control-theory definition: a system is observable if you can infer its internal state from its external outputs. Devi's dashboard was monitoring (one graph, one predicted question). What she built afterward — high-cardinality, structured, correlated data she could slice any way — was observability.

Monitoring asks:"Is the thing I'm watching OK?" — dashboards, thresholds, predefined alerts. Great for the failure modes you've seen before.
Observability asks:"What is happening, and why — including things I never imagined?" — arbitrary slicing of rich, high-cardinality data. Essential for novel failures, which at scale are most of them.

You need both. Monitoring catches the known fires fast; observability lets you fight the fire you've never seen before — and in a distributed system, every big incident is one you've never seen before.

The Hard Parts — Why This Isn't Free

Observability sounds like a pure good — just log everything! — but it's a genuine engineering discipline precisely because the naive version bankrupts you or blinds you. Here's the honest ledger.

1. Cardinality is the enemy of metrics

A metric with a label is really one time-series per distinct label value. Add user_id as a label and a system with 10 million users spawns 10 million time-series — your metrics database falls over. This cardinality explosion is why metrics must stay low-cardinality (status code, endpoint, region — not user or request ID), and why per-user or per-request questions belong to logs and traces instead. Choosing what becomes a metric label vs a log field is a core skill.

2. You can't keep everything — sampling means blind spots

Full-fidelity logs and traces for every request can cost more than running the actual product. So you sample and you set retention limits — and every sample rate is a bet. Sample 1% and the one trace that would've explained the incident may be the 99% you threw away. Tail-based sampling helps (keep the errors and slow ones), but it adds buffering and complexity. There is no setting that is both complete and affordable.

3. The cost bill is real — and it can exceed the app

It is genuinely common for a company's observability bill (ingest + storage + a vendor like Datadog) to rival or exceed its production compute bill. Telemetry is data, data is money, and "log everything at debug forever" is how teams get a six-figure surprise. Observability is an ongoing cost/value tradeoff, not a one-time setup.

4. Correlation doesn't happen by accident

The magic trick — jump from metric to trace to log for one request — only works if a trace/correlation ID is propagated through every hop and stamped on every signal. Miss it in one service, one async queue, one background job, and the trail goes cold exactly there. Getting consistent context propagation across many teams, languages, and third-party libraries is the hardest organizational part, which is why the industry rallied around one standard — OpenTelemetry — to make instrumentation vendor-neutral and consistent.

The through-line: observability trades money and engineering discipline for the ability to answer questions you didn't plan for. Do it well and a 4-hour mystery becomes a 4-minute pivot from graph to trace to log. Do it naively — unstructured logs, no trace IDs, one giant averaged dashboard, or a firehose you can't afford — and you get Devi's 2 a.m.: drowning in data and still blind.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"We have dashboards, so we have observability."Dashboards are monitoring — answers to questions you predicted. Observability is being able to ask new questions after the fact. A wall of graphs you built last quarter won't answer "why is it only failing for gift-card orders?"
"Metrics, logs, and traces are three separate tools I can adopt one at a time and be fine."Their power is in the workflow between them (metric→trace→log), which only works if a shared trace/correlation ID links all three. Three disconnected silos give you three dead ends, not one path to root cause.
"Just log everything at debug level, forever — more data is safer."That's how you get a firehose you can't search and a bill bigger than your app. Volume without structure and sampling is noise. Log structured events, sample intelligently, set retention.
"Put user_id as a label on every metric so we can drill into any user."Cardinality explosion: one time-series per user melts your metrics database. High-cardinality fields (user, request, order ID) belong on logs and traces, not metric labels.
"The average latency is 50 ms, so we're fast."Averages hide the tail. If p99 is 3 seconds, 1 in 100 users is furious — and on a page that makes 100 calls, nearly every user hits that tail. Always watch p95/p99, not the mean.
"Health check is green, so the service is observable/healthy."A shallow /health ping is the opposite of observability — it's one predicted, aggregate signal. It's exactly what gray failures (Day 25) sail through. Trust real user error rate and tail latency.

(1) The three signals and their questions: Metrics — cheap, always-on numbers aggregated over time (error rate, p99 latency, requests/sec); they answer "is it broken, and when?" and are what alerts fire on. Traces — the end-to-end waterfall of one request across all the services it touches, built from timed spans; they answer "where is it broken?" (which service/hop). (2) The path: Detect — the metric alert already fired (error rate up), so I know that it's broken and roughly when. That's monitoring without observability.

Real-World Engineering

🔭 Google — Dapper & the Golden Signals

Google's Dapper paper (2010) invented large-scale distributed tracing — trace IDs, spans, and sampling — and seeded nearly every tracing system since (Zipkin, Jaeger). Google's SRE book gave the industry the Four Golden Signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation) and the SLI/SLO/error-budget discipline that turns metrics into promises.

🤝 OpenTelemetry (CNCF)

OpenTelemetry (OTel) is the industry's answer to vendor lock-in: one vendor-neutral standard and set of libraries for emitting metrics, logs, and traces, with context propagation baked in (the W3C traceparent header). Instrument once with OTel and ship the data to Jaeger, Prometheus, Datadog, or Honeycomb — the correlation glue, standardized.

📊 Prometheus + Grafana & Jaeger

The open-source backbone: Prometheus scrapes and stores metrics (pull-based, label-aware — and famously strict about cardinality), Grafana visualizes them, and Jaeger stores and renders trace waterfalls. Together they're the default free stack for the metric→trace→log workflow.

🍯 Honeycomb — Wide Events & High Cardinality

Honeycomb (founded by ex-Facebook Scuba engineers) championed the "observability is not three pillars" view: emit one arbitrarily-wide, structured event per request with high-cardinality fields, and you can slice by anything — user, build, feature flag — to catch the unknown-unknowns metrics can't. It reframed the field around asking novel questions, not watching fixed dashboards.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What is observability, and how does it differ from monitoring?
A: Observability is the ability to understand a system's internal state from the signals it emits — enough to answer questions you didn't anticipate, without shipping new code (unknown-unknowns). Monitoring is watching predefined dashboards/alerts for failure modes you predicted (known-unknowns). You need both; observability is what saves you in a novel incident.
Q: Name the three signals and the single question each one answers best.
A: Metrics → "is it broken, and when?" (cheap, always-on, aggregate numbers you alert on). Traces → "where is it broken?" (one request's waterfall of spans across services). Logs → "why is it broken?" (detailed, structured, per-event records).
Q: What ties metrics, logs, and traces together, and why does it matter?
A: A shared trace ID / correlation ID, minted at the front door and propagated through every hop and stamped on every signal. It's the glue that lets you pivot metric → trace → log for one specific request. Without it, the three signals are disconnected dead ends.
Q: What is a distributed trace made of, and what two things must every service do?
A: A trace is a tree of spans — each span is one operation with a start, duration, and parent — sharing one trace ID. Every service must (1) record its own span and (2) propagate the trace/span context onward (e.g. the traceparent header). Miss propagation on one hop and the trace breaks.
Q: Why can't you just add user_id as a label on your metrics?
A: Cardinality explosion. A metric label creates one time-series per distinct value; a per-user label with millions of users creates millions of series and overwhelms the metrics store. Keep metric labels low-cardinality (status, endpoint, region); put high-cardinality fields (user, request, order ID) on logs and traces instead.

If you only remember one thing: you cannot fix what you cannot see — observability is the discipline of emitting enough signal to answer questions you never planned for. It stands on three signals, best remembered by the question each answers: metrics tell you is it broken, and when (cheap, always-on, what you alert on); traces follow one request across every service to tell you where; and logs give the detailed record of why. Their real power is the workflow between them — metric → trace → log — made possible only by a shared trace/correlation ID threaded through every hop. Watch the vital signs (latency, traffic, errors, saturation), always at the tail (p99, not the average); keep metrics low-cardinality and push the rich detail to structured logs and traces; and respect the real costs — cardinality, sampling, and a telemetry bill that can rival your app. Do it well and yesterday's gray, ambiguous, four-hour mysteries become four-minute pivots from a graph to a trace to a log.
