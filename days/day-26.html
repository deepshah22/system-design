Observability
Seeing Through the Fog

Yesterday ended on a promise. We spent Day 25 staring at the humbling ways big systems break — partial failures, ambiguous timeouts, and worst of all the gray failure: a service that passes every health check while its real users quietly suffer. The lesson closed with a warning that has haunted on-call engineers for decades: you cannot survive what you cannot see. Today we build the instruments. Observability is your ability to understand what's happening inside a running system from the outside — not just to answer the questions you thought to ask in advance, but to ask brand-new questions at 3am when something you never imagined is on fire. It stands on three pillars: metrics (numbers over time — something is wrong, and roughly when), logs (timestamped events with detail — what exactly happened), and traces (the journey of one single request across every service it touched — where the time went). Today you'll learn how each pillar works, why they're complementary rather than interchangeable, the difference between monitoring and observability, Google's Four Golden Signals, how distributed tracing actually stitches a request together, why you must watch the p99 and never the average, and the tradeoffs of cost, cardinality, and sampling that decide how much you can see.

Picture this

Picture a patient wheeled into a hospital emergency room. Nobody knows yet what's wrong. Three different instruments go to work at once. First, a vital-signs monitor starts beeping beside the bed — heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen — a handful of numbers that update every second. When one crosses a red line, an alarm shrieks. That monitor is your metrics: it can't tell you why anything is wrong, but it screams the instant something is, and it shows you the trend that led there. Second, the nurses keep a chart — timestamped notes: "14:03 administered epinephrine · 14:05 patient coughed · 14:07 rash appeared on left arm." Discrete, detailed, specific events. That chart is your logs: rich detail about exactly what happened at each moment. Third, to actually diagnose a hard case, a good doctor traces the patient's whole journey through the hospital — admitted to the ER at 14:00, sent to X-ray at 14:12 (waited 20 minutes for a free machine), to the lab at 14:40, to surgery at 15:10 — and instantly sees that the 20-minute wait at X-ray is where all the time went. That journey is a trace: it follows one patient across every department and shows you exactly where the delay lives. Here's the magic: with these three instruments, a doctor can diagnose a patient they've never met, suffering a condition they didn't anticipate. Burn in the image: metrics are the beeping monitor (something's wrong, and when), logs are the chart notes (what exactly happened), and traces are the patient's path through the hospital (where the time went). Observability is having all three — so you can diagnose a failure you never saw coming.

The Story

Guessing Grace was on call the night everything went wrong. At 2:58am her phone lit up — not with an alert from her own systems, but with an angry message from the CEO: "Checkout is broken. Customers are tweeting. Fix it." That was the first humiliation: the users noticed before she did. Her company ran twelve microservices, and Grace had almost no way to see inside them.

So she did what she always did: she SSH'd into a box and started running grep against log files. But which box? The checkout flow touched the web tier, the API gateway, the cart service, payments, inventory, pricing, and a fraud check — each running on several machines, each writing its own logs in its own format. She found errors everywhere and errors nowhere. A payment log said "downstream timeout". A cart log said "slow response from upstream". Nothing tied a single customer's broken checkout to the exact chain of calls it made. She was reading twelve separate diaries and trying to reconstruct one conversation. Two hours later, exhausted, she rolled back the last three deploys at random. The pain stopped. She never learned why.

The following week, chastened, Grace built the three pillars. First, metrics: every service now emitted request rate, error rate, and latency, scraped every 15 seconds into a time-series database and drawn on a dashboard. Second, structured logs: every log line became JSON with a shared trace_id field. Third — the piece that changed everything — distributed tracing: when a request entered the API gateway it was stamped with a unique trace_id, and that ID was passed along in a header to every downstream call, so the whole journey could be stitched back together.

The next incident arrived at 2:47pm on a Tuesday. This time her alert fired first: the checkout p99 latency metric had jumped from 300ms to 3 seconds. She opened a trace of one slow checkout. The waterfall told the whole story in one glance: of the 2.9 seconds, 2.7 were spent inside a single database call in the inventory service — not payments, which everyone had blamed last time. She clicked into that span's logs (same trace_id) and saw a full-table scan: a deploy that morning had added a query with no matching index. She added the index. Latency dropped back to 300ms. Total time from alert to fix: eleven minutes. Same class of problem, same twelve services — but this time she wasn't guessing. She could see.

What should stay in your head
Observability is your ability to understand a system's internal state from its external outputs — crucially, to answer questions you didn't anticipate. It stands on three complementary pillars. Metrics are cheap numbers aggregated over time (requests/sec, error rate, p99 latency); they answer "is something wrong, and when did it start?" — they detect. Traces follow one request across every service, showing where the time and errors went; they answer "which service is to blame?" — they localize. Logs are detailed timestamped events; they answer "what exactly happened in that service?" — they explain the root cause. The workflow is a funnel: a metric alerts you, a trace localizes the failure to one span, and that span's logs explain it. None replaces the others — logs without metrics mean you learn of outages from Twitter; metrics without traces mean you know something's slow but not where; traces without logs mean you know where but not why. The thread that stitches it together is the correlation ID (trace ID), propagated across every service boundary. And the golden rule of measurement: watch percentiles (p99), not averages — the average hides the very users who are suffering.

The Three Pillars — And What Each One Is Blind To

The phrase "three pillars of observability" is everywhere, but the useful insight isn't that there are three — it's that each answers a different question and is useless for the others. Learn them by what they see and what they can't.

📈 Metrics

Answers: "Is something wrong? When did it start? Is it getting worse?"

A number measured over time — requests/sec, error rate, CPU %, p99 latency, queue depth. Stored in a time-series database, cheap to keep for years, instant to graph and alert on. This is what you build dashboards and alarms out of.

Cheap & aggregated: 1M requests become one "errors/sec" line. Great for detect.
Blind spot: it's aggregate — it can't tell you which request, which user, or why. A number can alarm; it can't explain.

📓 Logs

Answers: "What exactly happened, in detail, at this moment?"

A timestamped record of a discrete event — an error with a stack trace, a query that ran, a decision the code made. Best when structured (JSON with fields) rather than free text, so you can search and filter. This is where root-cause detail lives.

High detail: the exact SQL, the exact stack trace, the exact input. Great for explain.
Blind spot: expensive and voluminous at scale, and without a shared ID you can't follow one request across services — you drown in disconnected lines.

🧭 Traces

Answers: "For this one request, where did the time and the errors go?"

The end-to-end journey of a single request across every service, broken into spans (one span per operation) with timings, drawn as a waterfall. The missing link that logs and metrics leave out: causality and location across service boundaries.

Cross-service: shows the 2.7s was in inventory's DB call, not payments. Great for localize.
Blind spot: usually sampled (you keep a fraction), so the one trace you need may not have been recorded; and it shows shape, not the deepest why (that's the span's logs).
1️⃣
Metric alerts you — "checkout p99 jumped to 3s at 2:47pm." You know something is wrong and when. (Detect.)
2️⃣
Trace localizes it — open a slow request's waterfall; 2.7s of the 2.9s sits in one inventory-service DB span. You know where. (Localize.)
3️⃣
Logs explain it — jump to that span's logs by its shared trace_id; a full-table scan from a missing index. You know why. (Root cause.)

That funnel — detect → localize → explain — is the single most important thing to remember about observability. Each pillar hands off to the next.

Monitoring vs Observability — Known-Unknowns vs Unknown-Unknowns

These two words get used interchangeably, and the distinction is one of the most-loved interview questions on this topic. The cleanest way to hold it: monitoring is watching for the problems you already predicted; observability is being able to investigate the problems you didn't.

🔔
Monitoring = known-unknowns. You decided in advance what to watch: "alert me if CPU > 90%," "page me if error rate > 1%." You built the dashboard and the alarm ahead of time. It's essential — but it can only catch failure modes you anticipated.
🔎
Observability = unknown-unknowns. A property of the system: can you ask a new question you never pre-built a dashboard for — "why are only Android users in Brazil on checkout seeing 3s latency since the 2pm deploy?" — and get an answer from data you already collect? That needs rich, high-cardinality, correlated data (traces + structured logs with many fields).
🧭
The relationship. Monitoring is a use of your telemetry (pre-set alerts); observability is a capability of it (open-ended exploration). You want both: alerts to tell you when to look, and observability to figure out what's actually going on once you do.

A one-liner worth memorizing, popularized by Charity Majors: "Monitoring tells you whether the system works; observability lets you ask why it doesn't."

The Four Golden Signals — What to Actually Measure

You can measure a thousand things and still miss the outage. Google's SRE book distilled it to four signals that, watched together, catch the vast majority of user-facing problems. If you instrument nothing else on a service, instrument these.

SIGNAL 1

⏱ Latency

How long requests take — measured as percentiles (p50/p95/p99), and crucially, separating successful from failed requests (a fast error is still a failure).

SIGNAL 2

📊 Traffic

How much demand — requests/sec, transactions/sec, connections. Context for everything else: a latency spike during a 10× traffic surge means something different than one at 3am.

SIGNAL 3

❌ Errors

The rate of failing requests — explicit (HTTP 500s) and implicit (a 200 with a wrong body, or one that blew the latency budget). Often the fastest signal that a deploy went bad.

SIGNAL 4

🌡 Saturation

How "full" the system is — CPU, memory, disk, queue depth, connection-pool usage. The leading indicator: things usually get saturated before they get slow before they fail.

Two close cousins are worth knowing by name because interviewers use them: RED (Rate, Errors, Duration — a request-centric view, ideal for services) and USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors — a resource-centric view, ideal for machines and queues). They're the same instinct sliced two ways: watch how much work is coming in, how much is failing, how long it takes, and how close to the limit you are.

How Distributed Tracing Actually Works

Tracing is the pillar people understand least, so let's build it from scratch. The problem: a single user click fans out into dozens of calls across many services and machines. Logs on each machine are islands. How do you reconstruct the one journey? The answer is beautifully simple — a shared ID passed along for the ride.

Trace, span, and the ID you pass along

🆔
Trace ID. When a request first hits your system (at the API gateway), it's stamped with a unique trace_id. That single ID names the whole journey.
📦
Span. Each unit of work — one service handling the request, one DB query, one outbound call — is a span, with its own span_id, a start time, a duration, and a parent span ID pointing at whoever called it. Spans nest into a tree.
➡️
Context propagation. The magic step: when service A calls service B, it passes the trace context in a header (the W3C standard is the traceparent header). B reads it, makes its work a child span, and passes it on again. That's how islands become one map.
🌊
The waterfall. A collector gathers all the spans sharing a trace ID and reassembles them into a waterfall — horizontal bars showing when each span started and how long it took. One glance shows you where the time went.
Context propagation — the whole idea in a few lines
# API gateway: a request arrives with no trace → start one span = tracer.start_span("checkout") # trace_id=abc123, span_id=1 # Calling the inventory service — inject the context into the HTTP headers headers["traceparent"] = "00-abc123-0000000000000001-01" inventory.get(item, headers=headers) # Inventory service: EXTRACT the context, make a CHILD span ctx = tracer.extract(request.headers) # trace_id=abc123, parent=1 with tracer.start_span("inventory.db_query", parent=ctx) as s: s.set_attribute("db.statement", sql) # attach detail to the span db.query(sql) # the 2.7s full-table scan lives here

Sampling is the catch. Recording every span of every request is too expensive at scale, so systems keep only a fraction — say 1% (head sampling, decided up front) or, more cleverly, keep 100% of the slow or errored traces (tail sampling, decided after the fact). Sampling is the central tracing tradeoff: sample too little and the one trace you need at 3am was thrown away; sample everything and you pay a fortune to store millions of boring, identical, fast requests.

Why You Watch p99, Not the Average

This deserves its own section because it's the single most common measurement mistake, and a favorite interview trap. Averages lie about latency. Suppose 99 requests take 100ms and 1 request takes 10 seconds. The average is about 200ms — which looks fine, and hides the fact that 1 in 100 of your users just waited ten seconds. That one user is real, and at a million requests that's 10,000 furious people.

📉
p50 (median): half of requests are faster than this. The "typical" experience — useful, but it ignores the tail entirely.
📈
p99: 99% of requests are faster than this; 1% are slower. This is where your worst regular experience lives — and at scale that 1% is a huge number of real users.
🚨
p999 (p99.9): the extreme tail. Matters enormously for services others depend on — because in a fan-out request that touches 100 services, the slowest of the 100 decides your latency, so rare slowness compounds ("tail latency amplification").

Rule of thumb: alert on percentiles, never on the mean. And never average a percentile across machines (the average of two p99s is not a p99) — aggregate the underlying distribution instead. Watching the average is how a system can look perfectly healthy on the dashboard while a steady stream of users rage-quit.

The Observability Toolkit

You don't build these from scratch — a mature ecosystem exists, and the pieces map cleanly onto the three pillars.

📈 Metrics store

A time-series database that scrapes and stores numeric series and evaluates alert rules.

Prometheus, VictoriaMetrics, Graphite, Datadog, CloudWatch.

📓 Log pipeline

Collects, ships, indexes, and searches structured logs from every service.

ELK (Elasticsearch/Logstash/Kibana), Grafana Loki, Splunk, Fluent Bit.

🧭 Trace backend

Collects spans, stitches traces, and draws waterfalls.

Jaeger, Zipkin, Grafana Tempo, AWS X-Ray, Honeycomb.

🔌 OpenTelemetry

The vendor-neutral standard and SDK for emitting metrics, logs, and traces — instrument once, send anywhere. Now the default.

OTel SDKs + Collector.

📊 Dashboards

Visualize signals across all three pillars in one place; the on-call engineer's cockpit.

Grafana, Kibana, Datadog dashboards.

🚨 Alerting & on-call

Turns threshold/anomaly rules into pages and routes them to a human.

Alertmanager, PagerDuty, Opsgenie.

🎯 SLOs & error budgets

Define the target ("99.9% of requests < 300ms") and alert on burn rate against the budget, not raw noise.

Feeds directly into Day 30.

🧵 Correlation IDs

Not a product — a discipline. A shared trace_id on every log line and span is what makes the other tools work together.

Propagated via headers.

The Hard Parts

Observability sounds like "just collect more data," but every choice is a tradeoff, and getting them wrong is how teams end up paying millions to store data they never look at — or blind in the one moment they need to see.

Cost vs coverage — telemetry isn't free

At scale, your observability bill can rival your production bill. Logs are the usual budget-killer (verbose text at millions of lines/sec), traces need sampling, and metrics are cheap until cardinality bites (below). The art is collecting enough to answer tomorrow's unknown question without storing every heartbeat forever.

Cardinality — the silent metrics killer

A metric's cost explodes with the number of unique label combinations (its cardinality). Tag a request-count metric with status and endpoint (a few dozen combos) → fine. Add user_id as a label (millions of values) → you've just created millions of separate time series and blown up your metrics store. Rule: high-cardinality data (user IDs, request IDs) belongs in logs and traces, never as a metric label.

Sampling — you can't keep everything, so what do you throw away?

Head sampling (decide at the start, keep 1%) is cheap but may discard the exact slow request you needed. Tail sampling (keep 100% of slow/errored traces, drop the boring fast ones) is smarter but harder to run. Either way, the trace you want at 3am might be the one you didn't keep — a real limit, not a bug.

Alert fatigue — the human failure mode

Too many alerts is worse than too few: when everything pages, people mute everything, and the one real page is lost in the noise. Alert on symptoms users feel (SLO burn, error rate, latency) — not every internal cause. A good rule: every page must be actionable and urgent; everything else is a dashboard, not a page.

The through-line: observability trades money and complexity for the ability to ask questions you didn't plan for. You'll never see everything; the craft is choosing what to see so that the answer is there when the fog rolls in.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"We have logging, so we have observability."Logs are one pillar and the weakest one alone. Without metrics you learn of outages from users, and without traces you can't follow a request across services. Observability is the combination plus the correlation ID that links them.
"Monitoring and observability are the same thing."Monitoring watches for predicted problems (known-unknowns) via pre-built dashboards and alerts. Observability is the ability to investigate unpredicted ones (unknown-unknowns) by asking new questions of rich data. You need both.
"The average latency looks fine, so users are happy."Averages hide the tail. 99 fast requests and one 10-second request average to ~200ms while 1% of users suffer. Watch p99 / p999, and at scale that 1% is thousands of real people.
"Just log everything and alert on everything — more is safer."More is a liability. Verbose logs cost a fortune and bury the signal; too many alerts cause alert fatigue and the real page gets muted. Collect deliberately; page only on actionable, user-facing symptoms.
"Let's tag our metrics with user ID so we can slice by user."That's a cardinality explosion — millions of label values become millions of time series and melt your metrics store. High-cardinality data belongs in logs and traces, not metric labels.
"A green health check means the service is healthy."The Day 25 gray failure passes a shallow ping while real requests fail. Trust your golden signals — real user error rate and p99 — over a self-reported /health 200.

Active Recall

❓ It's 3am. An alert fires: checkout p99 latency has spiked to 3 seconds. Walk through exactly how you'd use the three pillars to go from that alert to a fix — naming which pillar does what, and the one mechanism that lets you jump between them. Then explain why watching the average latency instead of the p99 might have meant no alert fired at all. (click to reveal)
The detect → localize → explain funnel: (1) Metrics detected it. A metric (p99 checkout latency, one of the Four Golden Signals) crossed its alert threshold and paged me — so I know something is wrong and when it started (2:47pm, likely tied to a deploy). Metrics can't tell me which of my services is to blame, only that the user-facing symptom is real. (2) A trace localized it. I open a trace of one slow checkout request and read its waterfall: of the 2.9s total, 2.7s sits inside a single database span in the inventory service — not payments, where I might have guessed. The trace shows me where across twelve services the time actually went. (3) Logs explained it. Because every log line carries the same trace_id as the span, I jump straight to that DB span's logs and see the exact query — a full-table scan from an index that a morning deploy forgot to add. That's the why. I add the index; p99 drops back to 300ms. The linking mechanism: the correlation / trace ID, stamped at the gateway and propagated in a header to every downstream service, is what lets me hop from metric → trace → logs about the same request. Without it, the three pillars are disconnected islands. Why the average would have hidden it: if most requests were still fast (say 300ms) and only a fraction were 3s, the average might have risen only slightly — staying under any sane threshold — so no alert fires and the suffering minority is invisible. The p99 captures exactly that "worst 1%," which at scale is thousands of real, angry users. Alert on percentiles, never the mean.

Real-World Engineering

🔎 Google — Dapper & the SRE book

Google's Dapper paper (2010) introduced production distributed tracing — trace IDs, spans, and sampling — and seeded nearly every tracing system since (Zipkin, Jaeger, OpenTelemetry). Google's SRE book gave the world the Four Golden Signals and the discipline of SLOs and error budgets, which reframed reliability from "never fail" to "fail within an agreed budget."

🐝 Honeycomb — the observability distinction

Charity Majors and team built Honeycomb around high-cardinality, high-dimensionality events specifically to answer unknown-unknowns — the "why are only Android users in Brazil slow since 2pm?" questions that pre-built dashboards can't. They popularized the sharp monitoring-vs-observability framing this lesson uses.

🔌 OpenTelemetry — the standard

OpenTelemetry (a CNCF project, merging OpenTracing + OpenCensus) is now the industry-standard, vendor-neutral way to emit metrics, logs, and traces. You instrument your code once against OTel and ship the data to any backend — ending the era of proprietary agents locking you in.

📊 Prometheus + Grafana — the open stack

The default open-source duo: Prometheus scrapes and stores metrics and evaluates alert rules; Grafana draws the dashboards on top (and, with Loki + Tempo, unifies logs and traces). Nearly every Kubernetes shop runs some version of this — you'll meet it again in Phase 3.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What are the three pillars of observability, and what question does each answer?
A: Metrics — numbers over time; "is something wrong, and when?" (detect). Traces — one request's journey across services; "which service is to blame?" (localize). Logs — detailed timestamped events; "what exactly happened / why?" (root cause). They form a funnel: detect → localize → explain.
Q: What's the difference between monitoring and observability?
A: Monitoring watches for problems you predicted (known-unknowns) via pre-built dashboards/alerts. Observability is the ability to investigate problems you didn't predict (unknown-unknowns) by asking new questions of rich, correlated data. Monitoring tells you whether it works; observability lets you ask why it doesn't.
Q: How does distributed tracing stitch one request across many services?
A: A unique trace ID is stamped at entry and propagated in a header (e.g. W3C traceparent) to every downstream call. Each operation is a span with a parent-span pointer; a collector reassembles all spans sharing the trace ID into a waterfall showing where time went. Usually sampled to control cost.
Q: Why watch p99 latency instead of the average?
A: The average hides the tail — 99 requests at 100ms and one at 10s average to ~200ms while 1% of users suffer badly. At a million requests that 1% is 10,000 real people. Percentiles (p99, p999) capture the worst regular experience; averages make a suffering minority invisible.
Q: What are Google's Four Golden Signals?
A: Latency (how long requests take, as percentiles, splitting success from error), Traffic (demand — req/sec), Errors (rate of failing requests), and Saturation (how full the system is — CPU/memory/queues). Cousins: RED (Rate, Errors, Duration) and USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors).

If you only remember one thing: you cannot fix what you cannot see — observability is the sense organ of a distributed system. It rests on three complementary pillars: metrics (numbers over time — detect that something's wrong and when), traces (one request's journey across services — localize which service is to blame), and logs (detailed events — explain the root cause). They form a funnel — a metric alerts you, a trace localizes the failure, its logs explain it — stitched together by a correlation ID propagated across every service boundary. Watch the Four Golden Signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation), always in percentiles (p99, never the average, because averages hide the suffering minority), and remember that monitoring catches the failures you predicted while observability lets you investigate the ones you didn't. Every pillar costs money, cardinality, and sampling decisions — collect deliberately, alert only on what a human must act on, and the answer will be waiting when the fog rolls in.
