Security Fundamentals
The Three Promises Every System Makes

Yesterday the doorman checked badges at the front door. But two questions were lurking behind that badge: what makes a badge trustworthy, and what is the door actually defending against? Today we open the vault. Here's the first thing to unlearn: security isn't a feature you sprinkle on at the end — it's a property you design in from the start, and it comes down to three promises a trustworthy system makes, together called the CIA triad. Confidentiality: only the right people can read the data. Integrity: nobody changed it without being caught. Availability: it's actually there when you need it. Almost every security tool you'll ever meet exists to protect one of those three. We'll see how encryption buys confidentiality (and why HTTPS cleverly uses two kinds of keys), how hashing and digital signatures buy integrity, why we never encrypt passwords but hash and salt them instead, the crucial difference between authentication (who are you?) and authorization (what may you do?), the real-world threats each defense stops, and the two ideas that make a whole system safe: defense in depth and zero trust — the posture that justified yesterday's mesh mTLS. By the end, security will feel less like a dark art and more like three questions you can always ask of any design.

Picture this

Picture a medieval castle guarding a treasure. The treasure is your users' data — their messages, their money, their secrets. Now, a naive builder thinks "security" means one tall wall. But a single wall fails the moment someone finds a single gap. A master builder thinks differently: the treasure must keep three separate promises, and each needs its own kind of defense. Promise one: no outsider can read the treasure. So the gold is locked inside a sealed vault whose contents are scrambled into gibberish — even a thief who sneaks in sees only noise without the key. That's Confidentiality, and the vault is encryption. Promise two: no one can swap or shave the treasure without us noticing. So every gold bar is stamped with a unique tamper-evident wax seal; the instant a bar is altered, the seal cracks and the swap is obvious. That's Integrity, and the seal is a hash / digital signature. Promise three: the treasure is actually there when the king asks for it. So the castle has many gates, backup guards, and supply lines — if a mob floods one gate, the others still serve the realm. That's Availability, the redundancy and rate-limiting you've been building all week. And no single wall does the job: the castle has a moat, an outer wall, an inner keep, locked doors, and a guard who checks every person at every door — because if one layer fails, the next still holds (defense in depth), and no one is trusted just for being inside the walls (zero trust). Burn in the image: a trustworthy system is a castle keeping three promises — a sealed vault (Confidentiality), wax-sealed bars (Integrity), and many guarded gates (Availability) — defended in layers, trusting no one by default.

The Story

Trusting Tara shipped a small app that let friends send each other private notes. It worked, people loved it, and in the rush she made the choices that felt fine at the time. Notes traveled over plain http://. Passwords were stored in the database exactly as typed, so she could "help users who forgot them." There was one admin account with the password admin123. Security, she figured, was something you'd add later, once there was something worth protecting.

The first lesson came from a coffee shop. A curious user opened the free Wi-Fi and, with a free tool, simply read everyone's notes flying past in plain text — including his neighbor's. Because the traffic wasn't encrypted, anyone on the path could eavesdrop. Tara had broken promise one, Confidentiality. She switched the whole site to https://, and suddenly that traffic was scrambled gibberish to anyone in the middle. (How HTTPS does that — with two kinds of keys — is the heart of today's lesson.)

The second lesson was worse. Tara's database was leaked in a backup misconfiguration, and because she had stored passwords as typed, the attacker instantly had every user's password — and since people reuse passwords, their email and bank logins too. If she had stored only a salted hash — a one-way fingerprint that can't be reversed back into the password — the leak would have been near-useless. She had confused "I need to check the password" with "I need to store the password." You never need the second.

The third lesson was about tampering. A clever user noticed the app trusted a hidden field that said role=user, and simply changed it to role=admin in the request. Nothing checked that the field hadn't been altered, so he promoted himself. Tara had broken promise two, Integrity, and also confused two different questions: who are you? (authentication) and what are you allowed to do? (authorization). She started signing such data so any change would be detected, and checking permissions on the server, never trusting what the client claimed.

The last lesson came on launch day. A rival pointed a flood of junk requests at her single server and it fell over — not breached, just unreachable. She'd broken promise three, Availability. The fixes were the whole of Week 4: redundancy, rate limiting (Day 24), circuit breakers (Day 23), a gateway at the edge (Day 27). Tara emerged with the realization every senior engineer reaches: security isn't one wall you build at the end; it's three promises — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — you design in from the start, defended in layers, trusting nothing by default.

What should stay in your head
Security is the practice of keeping three promises about data and systems — the CIA triad. Confidentiality: only authorized parties can read it — delivered by encryption (scramble the data so it's gibberish without the key). Integrity: the data wasn't altered without detection — delivered by hashing and digital signatures (a tamper-evident fingerprint). Availability: the system is reachable and working when needed — delivered by redundancy, rate limiting, and the reliability toolkit of Week 4. Two kinds of encryption exist: symmetric (one shared secret key, fast, but how do two strangers agree on it?) and asymmetric (a public key to lock, a private key to unlock — solves the sharing problem but is slow); HTTPS/TLS uses asymmetric to safely agree on a symmetric key, then symmetric for the bulk data — the best of both. Hashing is one-way (you can't get the input back), which is exactly why passwords are hashed-and-salted, never encrypted. Keep authentication (proving who you are) separate from authorization (deciding what you may do). And two postures bind it together: defense in depth (many independent layers, so one failure isn't fatal) and zero trust (never trust a request just because it came from "inside" — verify every time; this is why yesterday's mesh encrypts even internal hops). The one-line test for any design: for this data, how am I keeping each of Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability — and what happens when one layer fails?

The CIA Triad — The Three Questions to Ask of Any Design

Forget, for a moment, the long list of security buzzwords. Almost everything collapses into three promises. When you look at any piece of a system, ask: how am I keeping each of these? If you can answer all three, you understand its security; if one has no answer, that's your hole.

🔒 Confidentiality

Only authorized people can read the data.

Kept by encryption (data in transit via TLS/HTTPS, data at rest on disk) and by access control — the fewest people and services can see each secret.

Broken by: an eavesdropper on open Wi-Fi, a leaked database of plaintext data, over-broad access.

🔏 Integrity

The data wasn't changed without detection — it's authentic and complete.

Kept by hashes, message authentication codes (MACs), and digital signatures — a fingerprint that changes the instant the data does, so tampering is caught.

Broken by: a man-in-the-middle editing a request, a user flipping role=user to admin, a corrupted download.

🟢 Availability

The system is there and working when legitimate users need it.

Kept by redundancy, failover, rate limiting, autoscaling — the whole reliability toolkit of Week 4. Security isn't only about secrets; an unreachable system has failed its users too.

Broken by: a DDoS flood, a single point of failure, a resource-exhaustion attack.

Why bundle such different things together? Because real attacks target whichever promise is weakest, and a design that obsesses over one while ignoring another is a castle with a magnificent vault and no gate. A famous extension adds two more promises worth knowing: authenticity (the data really is from who it claims — closely tied to integrity and signatures) and non-repudiation (the sender can't later deny they sent it — a property digital signatures give you for free). But if you remember only three letters for the rest of your career, remember C-I-A.

Encryption & Keys — How Confidentiality Actually Works

Encryption turns readable data (plaintext) into scrambled nonsense (ciphertext) using a key, such that only someone with the right key can turn it back. There are two families, and the single most important idea in this lesson is why HTTPS needs both.

🔑 Symmetric encryption

One shared secret key both locks and unlocks.

Fast and cheap — great for encrypting lots of data (e.g. AES). The catch: both sides need the same key, so how do two strangers agree on a secret key over the open internet without an eavesdropper stealing it? That's the key-distribution problem.

Like one key that opens and locks the same padlock — whoever holds it can do both.

🗝️ Asymmetric encryption

A pair of keys: a public key and a private key.

Anything locked with the public key can only be unlocked with the matching private key, and vice-versa (e.g. RSA, elliptic curve). You can hand your public key to the whole world; only you hold the private one. This solves key distribution — but it's slow, bad for bulk data.

Like an open padlock you mail to anyone: they snap it shut on a box, and only your private key opens it.

The HTTPS/TLS trick: use both

HTTPS (Day 3's secure web) gets the best of each. When your browser connects to a site, it does a TLS handshake: it uses the server's public key (proven genuine by a certificate — more below) to safely agree on a fresh, random symmetric session key that an eavesdropper can't derive. From then on, the whole conversation is encrypted with that fast symmetric key. Asymmetric to safely exchange the key; symmetric to do the heavy lifting. That's the pattern burned into every secure connection on the internet.

Certificates & the trust problem

One gap remains: when the server hands you a public key, how do you know it's really the bank's and not an impostor's? That's what a TLS certificate solves. A trusted Certificate Authority (CA) digitally signs a statement — "this public key belongs to bank.com" — and your browser ships with a list of CAs it trusts. If the certificate's signature checks out, you know the public key is genuine. This is the chain of trust that stops an impersonator (a man-in-the-middle) from slipping you their key. And it's exactly the trust yesterday's service mesh automates internally with mTLS: every service presents a certificate, so even inside the walls nobody is trusted on faith.

Hashing & Signatures — How Integrity Works

Integrity is a different problem from confidentiality. You don't necessarily need to hide the data — you need to detect if anyone changed it. The tool is a hash function.

A hash is a one-way fingerprint

A cryptographic hash (e.g. SHA-256) takes any input and produces a fixed-size string of gibberish — its fingerprint. It has three magic properties: (1) the same input always gives the same fingerprint; (2) the tiniest change to the input produces a completely different fingerprint (so tampering is obvious); and (3) it's one-way — given the fingerprint, you cannot compute the original input back. That one-way property is the whole point and it's what makes hashing different from encryption (which is designed to be reversible with the key).

The same data, two different goals
# ENCRYPTION is reversible — meant to be undone with the key (Confidentiality) encrypt("transfer $100", key) → "9f3a...c2" # ciphertext decrypt("9f3a...c2", key) → "transfer $100" # back again # HASHING is one-way — you can verify but never reverse (Integrity) sha256("transfer $100") → "a1b2...e9" # fingerprint sha256("transfer $900") → "7c4d...11" # one char changed → totally different # there is NO un-hash() — that's the point

From hash to signature

A hash alone proves data wasn't accidentally corrupted, but an attacker who edits the data could just recompute the hash. The fix combines hashing with asymmetric keys to make a digital signature: the sender hashes the message and then encrypts that hash with their private key. Anyone can verify it with the sender's public key — proving two things at once: the message wasn't altered (integrity) and it genuinely came from that sender (authenticity + non-repudiation). A MAC/HMAC is the symmetric cousin: a keyed hash both sides can check with a shared secret. Signatures are how a TLS certificate is trusted, how software updates prove they're genuine, and how yesterday's signed role field would have caught Tara's privilege-escalation attacker.

Storing Passwords — The Canonical "Hash, Don't Encrypt" Rule

This is the single most common security mistake, and the fix is a perfect application of everything above. The key insight: you never actually need to know a user's password — you only need to check whether the one they just typed is correct. That's a job for a one-way hash, not reversible encryption.

❌
Never store plaintext. A database leak instantly hands the attacker every password — and, since people reuse passwords, their other accounts too. This was Tara's disaster.
❌
Don't "encrypt" passwords either. Encryption is reversible, so whoever steals the key gets every password back. You don't need reversibility — so don't take on its risk.
✅
Store a hash. On signup, store hash(password). On login, hash what they typed and compare fingerprints. You can verify correctness while never storing the secret.
🧂
Add a unique salt per user. A salt is a random value mixed in before hashing, stored alongside the hash. It ensures two users with the same password get different fingerprints, and defeats precomputed "rainbow table" attacks.
🐢
Use a slow, purpose-built hash. Not plain SHA-256 — use bcrypt, scrypt, or Argon2, deliberately slow and memory-hard so an attacker can only test a few guesses per second instead of billions. "Slow" is a feature here.

Memorize the rule: encrypt data you need to read back; hash data you only need to verify. Passwords are the textbook case of the second.

Authentication vs Authorization — Two Questions, Not One

These two words sound alike and are constantly confused, but they answer completely different questions — and mixing them up is how Tara's attacker made himself an admin.

🪪 Authentication (AuthN)

"Who are you?" — proving identity.

Checking a password, a token, a fingerprint, a one-time code. It happens first and establishes who is making the request. (Tomorrow — Day 29 — is entirely about doing this well with OAuth 2.0 and JWT.)

Like the guard checking your ID at the castle gate.

🗂️ Authorization (AuthZ)

"What are you allowed to do?" — granting permissions.

Once we know who you are, this decides which actions and data you may touch — read this file, delete that order, enter the treasury. Enforced with roles (RBAC), policies, or access lists.

Like your ID being allowed into the courtyard but not the king's vault.

Two rules that follow directly. First, always authenticate before you authorize — you can't decide what someone may do until you know who they are. Second, and the one that bit Tara: never trust the client to authorize itself. A browser saying "role=admin" is just a claim; the server must decide, based on the authenticated identity, and must treat anything the client sends as untrusted input. Two more principles belong here: least privilege (give every user and service the minimum access it needs, nothing more) and fail closed (when in doubt, deny — an error should lock the door, not open it).

The Threats — What Each Defense Actually Stops

Defenses make the most sense when you can name the attacker they stop. Here are the classic ones you should be able to recognize on sight, each mapped to the promise it attacks.

BREAKS CONFIDENTIALITY

👁 Eavesdropping / sniffing

Reading data as it crosses the network (open Wi-Fi, a tapped link). Stopped by encryption (TLS), which turns the traffic into gibberish without the key.

BREAKS INTEGRITY + CONF

🎭 Man-in-the-middle (MITM)

An attacker sits between you and the server, reading and altering traffic, impersonating each side. Stopped by TLS certificates — the CA-signed proof that the public key is really the server's.

BREAKS INTEGRITY / CONF

💉 Injection (SQL / command)

Smuggling code into an input field so the server runs it ('; DROP TABLE users;--). Stopped by never trusting input — parameterized queries, validation, escaping.

BREAKS INTEGRITY / CONF

🧨 XSS & CSRF

XSS injects malicious script into a page other users load; CSRF tricks a logged-in user's browser into firing an unwanted request. Stopped by output encoding, a Content Security Policy, and anti-CSRF tokens.

BREAKS CONFIDENTIALITY

🔓 Credential & data leaks

Stolen passwords (reused across sites), leaked databases, secrets hard-coded in source. Stopped by salted-hashed passwords, encryption at rest, secret managers, and MFA.

BREAKS AVAILABILITY

💥 DDoS / resource exhaustion

Flooding the system with junk traffic until real users can't get in. Not a breach — an outage. Stopped by rate limiting (Day 24), autoscaling, CDNs/scrubbing, and redundancy (Day 22).

A useful habit from the industry: the OWASP Top 10 is a regularly-updated list of the most critical web-application risks (injection, broken access control, cryptographic failures, and friends). You don't need to memorize it today — just know it exists as the field's shared checklist of "the things that actually get people breached."

Defense in Depth & Zero Trust — The Two Postures

Individual defenses are tools. How you arrange them is the posture, and two ideas define modern practice.

Defense in depth — many layers, no single point of failure

Never rely on one wall. A request to a well-built system passes through many independent layers — a firewall, a CDN with DDoS scrubbing, a gateway doing authN and rate limiting (Day 27), input validation, TLS, authorization checks, encryption at rest, audit logging. The point is that if any one layer is bypassed, the next still holds. It's the same instinct as the castle's moat and wall and keep and guarded doors — the attacker has to beat every layer, and you only have to catch them at one.

Zero trust — never trust, always verify

The old model was a hard shell and a soft interior: authenticate at the perimeter, then trust anything "inside the network." That assumption is exactly how a single breach spreads laterally into a catastrophe. Zero trust flips it: no request is trusted just because of where it came from. Every request — even service-to-service, even "internal" — is authenticated, authorized, and encrypted. This is precisely why yesterday's service mesh wraps every east-west hop in mTLS: inside the walls is no longer a safe place to be un-checked.

🧅
Defense in depth is about layers — redundant, independent defenses so one failure isn't fatal. Assume any single control will eventually fail.
🚫
Zero trust is about assumptions — verify every request every time, regardless of origin. "Inside the network" grants you nothing.
🔑
Least privilege underlies both — the minimum access necessary, so a compromised component can do the least possible damage (its "blast radius").

The Hard Parts

Security is a field of tradeoffs, and the most senior thing you can say is that perfect security is both impossible and undesirable — the real job is managing risk against usability and cost.

Security vs usability

Every lock is friction. Force 20-character passwords rotated weekly plus MFA on every click, and users write passwords on sticky notes and route around you — making the system less safe. The best security is often the most invisible (TLS the user never notices, passkeys that replace passwords). Design for the human, or they'll defeat you.

You can't bolt it on at the end

Security retrofitted after launch is expensive and leaky; it has to be a design input from day one ("shift left"). But the opposite failure is real too — premature or cargo-cult security (rolling your own crypto, gold-plating a weekend project) wastes effort and often adds risk. Match the defense to the threat model and the value of what you're protecting.

Never roll your own crypto

The single most repeated rule in the field. Cryptography is brutally easy to get subtly, invisibly wrong. Use well-reviewed, battle-tested libraries and standard protocols (TLS, AES, Argon2) — never invent your own cipher or password scheme. "It looks scrambled to me" is not a security analysis.

The human is the weakest link

Most breaches don't defeat the math — they defeat a person: phishing, reused passwords, a leaked API key in a public repo, a social-engineered support agent. This is why multi-factor authentication, secret scanning, and least privilege matter more in practice than exotic cryptography. The strongest vault with the key under the doormat is not secure.

The through-line: security trades friction, cost, and complexity for confidentiality, integrity, and availability you can actually trust. You will never make a system perfectly secure; you make it secure enough for what it protects, in layers, assuming any one layer will fail — and you never, ever store a plaintext password.

Active Recall

❓ A fintech app sends users' bank transfers to its server. Walk through how it keeps each of the three CIA promises: how does it stop an eavesdropper from reading a transfer, stop a man-in-the-middle from changing the amount, and stay up during a flood? Separately: how should it store the user's login password, and why not just encrypt it? (click to reveal)
Three promises, three defenses. (1) Confidentiality → encryption (TLS/HTTPS). The transfer travels over HTTPS: the browser and server do a TLS handshake using the server's asymmetric public key (proven genuine by a CA-signed certificate, which also defeats an impersonator) to agree on a fast symmetric session key, then encrypt all traffic with it. An eavesdropper on open Wi-Fi sees only gibberish. (2) Integrity → hashing & signatures. TLS also carries a message-authentication code, and sensitive fields can be digitally signed (hash the data, encrypt the hash with a private key). If a man-in-the-middle changes $100 to $900, the fingerprint no longer matches and the tampering is detected and rejected. (And authorization is checked on the server, never trusting a client-supplied field.) (3) Availability → redundancy & rate limiting. A flood is defeated not by crypto but by Week 4's toolkit: rate limiting at the gateway (Day 24), a CDN/scrubbing layer (Day 4), autoscaling, and redundant servers with failover (Day 22) so the service stays reachable. Password storage: store a salted hash using a slow algorithm (bcrypt/scrypt/Argon2) — on login, hash the typed password and compare fingerprints. Don't encrypt it, because encryption is reversible: whoever steals the key gets every password back (and users reuse passwords across sites). You never need to read the password — only verify it — so take the one-way hash and avoid the reversibility risk entirely. The salt makes identical passwords hash differently and kills rainbow-table attacks. Binding idea: encrypt what you must read back; hash what you only verify — all under defense in depth and zero trust.

Real-World Engineering

🔐 Let's Encrypt — HTTPS for everyone

Before 2015, TLS certificates cost money and were fiddly, so much of the web stayed on plain HTTP. Let's Encrypt, a free automated Certificate Authority, flipped the default: today the large majority of web traffic is encrypted. It's the clearest proof that the best security is the kind that becomes invisible and automatic.

💥 LinkedIn 2012 — why salt matters

LinkedIn leaked ~6.5M password hashes stored as unsalted SHA-1. Because they were unsalted and fast-hashed, attackers cracked most within days using rainbow tables and GPUs. The post-mortem is the industry's standard lesson: hash with a slow, salted algorithm (bcrypt/Argon2), never a bare fast hash.

🌐 Google — BeyondCorp & zero trust

Google pioneered BeyondCorp, abandoning the "trusted internal network" model entirely: every request is authenticated and authorized based on user and device, with no implicit trust from being on the corporate network. It turned zero trust from theory into a production architecture that reshaped the industry.

🛡️ Cloudflare — availability as security

Cloudflare absorbs some of the largest DDoS floods on record (tens of millions of requests per second) in front of millions of sites, using a global edge network, rate limiting, and traffic scrubbing. A reminder that Availability is a security property too — an unreachable bank has failed its customers as surely as a breached one.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What is the CIA triad, and what delivers each promise?
A: Confidentiality (only authorized can read) — delivered by encryption + access control. Integrity (not altered undetected) — delivered by hashing & digital signatures. Availability (there when needed) — delivered by redundancy, failover, and rate limiting. Three promises every trustworthy system keeps.
Q: Symmetric vs asymmetric encryption — and why does HTTPS use both?
A: Symmetric = one shared key, fast, but hard to exchange safely. Asymmetric = public/private key pair, solves key exchange, but slow. HTTPS/TLS uses asymmetric (with a CA certificate) to safely agree on a fresh symmetric session key, then symmetric for the bulk data — best of both.
Q: Why hash passwords instead of encrypting them?
A: Encryption is reversible — steal the key, recover every password. You never need to read a password back, only verify it, so store a salted hash with a slow algorithm (bcrypt/scrypt/Argon2). A leak of hashes is near-useless; a leak of plaintext or decryptable passwords is catastrophic.
Q: Authentication vs authorization?
A: Authentication (AuthN) = "who are you?" (proving identity — password, token, MFA). Authorization (AuthZ) = "what may you do?" (permissions — roles, policies). Authenticate first, then authorize — and the server decides, never trusting a client-supplied role.
Q: Defense in depth vs zero trust?
A: Defense in depth = many independent layers, so one failure isn't fatal (moat + wall + keep + guards). Zero trust = never trust a request for its origin; verify and encrypt every request even internally (why the mesh does mTLS on every east-west hop). Both lean on least privilege.

If you only remember one thing: security is keeping three promises — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — designed in from the start, defended in layers, trusting no one by default. Confidentiality comes from encryption: symmetric keys are fast but hard to share, asymmetric keys solve sharing but are slow, and HTTPS uses asymmetric (via a CA-signed certificate) to agree on a symmetric session key — the pattern under every secure connection. Integrity comes from one-way hashes and digital signatures, which detect any tampering — and because hashing is one-way, it's exactly why passwords are hashed and salted, never encrypted (you verify a password, you never need to read it back). Keep authentication ("who are you?") separate from authorization ("what may you do?"), authenticate first, and never let the client authorize itself. Name your threats — eavesdroppers, man-in-the-middle, injection, leaks, floods — and match each to its defense. Finally, arrange it all with defense in depth (many layers, so one failure isn't fatal) and zero trust (verify every request, even internal — the posture behind yesterday's mesh mTLS).
