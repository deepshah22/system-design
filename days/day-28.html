Security Fundamentals
The Vault Behind the Front Door

Yesterday the doorman at the gate checked everyone's badge. But that raises a deeper question the doorman can't answer: what makes a badge trustworthy at all? How do two strangers who've never met agree on a secret over a wire anyone can tap? How do you keep a password safe even after an attacker steals your entire database? How do you know a message wasn't quietly altered on its way to you? Today we open the vault. Security isn't one feature you bolt on at the end — it's a way of thinking that runs through every layer of a system. We start with the CIA triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability — the three things you're always protecting. Then we meet the three tools that do almost all the work: encryption (reversible — keeps secrets), hashing (one-way — proves nothing changed, and safely stores passwords), and digital signatures (prove who sent something and that it wasn't touched). We'll see how symmetric vs asymmetric keys and certificates (PKI) let total strangers trust each other, exactly how to store a password so a stolen database is nearly worthless, and the mindset that separates a senior engineer from a junior one: defense in depth, least privilege, and zero trust — designing as if every single layer will eventually be breached.

Visual Memory Anchor

🏰 🔒 💎

Picture this

Picture a castle guarding the crown jewels. The jewels — your users' data — sit in a vault at the very center. A thief who wants them has to get past layer after layer: a wide moat, then thick outer walls, then armed guards checking everyone inside, then finally the vault's combination lock. No single wall is assumed to be perfect — the whole point is that even if the thief scales one wall, three more stand behind it. That's defense in depth: many independent layers, because you plan for each one to eventually fail. Now look at what the castle is actually protecting. Three things. First, the jewels must stay secret — only the king's key opens the vault. That's Confidentiality, and the tool is the lock (encryption). Second, every royal scroll leaving the castle carries a wax seal pressed with the king's signet ring — if the seal is broken or the ring is wrong, you know instantly the message was tampered with or forged. That's Integrity and authenticity, and the tools are the fingerprint and the seal (hashing and digital signatures). Third, the drawbridge has to stay down for allies even while under siege — a castle no one can enter has failed its people just as surely as one that's been robbed. That's Availability. Together these three — keep it secret, keep it unaltered, keep it reachable — are the CIA triad, the north star of everything security. Burn in the image: a castle of many walls (defense in depth) guarding jewels that must stay secret (Confidentiality = the lock/encryption), sealed against tampering (Integrity = the fingerprint & wax seal = hashing & signatures), and still reachable by its people (Availability). Assume every wall will be attacked — so build more than one.

The Story

Plaintext Pat shipped a beloved little app fast. Users signed up, and to keep things "simple" Pat stored their passwords in the database exactly as typed — hunter2, letmein, right there in a column called password. Traffic ran over plain HTTP because HTTPS "was a hassle to set up." Internal services trusted each other completely — after all, they were behind the company firewall. It worked. It was fast to build. And it was a disaster waiting for a trigger.

The trigger came on café Wi-Fi. A user logged in over the coffee-shop network, and someone at the next table was quietly sniffing the traffic. Because there was no encryption on the wire, that person read the username and password in plaintext as clearly as a postcard. That alone was bad. But it got catastrophically worse: months later, an attacker found a hole in an unrelated service and dumped the whole database. Every password, in plaintext. Within hours those email-and-password pairs were being tried on banks, email providers, and other sites — because people reuse passwords — and Pat's breach became dozens of breaches. Pat learned the hard way that "it's internal" is not a security model, and that a database is stolen sooner or later — the only question is what the thief gets when it happens.

Rebuilding, Pat stopped thinking in features and started thinking in the CIA triad. First, Confidentiality: every connection now used TLS (HTTPS), so traffic on the wire became unreadable scramble — the café eavesdropper would now see only ciphertext. Second, and the lesson that stung most, passwords. Pat never stored a password again. Instead each one went through a salted, slow hash (bcrypt): a one-way function that turns hunter2 into $2b$12$Nq7… and cannot be reversed. Now even a full database dump gives the attacker useless gibberish — there's nothing to "decrypt," because a hash was never encryption to begin with.

Then Integrity: the tokens Pat's services passed around got digitally signed, so a client couldn't quietly change role=user to role=admin without the signature failing — any tampering was caught instantly. And to make sure clients were really talking to Pat's server and not an impostor on that same café network, the app validated the server's certificate against trusted authorities — the mechanism that lets a stranger prove who they are.

Finally, Pat threw out "internal means safe." Every service now had to prove its identity to every other service (zero trust), each ran with only the permissions it strictly needed (least privilege — the reporting service could read orders but never delete them), and defenses were layered so that no single failure meant game over. The next time an attacker probed the system, they hit a wall — and behind it, another wall. Pat finally understood the sentence that separates senior engineers from juniors: security isn't a feature you add; it's an assumption you design around — that every layer will, eventually, be attacked.

What should stay in your head
Security is about protecting three things — the CIA triad: Confidentiality (only the right people can read it), Integrity (no one altered it without detection), and Availability (it stays reachable). You defend them with three cryptographic tools that are easy to confuse and must be kept straight: encryption is reversible and keeps secrets (you can decrypt with the key); hashing is one-way and proves nothing changed — it's how you store passwords, and you can never get the original back; digital signatures prove who sent something and that it wasn't tampered with. Two flavors of encryption: symmetric (one shared secret key — fast, but how do two strangers agree on it?) and asymmetric (a public key anyone can use to encrypt or verify, and a private key only you hold to decrypt or sign — this is what makes TLS and certificates possible, letting strangers trust each other). Above the tools sits the mindset: defense in depth (many independent layers because each will eventually fail), least privilege (grant the minimum access needed), and zero trust (never trust a request just because it's "internal" — verify every time). The one-line test: am I keeping it secret (encrypt), proving it's unchanged (hash / sign), or proving who sent it (sign / certificate)?

The CIA Triad — What You're Actually Protecting

Before any tool, name the goal. Almost every security decision maps to protecting one of three properties. Miss one and you have a real, if less obvious, security failure — a system no one can reach is broken even if its secrets are perfectly safe.

🔒 Confidentiality

Only authorized people can read the data.

Keeping secrets secret — on the wire and on disk. The main tool is encryption, backed by access control (authN/authZ, Day 29).

Broken by: eavesdropping, a stolen unencrypted database, an over-broad permission.

🪬 Integrity

The data wasn't altered — and you can prove it.

Detecting any tampering, accidental or malicious. Tools: hashing (checksums), MACs, and digital signatures (which also prove who).

Broken by: a man-in-the-middle flipping a value, a corrupted file, a forged token.

⛲ Availability

The system stays reachable when legitimate users need it.

Security's third leg, and the one engineers forget. Defended by everything in Week 4 — redundancy, rate limiting, DDoS protection, circuit breakers.

Broken by: a DDoS flood, a ransomware lockout, an outage that denies service.

Why hold all three at once? Because they trade off. Encrypt everything and lose the key → perfect confidentiality, zero availability. Make a system trivially reachable by everyone → great availability, no confidentiality. Senior security work is balancing the triad for your threats, not maximizing one. A fourth idea rides alongside integrity — authenticity (proving who an actor really is) and non-repudiation (they can't later deny it) — both delivered by digital signatures. Keep the picture: secret, unaltered, reachable.

The Three Cryptographic Tools

Nearly all of applied security is three primitives used well. The single most common interview stumble is confusing encryption with hashing — so nail the difference: encryption is a locked box you can reopen with the key; hashing is a fingerprint you can never turn back into the finger.

TOOL 1

🔒 Encryption — reversible secrecy

Scrambles data with a key so only a key-holder can unscramble it. Two-way: encrypt → ciphertext → decrypt → original. Use it for secrets you need back later: traffic on the wire (TLS), data on disk, secrets in a config store. Never for passwords.

TOOL 2

#️⃣ Hashing — one-way fingerprint

Maps any input to a fixed-size digest that can't be reversed, where the tiniest input change flips the whole output. Same input always → same digest. Use it to verify without storing the original: password storage, file/data integrity checks, deduplication. (SHA-256 for integrity; bcrypt/argon2 for passwords.)

TOOL 3

✍️ Digital signatures — who + unaltered

The sender signs with their private key; anyone verifies with the matching public key. A valid signature proves two things at once: the message wasn't tampered with (integrity) and it really came from that sender (authenticity). It's what makes certificates and signed tokens trustworthy.

A close cousin of the signature is the MAC (message authentication code, e.g. HMAC): like a signature it proves integrity + authenticity, but it uses a shared secret key instead of a public/private pair — faster, but both sides must already share the secret. Rule of thumb: HMAC when both parties share a key; a digital signature when the verifier only has your public key.

Keys & Certificates — How Strangers Come to Trust Each Other

Encryption needs keys, and there are two families. The difference is the whole reason HTTPS between total strangers is even possible.

Symmetric vs asymmetric

DimensionSymmetricAsymmetric (public-key)
KeysOne shared secret key encrypts and decryptsA key pair: public (share freely) + private (never share)
SpeedVery fast — good for bulk dataMuch slower — good for small payloads & setup
The hard partKey distribution: how do two strangers agree on the secret without an eavesdropper hearing it?Solves distribution — you can hand out the public key in the open
ExamplesAES, ChaCha20RSA, ECC / Elliptic Curve, Diffie-Hellman

The magic of asymmetric keys: what one key locks, only the other unlocks. Encrypt with someone's public key → only their private key can decrypt (secrecy). Sign with your private key → anyone with your public key can verify it was you (authenticity). This is how TLS starts a connection: asymmetric keys are used briefly to safely agree on a fast symmetric key, then the rest of the conversation uses the speedy symmetric cipher. Best of both — asymmetric to bootstrap trust, symmetric to move data.

Certificates and PKI — trusting a public key

One problem remains. When your browser gets a public key claiming to be bank.com, how does it know an attacker didn't substitute their public key? The answer is a certificate: a document that binds a public key to an identity (bank.com), digitally signed by a Certificate Authority (CA) your device already trusts. Your browser ships with a list of trusted CAs; it checks the CA's signature on the certificate, and if it's valid, it trusts the key. That chain of trust — CAs vouching for identities — is PKI (Public Key Infrastructure). It's exactly the "certificate validation" toggle in the simulator, and it's what stops the café impostor from impersonating your bank. mTLS from yesterday's mesh is just this in both directions: client and server each present a certificate.

Storing Passwords Safely — The Canonical Example

This is the single most-asked applied-security question, and it ties the tools together. The rule is blunt: never store passwords, and never encrypt them — hash them. Here's why, and how, step by step.

❌
Plaintext. Store hunter2 as-is. One database leak and every account — plus every other site where the user reused that password — is compromised. This was Pat's original sin.
❌
Encryption. Tempting, but wrong: encryption is reversible, so the decryption key lives somewhere — and whoever steals the database can usually steal the key too. You don't ever need the original password back; you only need to check a guess. So don't make it recoverable.
⚠️
Plain fast hash (MD5/SHA-256). One-way, good — but too fast and unsalted. Attackers precompute rainbow tables of common passwords' hashes and reverse yours by lookup; and identical passwords produce identical hashes, exposing patterns.
✅
Salted, slow hash (bcrypt / scrypt / argon2). Add a random salt to each password before hashing — so identical passwords get different hashes and rainbow tables are useless — and use a deliberately slow algorithm so brute-forcing billions of guesses becomes economically impossible. This is the correct answer.
Signup and login — never the plaintext, only the salted slow hash
# SIGN UP — store only the salted, slow hash (bcrypt), never the password salt = random_bytes() # unique per user digest = bcrypt(password, salt, cost=12) # slow on purpose db.save(user, digest) # salt is stored inside the digest # LOG IN — hash the guess the same way and compare, in constant time ok = bcrypt.verify(guess, db.load(user).digest) if ok: "welcome" # we NEVER decrypted anything — a hash can't be reversed

Two finishing touches senior engineers add: a per-user salt (above) plus optionally a global secret pepper kept outside the database, and rate limiting + MFA so that even a correct password guess is throttled and a second factor is required. Notice the whole design assumes the database will be stolen — and makes the theft nearly worthless anyway. That is defense in depth applied to one field.

Anatomy of a Secure Request — The Tools Working Together

Watch a single login flow and see all three tools cooperate. Confidentiality on the wire, authenticity of the server, integrity of the token, and a hash guarding the password — each doing exactly one job.

📜
1. Verify the server (PKI + signature). The client opens TLS; the server presents a certificate. The client checks the CA's digital signature on it — proving this really is bank.com, not the café impostor. (Authenticity.)
🤝
2. Agree on a key (asymmetric → symmetric). Using public-key crypto, the two sides safely agree on a fast symmetric session key without any eavesdropper learning it.
🔒
3. Encrypt the traffic (symmetric). The password travels inside the TLS tunnel as ciphertext. The café sniffer sees only scramble. (Confidentiality.)
#️⃣
4. Check the password (hashing). The server hashes the submitted password with the stored salt and compares digests — it never decrypts anything, because there's nothing to decrypt.
✍️
5. Issue a signed token (signature). On success the server returns a signed session token. If the client edits role=user→admin, the signature fails and the server rejects it. (Integrity + authenticity — tomorrow's JWT.)
Each tool does exactly one job
# CONFIDENTIALITY — reversible, keeps secrets on the wire & on disk tls: encrypt(traffic, session_key) # decryptable with the key # INTEGRITY / AUTHENTICITY — prove unaltered & who sent it token.sig = sign(payload, server_private_key) # verify with public key # PASSWORD — one-way, NEVER decrypted, only re-hashed & compared stored = bcrypt(password, salt) # no key can reverse this

Security Principles — The Mindset Above the Tools

Tools are necessary but not sufficient. These principles are how senior engineers reason about security — and they show up in every design review.

🧅 Defense in depth

Never rely on one control. Layer independent defenses so a single failure isn't a breach — the castle's moat, walls, guards, and vault.

WAF + TLS + authZ + encrypted DB + least privilege, all at once.

🔑 Least privilege

Grant every user, service, and token the minimum access it needs, and nothing more — so a compromise has a small blast radius.

The reports service can read orders, never delete them.

🚫 Zero trust

"Internal" is not a security boundary. Authenticate and authorize every request, even service-to-service — the assumption Pat learned the hard way.

mTLS between services (Day 27); verify every hop.

🛑 Secure by default / fail closed

The safe option is the default; when something errors, deny access rather than allow it.

A permission check that throws should block, not wave the user through.

📉 Minimize attack surface

Fewer open ports, endpoints, and dependencies = fewer ways in. Turn off what you don't use.

Close unused ports; don't expose the admin panel to the internet.

🧩 Don't roll your own crypto

Use vetted, battle-tested libraries and protocols. Amateur crypto is where breaches hide.

Use TLS, bcrypt, and a real auth library — not a homemade cipher.

The Hard Parts

Security is never free, and the most senior thing you can say is where it costs — because a control applied blindly can hurt more than the threat it addresses.

Security vs usability vs performance

Every control trades against the other legs. Force a 32-character password rotated weekly and users write it on a sticky note — you lowered security by fighting usability. Encrypt and hash everything on the hot path and you pay latency. The craft is proportionality: strong controls where the data is sensitive, light touch where it isn't.

Key management is the real problem

Crypto rarely fails because the math broke; it fails because a key leaked — committed to a repo, logged, or left in an environment variable. Secrets need a real home (a KMS / secrets manager), rotation, and tight access. "Where does the key live, and who can read it?" is the question that decides whether your encryption is real.

The human layer & the supply chain

The most common breach isn't a cracked cipher — it's phishing, a leaked token, or a compromised dependency you pulled from a package registry. Defense in depth has to include people (MFA, training) and your software supply chain (pinned, scanned dependencies), not just your own code.

Compliance is a floor, not a ceiling

GDPR, PCI-DSS, HIPAA, SOC 2 set minimum bars for handling personal, payment, or health data. Meeting them is necessary but not the same as being secure — treat them as the starting line.

The through-line: security buys reduced risk with added complexity, latency, and friction. You'll never make a system perfectly secure; you make it expensive enough to attack that attackers move on — and you assume every layer will eventually be tested.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"We encrypt the passwords, so they're safe."Passwords should be hashed, not encrypted. Encryption is reversible — the key is stealable. Use a salted, slow hash (bcrypt/argon2); you never need the original back.
"Hashing and encryption are basically the same thing."Opposite intents. Encryption is two-way (you can decrypt with a key). Hashing is one-way (no key reverses it). Encrypt to keep secrets you'll need back; hash to verify without storing the original.
"Internal traffic is safe — it's behind the firewall."That's how breaches spread laterally. Zero trust: authenticate and encrypt every hop, even internal ones (the mesh's mTLS from Day 27).
"A hash is unreadable, so it's fine for secrets."A hash isn't secrecy — it can't be decrypted because it destroys the data. Fast unsalted hashes of common passwords are reversed by rainbow tables. Salt + a slow algorithm are what matter.
"HTTPS means my whole app is secure."TLS secures data in transit only. It does nothing for SQL injection, broken access control, stored plaintext passwords, or a leaked key. Security is every layer.
"We'll add security before launch."Bolted-on security misses the architecture-level decisions (trust boundaries, key management, least privilege). It's a design assumption, threaded through from the start.

❓ An attacker steals your entire user database — every row. In one design every account is instantly compromised (and so are those users' accounts on other sites); in another the dump is nearly worthless. Explain exactly what the second design did to its passwords and why it works, why you must not simply "encrypt" them instead, and name the three cryptographic tools and the one-line job of each. (click to reveal)
The safe design hashed the passwords with a salted, slow one-way function. Instead of storing hunter2 (or an encrypted, and therefore reversible, version of it), each password was run through bcrypt/scrypt/argon2 with a unique random salt and stored only as the resulting digest. This works for three reasons: (1) one-way — a hash can't be reversed, so a stolen digest gives the attacker nothing to "decrypt"; there is no key to steal because a hash never used one. (2) salted — the per-user random salt means identical passwords produce different digests, which makes precomputed rainbow tables useless and hides patterns. (3) slow — a deliberately expensive algorithm makes brute-forcing billions of guesses economically impossible. Why not encrypt? Encryption is reversible, so a decryption key must exist somewhere — and whoever steals the database can usually steal the key too, turning every password back into plaintext. You never need the original password back; you only need to check a guess, so make it irreversible. The three tools: Encryption — reversible; keeps secrets you'll need back (TLS on the wire, data at rest). Hashing — one-way fingerprint; verify without storing the original (passwords, integrity checks). Digital signatures — sign with a private key, verify with the public key; prove who sent it and that it wasn't altered. Bonus for the senior answer: layer on rate limiting + MFA so even a correct guess is throttled and needs a second factor — defense in depth applied to one field.

Real-World Engineering

🔓 The plaintext-password breaches

Time and again, giant leaks turned into cross-site catastrophes because passwords were stored in plaintext or with fast unsalted hashes — attackers cracked them and replayed the credentials elsewhere (credential stuffing). The industry moved to bcrypt/argon2 + salt precisely because a database will eventually leak; the goal is to make the leak worthless.

🔒 Let's Encrypt — TLS for everyone

Let's Encrypt issued free, automated certificates and helped flip the web from majority-HTTP to majority-HTTPS. It's PKI at planetary scale: a trusted CA vouching for identities so any two strangers — your browser and any server — can establish confidentiality and authenticity automatically.

☁️ Google BeyondCorp — zero trust

Google's BeyondCorp abandoned the "trusted internal network" model entirely: every request is authenticated and authorized based on device and user identity, not network location. It's the industry blueprint for zero trust — the exact lesson Plaintext Pat learned about "internal isn't safe."

🗝️ AWS KMS & HashiCorp Vault — key management

Because leaked keys, not broken math, cause most crypto failures, teams centralize secrets in a KMS (AWS KMS, Google Cloud KMS) or Vault: keys are generated, stored, rotated, and access-controlled in one hardened place, never scattered in code or config. It answers the real question: where does the key live?

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: What is the CIA triad?
A: The three properties security protects: Confidentiality (only authorized people can read it — encryption), Integrity (it wasn't altered, and you can prove it — hashing / signatures), and Availability (it stays reachable for legitimate users — redundancy, rate limiting, DDoS defense).
Q: Encryption vs hashing — what's the core difference and when do you use each?
A: Encryption is reversible (decrypt with a key) — use it for secrets you need back: TLS traffic, data at rest. Hashing is one-way (no key can reverse it) — use it to verify without storing the original: passwords (salted + slow), integrity checks. Never encrypt passwords; hash them.
Q: Symmetric vs asymmetric encryption, and how does TLS use both?
A: Symmetric = one shared key, fast, but hard to distribute to strangers. Asymmetric = a public/private key pair that solves distribution (share the public key openly). TLS uses asymmetric keys to safely agree on a fast symmetric session key, then moves the data with the symmetric cipher — bootstrap trust asymmetrically, move bulk data symmetrically.
Q: How should you store a user's password, and why not just encrypt it?
A: With a salted, slow one-way hash (bcrypt / scrypt / argon2). The salt defeats rainbow tables; the slowness defeats brute force; one-wayness means a stolen DB has nothing to decrypt. Don't encrypt — encryption is reversible, so a stealable key exists, and you never need the original password back.
Q: What do defense in depth, least privilege, and zero trust each mean?
A: Defense in depth = many independent layers because each will eventually fail. Least privilege = grant the minimum access needed, shrinking the blast radius of any compromise. Zero trust = never trust a request just because it's "internal"; authenticate and authorize every request, every hop.

If you only remember one thing: security means protecting the CIA triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability — with three tools you must never confuse: encryption (reversible, keeps secrets), hashing (one-way, proves nothing changed and stores passwords), and digital signatures (prove who sent it and that it wasn't altered). Symmetric keys are fast but hard to share; asymmetric key pairs solve distribution and, through certificates and PKI, let total strangers trust each other — which is exactly how TLS and mTLS work. Store passwords with a salted, slow hash so a stolen database is worthless, never with reversible encryption. And above every tool sits the mindset that separates senior engineers from juniors: defense in depth, least privilege, and zero trust — design as if every layer will eventually be attacked, because it will.
