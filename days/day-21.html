Event-Driven Architecture
Announce a Fact, Then Walk Away

For twenty lessons, services have communicated by asking each other directly: "Shipping, ship this. Now wait. Billing, charge this. Now wait." That's request-driven, and it quietly welds your whole system into one brittle chain — one slow or broken service stalls or crashes everything upstream, and every new feature means editing the code that kicked off the flow. Today we invert it. In Event-Driven Architecture, a service does its own small job, announces a fact — "an order was placed" — and walks away. It doesn't know or care who's listening. Anyone who cares reacts on their own time. This one change — from commanding to announcing — is how modern systems get loosely coupled, endlessly extensible, and resilient to failure. And it comes with a bill you must understand before you pay it.

Picture this

Two ways to run a busy office when an order comes in. In the first, the manager grabs a phone and calls each department in turn: "Warehouse — ship it. …Done? Good. Accounting — bill it. …Done? Good. Front desk — email the receipt. …Done?" The manager must personally know every department, call them one by one, and wait for each — and if the Accounting line is busy, the whole order jams right there. That's request-driven: command, wait, command, wait. In the second, the manager simply steps to the door and rings a bell: "Order forty-two has been placed!" — then goes back to work. The warehouse hears it and starts packing. Accounting hears it and bills. The front desk hears it and emails. Nobody was phoned; each department reacts on its own, in parallel, and if one is on a coffee break the bell was still rung — they'll catch up. The manager doesn't know or care who's listening. That bell is an event. Burn in the image: request-driven is phoning each department and waiting; event-driven is ringing one bell and walking away.

The Story

Coupling Omar built the checkout for a fast-growing online store, and at first it was one tidy function. When a customer clicked Pay, his code ran down a list: save the order, then call the payment service and wait, then call the warehouse and wait, then call the email service and wait, then update loyalty points and wait, then notify analytics and wait — and only then tell the customer "Order confirmed!" Clean, readable, and easy to follow top-to-bottom. It worked beautifully in the demo.

Then reality arrived. The email provider had a slow afternoon, and because checkout waited for it, customers stared at a spinner for eight seconds — some gave up and their carts died. Worse, one evening the loyalty service crashed, and because it sat in the middle of the chain, checkout itself started failing — people literally couldn't buy anything because a points service was down. Omar had accidentally made every downstream service a single point of failure for his most important flow. And every time the company wanted something new — fraud screening, a warehouse in a second country, a "you might also like" recommender — Omar had to crack open the sacred checkout function again, re-test the whole thing, and pray. Checkout had quietly become a spider at the center of a web it couldn't stop growing.

So Omar made one change that changed everything. Checkout would now do only its own job — validate the cart, save the order — and then publish a single event to the broker (the same Kafka/pub-sub bus from Days 18–19): OrderPlaced { orderId, userId, total }. Then it returned "Order confirmed!" immediately. It no longer called anyone. It no longer waited for anyone. It didn't even know who was out there. Payment, warehouse, email, loyalty, and analytics each became an independent subscriber to OrderPlaced, doing its work on its own time.

The wins stacked up fast. The slow email provider? Now irrelevant to checkout — the receipt just went out a second later while the customer already had their confirmation. The loyalty crash? Now harmless to checkout — the points event simply waited in the queue and was processed when the service recovered, and nobody was blocked from buying. Adding fraud screening? Omar wrote a new service that subscribed to OrderPlaced and shipped it — without touching checkout at all. The system had gone from a rigid chain to a loose federation of services that each minded their own business.

But Omar also met the new bill. The order's side-effects no longer all happened by the time checkout returned — the receipt, the shipment, the points now settled a little later (welcome to eventual consistency). When something went wrong, there was no single stack trace to read — a problem meant tracing an event across five services. And when the broker once delivered an OrderPlaced event twice, a customer got charged twice until Omar made his consumers idempotent. He'd traded the comfort of a straight line for the power of decoupling — and, like every real engineer, he now had to manage that trade instead of pretending it was free.

What should stay in your head
A request-driven system moves by commands: "do this, and I'll wait." The caller must know every service it calls, and it's coupled to them in three ways at once — in time (both must be up together), in space (it needs their addresses), and in knowledge (it must know they exist). An event-driven system moves by facts: a producer announces "this happened" (OrderPlaced, PaymentFailed) to a broker, and any number of consumers react on their own. The producer knows nothing about the consumers — that's the whole point. A command targets one handler and expects it to act; an event targets no one and simply reports the past. This inversion buys three superpowers — loose coupling (change consumers freely), extensibility (add new reactions without touching the producer), and resilience (a down consumer just delays its own work; the producer already moved on) — and charges three real costs: eventual consistency (side-effects settle later), harder reasoning & debugging (no single call stack — you trace events across services), and the duplicate/ordering problem (consumers must be idempotent and tolerate out-of-order delivery). Use it where reactions are many, independent, and can settle asynchronously; avoid it where you truly need one synchronous answer right now.

Commands, Events & the Three Kinds of Coupling

The whole shift lives in one distinction. A command is an instruction aimed at a specific handler: "ShipOrder", "ChargeCard". It's phrased in the imperative, it expects to be obeyed, and the sender usually wants a result. An event is a statement of fact about the past, announced to no one in particular: "OrderPlaced", "PaymentFailed". It's phrased in the past tense, it expects nothing, and the sender doesn't know who (if anyone) will act on it. Same wire, opposite intent — and that intent is what decides how tightly your system is welded together.

Command → tight coupling."ChargeCard" names a target and awaits an answer. The sender must know the payment service exists, know its address, and have it be up right now. One recipient, synchronous expectation, three-way coupling.
Event → loose coupling."OrderPlaced" names no one. Zero or many consumers may react, later, on their own. The producer needs to know only the broker. This is what "decoupled" actually means in practice.
Decoupled in time.Producer and consumer don't have to be running at the same moment. The event waits in the broker; a consumer that's down catches up when it returns. (This is exactly the queue durability of Day 18 and the retained log of Day 19.)
Decoupled in space.The producer doesn't know consumers' addresses, count, or location. You can move, replace, or scale a consumer with the producer none the wiser.
Decoupled in knowledge.The producer doesn't know consumers exist. This is the superpower behind extensibility: add a brand-new consumer and the producer's code never changes.

Orchestration vs Choreography

Once work spans many services, there are two ways to coordinate it — and the words come up in every senior interview. Orchestration is a central conductor: one service (an "orchestrator") explicitly tells each other service what to do and in what order — closer to request-driven, easy to see the whole flow in one place, but the conductor knows and is coupled to everyone. Choreography is a dance with no conductor: each service just reacts to events and emits its own, and the overall behavior emerges from those reactions — maximally decoupled and extensible, but there's no single place that shows the whole flow. Real systems mix both: choreography for the loose, fan-out reactions; a deliberate orchestrator (often a Saga, a Phase-2 topic) when a multi-step process needs a clear owner and a way to undo half-finished work.

The inversion, in code — producer stops calling, starts announcing
// BEFORE — request-driven: checkout knows everyone and waits for each function checkout(cart) { const order = saveOrder(cart); payment.charge(order); // wait… ← knows payment warehouse.ship(order); // wait… ← knows warehouse email.sendReceipt(order); // wait… ← knows email (and it's slow) loyalty.addPoints(order); // wait… ← if this is DOWN, checkout FAILS return "confirmed"; // only now, after everyone } // AFTER — event-driven: checkout does its job, announces a fact, returns function checkout(cart) { const order = saveOrder(cart); bus.publish("OrderPlaced", { orderId: order.id, userId: order.user, total: order.total }); return "confirmed"; // immediately — knows no one downstream } // Each reaction is an independent subscriber. Adding fraud = a NEW file, checkout untouched. bus.subscribe("OrderPlaced", (e) => emailReceipt(e.userId, e.orderId)); bus.subscribe("OrderPlaced", (e) => awardPoints(e.userId, e.total)); bus.subscribe("OrderPlaced", (e) => screenForFraud(e.orderId)); // ← added later, zero producer changes

Choreography & Cascades — How Events Trigger Events

An event-driven system rarely stops at one hop. A consumer reacts to an event and, in doing its job, emits a new event that other services react to — and so on. The business process becomes a cascade of reactions with no central controller directing traffic. This is choreography in motion, and it's both the beauty and the danger of EDA.

Notice three things. First, there is no orchestrator box — each service knows only the event it reacts to and the event it emits; the end-to-end flow is emergent, not written down anywhere. That's wonderfully flexible (drop a new service into the chain by subscribing) and genuinely hard to reason about (no one file shows you "what happens when an order is placed"). Second, the arrows take time: the customer sees "confirmed" at t=0, but the shipment and the notification settle over the next hundreds of milliseconds or seconds — the system is eventually consistent, not instantly consistent. Third, the very last hop is often the WebSocket/SSE layer from Day 20: a "Notify" service consumes Shipped and pushes it live to the user's browser. The realtime edge you learned yesterday is just one more consumer hanging off the event backbone.

Three Flavors of EDA — How Much Does the Event Carry?

"Event-driven" isn't one thing. The big design choice is how much data the event carries, and it changes coupling, autonomy, and storage in real ways. Here are the three you'll be asked to compare.

1 · Event Notification

The event is a thin ping: "Order 42 changed." Consumers that need details call back to the source to fetch them. Lowest coupling on data, but chatty — every reaction may trigger a fresh request, and you're partly back to request-driven for the details.

Event: { "orderId": 42 } → consumer fetches the rest.

2 · Event-Carried State Transfer

The event carries all the data a consumer needs: "Order 42 placed, user 7, $58, these items." Consumers act with zero callbacks — fully autonomous, resilient if the source is down — at the cost of duplicated data and bigger, versioned events.

Event: { orderId, user, total, items[] } → consumer needs no one.

3 · Event Sourcing

The events are the source of truth. Instead of storing current state, you store the full log of events and rebuild state by replaying them — the Day-19 append-only log as your database. Perfect audit trail and time-travel; more complex to query (usually paired with CQRS).

State = replay(Placed → Paid → Shipped → …). A Phase-2 deep dive.

Orchestration

A central coordinator commands each step in order and can undo them on failure. Easy to see and control the whole flow; the coordinator is coupled to everyone. The Saga pattern (Day 34) formalizes this for long transactions.

Best for: multi-step processes needing a clear owner + rollback.

Choreography

No coordinator — services react to events and emit their own; behavior emerges. Maximally decoupled and extensible; hardest to trace end-to-end. The default "shape" of most event-driven systems.

Best for: loose, fan-out reactions where adding consumers must be free.

vs · Request/Response

The Day 15–17 baseline: synchronous, one caller expects one answer now. Still the right choice when you genuinely need an immediate result (a price quote, an auth check). EDA doesn't replace it — it handles the reactions that don't need to be synchronous.

Best for: "I need the answer to continue," not "let others know."

The decision is short: Do others just need to know something happened? → emit an event (carry state if you want them autonomous). Does one step own a multi-part process with rollback? → orchestrate it (a Saga). Do you need an answer to keep going right now? → a plain synchronous request still wins.

The Hard Parts — What Decoupling Costs You

EDA feels liberating, so teams over-adopt it and then get hurt. The freedom is real, but it's bought with four hard currencies.

1. Eventual consistency is now your default

The moment you stop waiting, side-effects happen later. The order is "confirmed" before the receipt is sent, the points awarded, or the warehouse notified. Usually that's fine — even better UX. But you must design for the gap: what does the UI show while things settle? What if two events settle out of order? "It'll be consistent… eventually" is a promise you now have to keep on purpose (this is the CAP/consistency world of Days 10–12, made concrete).

2. There's no single stack trace

In request-driven code, a failure gives you one call stack top to bottom. In EDA, "placing an order" is a cascade across five services connected only by a broker. When something breaks, you can't read one trace — you must follow an event's journey. This is exactly why distributed tracing, correlation IDs, and observability (Day 26) stop being nice-to-haves and become survival gear. Debugging is genuinely harder; budget for the tooling.

3. Duplicates and ordering — make consumers idempotent

Brokers deliver at least once, which means sometimes twice (Day 18/19). If a naive consumer processes OrderPlaced twice, it double-charges or double-ships. The fix is idempotency: design each consumer so that handling the same event twice equals handling it once (dedupe on an event ID, use upserts). Likewise, events can arrive out of order, so consumers must tolerate that or rely on per-key ordering (Kafka partitions). Idempotency gets its own lesson later — for now, remember it's mandatory, not optional.

4. The distributed-monolith trap

The scariest failure mode: you split into services and connect them with events, but every service still depends on every other's events in a fixed order — so you get all the operational pain of distribution and none of the decoupling. If adding a feature still means editing five services, or if one service being down still breaks the flow, you didn't build event-driven — you built a distributed monolith. Events are a tool for independence; if your services aren't independent, the events are just expensive function calls.

The through-line: EDA trades the certainty of a straight line — one flow, one trace, immediate consistency — for independence and reach — services that evolve, scale, and fail alone. A brilliant trade when you have many independent reactions to one thing happening; an over-engineered mess when you had a simple, synchronous request all along.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"Events and commands are the same — it's all just messages."A command tells one known handler to do something and expects it to act ("ChargeCard"). An event announces that something happened, in the past tense, to no one in particular ("OrderPlaced"). The intent difference is what makes events decoupled and commands coupled.
"Event-driven means everything is asynchronous, so it's always faster."The producer returns faster because it stops waiting — but the overall work still takes time, now spread out and eventually consistent. You've moved latency out of the critical path, not deleted it. Some flows genuinely need a synchronous answer and shouldn't be events at all.
"We use Kafka, so we're event-driven."A broker is the plumbing, not the pattern. If your producer still knows every consumer, still waits on them, and adding a feature still edits five services, you built a distributed monolith over Kafka. Event-driven is about independence, not about which tool carries the bytes.
"A consumer only gets each event once."Brokers deliver at least once — occasionally twice. Non-idempotent consumers double-charge, double-ship, double-email. You must make handling the same event twice equal to handling it once (dedupe on event ID, upserts).
"Events arrive in the order they were sent."Not globally. Ordering holds only within a partition/key (Day 19), and across services events can settle out of order. Design consumers to tolerate reordering or pin related events to the same key.
"Choreography is always better because it's more decoupled."Choreography maximizes decoupling but hides the end-to-end flow — no single place shows what happens. When a process has many steps and needs rollback, a deliberate orchestrator/Saga is clearer and safer. Real systems use both.

Active Recall

❓ Your checkout is one synchronous function that calls payment, warehouse, email, and loyalty in turn and then returns "confirmed." Two incidents hit this week: (a) the email provider got slow and customers watched an 8-second spinner, and (b) the loyalty service crashed and checkout itself started failing so no one could buy anything. Product also wants to add fraud screening next sprint. Explain (1) the root architectural problem, (2) how converting to event-driven fixes all three, and (3) the two new problems you'd now have to handle. (click to reveal)
(1) Root problem: checkout is request-driven and tightly coupled — it knows and waits on every downstream service, so each one is both a latency contributor and a single point of failure for the checkout flow. A slow email provider stalls checkout (you wait for it); a crashed loyalty service fails checkout (a non-critical dependency takes down your most critical path). And every new feature means editing the checkout function. (2) Event-driven fix: checkout does only its own job (validate + save order), publishes one OrderPlaced event to the broker, and returns immediately. Payment, warehouse, email, and loyalty become independent subscribers. Now the slow email is irrelevant to checkout (the receipt just goes out a beat later); the loyalty crash is harmless (its event waits in the queue and is processed on recovery — nobody is blocked from buying); and fraud screening is a new subscriber to OrderPlaced, shipped without touching checkout. That's loose coupling, resilience, and extensibility in one move. (3) New problems: first, eventual consistency — the receipt/points/shipment now settle after checkout returns, so you must design the UX and logic for that gap; second, duplicates and no single trace — the broker may deliver an event twice (so consumers must be idempotent, e.g. dedupe on order ID) and a failure now means tracing an event across services rather than reading one stack trace (so you need correlation IDs / distributed tracing). Mantra: announce a fact, walk away — but now own the consistency gap and make every consumer idempotent.

Real-World Engineering

🛒 Amazon & e-commerce

A single "order placed" event fans out to dozens of independent reactions — payment, inventory, shipping, recommendations, fraud, analytics, email. Each team owns its consumer and ships on its own cadence; none can stall the buy button. The classic textbook case of EDA, and why checkout stays fast and always-available.

🎬 Netflix

Netflix runs a heavily event-driven backbone: a "play started," "title added," or "device registered" event triggers cascades across personalization, encoding, billing, and analytics. Decoupling lets hundreds of microservices evolve independently — and forces heavy investment in tracing and observability to follow events across the mesh.

🚗 Uber

A trip is a river of events — RequestCreated, DriverMatched, TripStarted, TripEnded — that dispatch, pricing, ETA, fraud, and payments all react to. Choreographed events let each domain react in real time while the last hop pushes updates to your phone over the Day-20 realtime edge.

🏦 Banks & event sourcing

Ledgers are the purest event sourcing: an account's balance is the replay of every deposit and withdrawal event, never overwritten. The append-only log of events gives a perfect audit trail and time-travel — regulators love it, and it pairs naturally with CQRS for fast reads.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: In one sentence, what is Event-Driven Architecture and what problem does it solve?
A: Instead of services commanding each other and waiting (request-driven, tightly coupled), a service does its job, announces a fact ("OrderPlaced") to a broker, and walks away — decoupling producers from consumers so the system becomes loosely coupled, extensible, and resilient to a single service failing.
Q: What's the difference between a command and an event?
A: A command is an instruction to a specific handler that expects action ("ChargeCard") — coupled. An event is a past-tense fact announced to no one in particular ("PaymentFailed") that zero or many consumers may react to — decoupled.
Q: Orchestration vs choreography?
A: Orchestration = a central coordinator commands each step in order (visible, controllable, coupled to all; e.g. a Saga). Choreography = no coordinator; services react to events and emit their own, behavior emerges (decoupled, extensible, hard to trace end-to-end).
Q: Name the three big costs of going event-driven.
A: (1) Eventual consistency — side-effects settle later; (2) harder debugging — no single stack trace, you trace events across services (need tracing/correlation IDs); (3) duplicates & ordering — at-least-once delivery means consumers must be idempotent and tolerate reordering.
Q: What's the "distributed monolith" trap?
A: Splitting into services connected by events but where each still depends on the others in a fixed way — so you get all the operational pain of distribution and none of the decoupling. Tell-tale signs: adding a feature still edits many services, or one service being down still breaks the flow.

If you only remember one thing: Event-Driven Architecture is the shift from commanding to announcing. A request-driven service tells others what to do and waits, welding itself to every one of them; one slow or broken dependency stalls or fails the whole flow, and every new feature edits the same code. An event-driven service does its own job, publishes a past-tense fact ("OrderPlaced") to a broker, and walks away — knowing nothing about who reacts. That inversion buys loose coupling, extensibility (new consumers, zero producer changes), and resilience (a down consumer only delays its own work). The price is real and must be managed: eventual consistency, no single stack trace (so tracing and observability become essential), and at-least-once delivery (so consumers must be idempotent and tolerate reordering). Use it for the many independent reactions to one thing happening; keep synchronous requests for when you truly need an answer now — and never mistake "we have Kafka" for "we're event-driven."
