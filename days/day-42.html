Design a Rate Limiter
The Bouncer That Protects Every Service

Yesterday our web crawler limited itself — a per-host crawl delay so it wouldn't knock over other people's servers. Today we build the reusable machine that lets any service do the same thing in reverse: protect itself by capping how many requests each caller may make. A rate limiter is the quiet component sitting in front of nearly every API you've ever used — it's why an API returns 429 Too Many Requests when you call it too fast. Strip away the mystique and a rate limiter answers exactly one question, millions of times a second: "has this caller used up their allowance for this window — yes or no?" The whole design is choosing how to count (token bucket, sliding window, and friends), where to count (at the edge, before the request wastes real work), and — the part that trips everyone up — how to keep one shared count correct across a fleet of limiter nodes without a race condition quietly letting double the traffic through. You'll fire requests at a live token bucket and watch it refill and reject in real time, then trigger the infamous fixed-window boundary burst and watch 2× your limit leak through — and watch a sliding window shut it down.

Picture this

Imagine a nightclub with one door and a bouncer standing in front of it. Next to the bouncer is a bucket, and above the bucket is a tap that drips tokens into it at a steady, unchanging rate — say one token every second. The bucket can only hold so many tokens before it overflows; call that its capacity. The club is your service; each person wanting in is a request; and the rule at this door is simple: to get in, you must take a token from the bucket.

When someone walks up, the bouncer looks in the bucket. Is there at least one token? If yes, the guest grabs a token and walks in — the request is allowed. If the bucket is empty, the bouncer says "sorry, come back in a moment" and turns them away — that's a 429, Too Many Requests. Then the tap keeps dripping, and in a second or two there's a token again and the next guest can enter. That drip rate is the sustained speed you allow — over a long night, people enter roughly as fast as the tap drips, no faster.

Now notice the quiet genius of the bucket itself. If the club is empty for a while, tokens pile up — right to the brim, capacity and no more. So when a sudden crowd arrives all at once, the first batch can all walk straight in, spending the tokens that accumulated during the quiet spell. That's a burst: short spikes are welcome, up to the bucket's capacity, because real traffic is bursty and you don't want to punish a legitimate flurry. But once those saved-up tokens are gone, everyone else waits for the drip. The drip rate caps your long-run speed; the bucket size caps your burst. Two numbers, and you've described almost every rate limiter in production. Hold the picture: a bouncer, a dripping bucket, "take a token or come back later." That image is the token bucket algorithm — and the rest of today is just making one bouncer's bucket work across a thousand doors at once.

The Story

Marco shipped a public API for his startup's weather data, and for a week it was beautiful. Then one morning the dashboards went red: the database was pinned at 100% CPU, legitimate customers were timing out, and the pager wouldn't stop. The culprit wasn't an attack — it was one customer whose buggy script had gotten stuck in a tight loop, firing thousands of requests per second at an endpoint meant to be called a few times a minute. One misbehaving caller was starving everyone else. Marco needed a way to say "you've had enough — slow down," per customer, without touching the application code on every endpoint.

His first fix was the obvious one: a counter. For each API key, keep a number in a table — "requests this minute" — increment it on every call, and reject anything over 1,000. He reset the counters at the top of each minute. It worked in testing and then misbehaved in two ways he didn't expect. First, a customer complained they were being throttled even though they'd made well under their limit — they'd sent a normal burst at 10:00:59 and another at 10:01:01, and because his counter reset exactly on the minute, the two bursts landed in different windows and together sailed through at double the intended rate, hammering the DB again at the boundary. His fixed window had a seam, and traffic poured through it.

Second, and worse, once he scaled the API behind two servers for redundancy, the throttling went haywire. Each server kept its own counter in memory, so a customer hitting both servers effectively got twice their limit — and when he moved the counter into a shared database, two requests arriving at the same millisecond both read "999," both incremented, and both were allowed, pushing the real count to 1,001 and beyond. A classic race condition (Day 33's cousin): read-modify-write without atomicity. He fixed it by letting the datastore do the increment atomically — Redis, a single INCR that returns the new value in one indivisible step — and for the smoother algorithms, a tiny Lua script so the check-and-decrement happened as one operation the datastore couldn't interleave.

Finally he swapped the leaky fixed window for a token bucket: a steady refill rate set the sustained limit, and the bucket size let honest customers burst a little without getting punished. He moved the whole check out of the app and into the API gateway at the edge, so abusive traffic got a cheap 429 before it ever touched a database connection — and he returned a Retry-After header so well-behaved clients knew exactly when to try again. The database CPU graph went flat and boring. Marco had, step by step, rebuilt exactly the rate limiter that sits in front of Stripe, GitHub, and Cloudflare.

What should stay in your head
A rate limiter answers one question — "should this request be allowed, or rejected with 429?" — by counting requests per caller per time window against a configured limit. Pick an algorithm for how you count: the token bucket (a bucket refilled at a steady rate; each request spends a token; empty → reject) is the default because two numbers — refill rate = sustained limit and bucket size = burst allowance — capture what you actually want. Fixed window is the simplest (one counter per clock-aligned window) but leaks up to 2× the limit at the window boundary; the sliding window (log or weighted counter) fixes that by always looking back a full window from now. Pick a place: run the limiter at the edge — in an API gateway or middleware — so rejected traffic costs almost nothing and never reaches your app or database. And pick a store: across many limiter nodes the count must be shared (usually Redis) and updated atomically (an INCR or a small Lua script) or a race condition silently lets extra traffic through. Everything else is tuning: identify the caller (API key / user / IP), choose per-endpoint limits, and return an honest 429 with Retry-After and X-RateLimit-* headers. One-line test: a rate limiter is a per-caller counter with an algorithm, placed at the edge, backed by a shared atomic store.

On the boundary burst, the fixed window lets a batch through at 9s (window 1) and another at 11s (window 2) — both legal in their own window, but together that's up to 10 requests inside a 2-second span, double the intended limit. The sliding window, looking back a full 10s from the second batch, still sees the first and rejects the overflow. That seam is the single most important reason to prefer a rolling window (or a token bucket) over a naïve fixed counter.

Why This Problem Exists

Every service has a finite capacity — a database handles so many queries per second, a server holds so many connections, a downstream API costs real money per call. But the callers on the other side of your API have no idea about your limits and no reason to be gentle. Some are buggy (a retry loop gone wild), some are greedy (scraping as fast as they can), some are malicious (a brute-force or credential-stuffing attack), and some are simply too popular (a viral moment sending a flood). Without a limiter, a single bad actor can consume all your capacity and take the service down for everyone — exactly Marco's red morning. A rate limiter is the component that enforces fairness and protects capacity: it caps how fast each caller may hit you, so no one caller can ruin it for the rest.

It solves four distinct problems at once. Protection: shield fragile backends and databases from overload. Fairness: make sure one noisy neighbor can't starve everyone else of a shared resource. Cost control: if every API call triggers an expensive operation (an LLM inference, an SMS, a third-party charge), a limiter caps your bill. Security: throttling logins and password resets blunts brute-force and enumeration attacks. The core logic is trivial — count and compare — but, exactly like the crawler, the difficulty is in the details: counting accurately without a race, counting cheaply at millions of requests per second, counting consistently across a fleet of machines, and counting in a way that forgives honest bursts while stopping abuse.

The entire rate limiter, in pseudocode — everything else is choosing the count() and sharing it
def handle(request): key = identify(request) # who is this? API key / user id / IP limit = policy_for(key, request.route) # e.g. 1000 req / 60 s for this endpoint allowed = rate_limiter.check(key, limit) # the one hard part — count, atomically, maybe shared if not allowed: return 429, headers={ # Too Many Requests "Retry-After": seconds_until_token, "X-RateLimit-Limit": limit, "X-RateLimit-Remaining": 0 } return forward_to_service(request) # under the limit → do the real work

The Design, Step by Step

Same discipline as the last four designs: scope it, size it, sketch the interface, then draw the boxes. The "data" here is just a counter per caller; the real design effort is the algorithm, the placement, and the shared, atomic store.

Step 1 — Scope the requirements

✅ Functional

Cap requests per caller (API key / user / IP) per time window.

Return 429 with Retry-After when over the limit.

Support different limits per endpoint / tier (free vs paid).

🎯 Non-functional

Fast & cheap — adds <1 ms; must not become the bottleneck.

Accurate — don't allow 2× the limit; don't wrongly block.

Highly available — if the limiter is down, don't take the API down with it.

🤔 Clarify early

Limit by what? (user, IP, key — IP is shared behind NAT.)

Hard reject, or queue/delay (throttle) the request?

How exact must it be — is a little over/undercount OK for speed?

Step 2 — Size it (back-of-the-envelope, Day 30)

Suppose we front an API doing 1,000,000 requests/second at peak, limiting per user, with tens of millions of active users.

# the limiter runs on EVERY request — so its own throughput is the API's throughput checks / sec = 1,000,000 # must be cheaper than the work it protects # state per caller: a tiny counter + a timestamp per-key state ≈ 2 numbers ≈ ~20 bytes # token-bucket: tokens + last-refill time 10M active keys × 20 B = ~200 MB # trivially fits in one Redis node's RAM # a single Redis node handles ~100k+ ops/sec; 1M/sec needs sharding shards ≈ 1,000,000 / 100,000 = ~10 Redis shards # shard by caller key — each key lives on one shard

The sizing reveals the shape: the state is tiny (a counter is bytes), so memory is never the problem — throughput and latency are. The limiter runs on the hot path of every request, so it has to be dramatically cheaper than the work it guards, which is why the count lives in fast in-memory storage (Redis / local memory), never a slow relational DB, and why the whole check must be a single round-trip.

Step 3 — The core interface

allow(key, cost=1) → { allowed: bool, remaining: int, retry_after: seconds } # key = the caller's identity (API key, user id, or IP) # cost = how many tokens this request spends (a heavy call can cost more) # returns the verdict AND the metadata for the 429 / rate-limit headers

One method. Notice cost: not every request is equal — a bulk endpoint might spend 10 tokens while a health check spends 0. The limiter returns not just yes/no but the remaining allowance and when to retry, which become the X-RateLimit-Remaining and Retry-After headers that let good clients self-pace instead of hammering blindly.

Step 4 — Draw the architecture

The limiter sits at the edge, in front of the app servers, and consults a shared counter store so the limit is global no matter which gateway node a request lands on. Rejected traffic turns around at the door, costing almost nothing.

1
Request hits the edge. It arrives at an API gateway (or a limiter middleware / sidecar) before any application logic or database connection is touched.
2
Identify the caller. Extract the key the limit applies to — the API key, the authenticated user id, or (as a fallback) the client IP — plus the route, to pick the right policy.
3
Check & update the count — atomically. Ask the shared store (Redis) "does this key have allowance left?" The read-and-decrement must be a single atomic step (INCR or a Lua script) so two concurrent requests can't both slip through.
4
Allow or reject. Under the limit → forward to the service. Over it → return 429 immediately with Retry-After and X-RateLimit-* headers. The expensive backend never even sees the rejected request.
5
Fail open, not closed. If the counter store is briefly unreachable, decide deliberately: usually allow the request (fail open) so a limiter outage doesn't become an API outage — availability over perfect enforcement.

The Heart: The Counting Algorithms

Every rate limiter is one of a small family of counting tricks. They all answer "allowed?", but they trade accuracy, memory, and burst behavior differently. Know these five and you can pick the right one on sight.

Fixed windowOne counter per clock-aligned window (e.g. per minute); increment, reject over the limit, reset on the boundary.Leaks up to 2× the limit at the boundary (the seam Marco hit).Dead simple, 1 counter. Inaccurate at the edges; prone to synchronized bursts.

Why token bucket is the usual default

Token bucket wins because its two knobs map directly onto what product and infra teams actually want to say: "sustained, you may do X per second (the refill rate), but we'll forgive a short spike of up to B requests (the bucket size)." Real traffic is bursty — a user loads a page and fires ten API calls in a blink, then goes quiet. A pure smooth limiter would reject nine of those ten; a token bucket with a sensible burst size lets the natural flurry through and only clamps down on sustained abuse. It's also cheap: two numbers per key (tokens and last_refill_time), and the refill is computed lazily on each request — tokens = min(size, tokens + rate × elapsed) — so there's no background timer ticking for millions of keys.

Why the fixed-window seam matters — and the two fixes

The fixed window's flaw isn't academic: it's the exact bug that pages you. Because every counter resets on the same clock boundary, a caller can place a full window's worth of requests just before the reset and another full window's worth just after, delivering up to double the limit in a tiny span — and if many callers are aligned to the same boundary (everyone retries at the top of the minute), they spike together. The sliding window log fixes it with perfect accuracy by remembering each request's timestamp and always counting the trailing window from now — but that's a lot of memory under load. The sliding window counter is the pragmatic middle: keep just the current and previous window counts and estimate the rolling total as prev × (overlap fraction) + current. Two numbers, no seam, near-perfect — which is why it's a favorite in production.

✔ Default to a token bucket (or sliding-window counter); keep the state tiny; refill lazily — two numbers per caller, computed on read, gives you a sustained limit and a forgiving burst with almost no memory and no background timers.
✘ Don't ship a naïve fixed-window counter for anything that matters — its boundary seam lets ~2× your limit through and invites synchronized bursts. If you must use windows, use the sliding variant.

Distributed Rate Limiting — One Limit Across Many Machines

On a single server the limiter is easy: keep the counters in local memory. The moment you run more than one limiter node — and you always will, for redundancy and scale — the question becomes: whose count is the truth? If each node counts independently, a caller spread across N nodes gets roughly N× their limit. There are three ways out, and a race condition waiting in two of them.

Option A — A shared store (the common answer)

Put the counters in a fast, central store that every limiter node reads and writes — almost always Redis, because it's in-memory (sub-millisecond) and has atomic operations built in. Every node consults the same key, so the limit is global. The cost is one network round-trip per request (fast, but not free) and a dependency on Redis being up. This is what most teams reach for first, and it scales by sharding the keys across several Redis nodes — since each caller's key lives on exactly one shard, there's no cross-shard coordination.

The race condition — and why the fix is atomicity, not locks
# BROKEN — read-modify-write, two requests interleave n = redis.get(key) # both read "999" if n < limit: redis.set(key, n + 1) # both write 1000 → real count is 1001, both allowed ✗ # CORRECT — one atomic operation the store cannot interleave n = redis.incr(key) # returns the NEW value atomically: 1000, then 1001 if n == 1: redis.expire(key, window) if n > limit: return 429 # only one request can be the one that crosses the line ✓ # token bucket needs read + compute + write as one step → a tiny Lua script, # which Redis runs atomically start-to-finish: allowed = redis.eval(token_bucket_lua, key, rate, size, now, cost)

The lesson from Marco's bug: you cannot do "read the count, decide, write the new count" as three separate steps under concurrency — two requests interleave and both win. The fix is to make the whole check-and-update a single atomic operation. For simple counters that's INCR; for token bucket (which must read, lazily refill, compare, and decrement) that's a small Lua script Redis executes as one indivisible unit. Atomicity, not a separate lock — locks would add latency and a new failure mode.

Option B — Local counters, no coordination (fast but approximate)

Skip the shared store: let each node enforce its share of the limit (limit ÷ N nodes) using local memory only. Blazing fast, no Redis dependency, perfectly available — but only approximate, because traffic is never evenly spread across nodes, so the global limit drifts. Good for coarse protection where exactness doesn't matter.

Option C — Local + periodic sync (the hybrid)

The production compromise large systems (and Cloudflare's famous design) use: enforce locally for speed, but have nodes gossip or periodically broadcast their counts so each node has an approximate global view, tightened every few seconds. You get the latency of local counting with accuracy close to a shared store — at the cost of more moving parts. It's the same "local decision, eventual global agreement" trade you've seen in gossip (Day 37) and eventual consistency (Day 10).

✔ Shared Redis with atomic INCR/Lua is the right default for an accurate global limit; shard by caller key to scale its throughput.
✘ Don't let each node count independently if you need an exact global limit — N nodes silently become N× the limit. If you choose local counting for speed, do it knowingly, and sync periodically (Option C) when accuracy starts to matter.

The Hard Parts

The counting is easy. These four edges are what separate a toy limiter from one that survives production traffic.

Accuracy vs. cost vs. memory

The central tension: a sliding window log is perfectly accurate but stores an entry per request; a fixed window is one counter but leaks at the seam; token bucket and sliding window counter sit in the sweet spot — tiny state, near-perfect behavior. There's no universally "best" algorithm; you choose based on whether you're protecting a fragile downstream that needs a smooth rate (leaky bucket), forgiving honest bursts (token bucket), or just need cheap coarse protection (fixed window). Match the algorithm to what the thing you're protecting actually needs.

What do you limit by?

Choosing the key is subtle. Limit by user id and anonymous traffic slips through. Limit by IP and you accidentally throttle an entire office or university behind one NAT gateway as if it were a single user — while an attacker rotates through thousands of cheap IPs to dodge you. Real systems layer limits: a generous per-IP limit as a coarse net, a tighter per-API-key limit for authenticated callers, and sometimes a global limit protecting the service as a whole. There's no single right key — you pick based on who you're defending against.

Availability — fail open or fail closed?

The limiter is on the hot path of every request, so its failure modes become your failure modes. If Redis is unreachable, do you reject everything (fail closed — safe for the backend, but you've just taken your whole API down over a cache blip) or allow everything (fail open — your API stays up, but briefly unprotected)? Most choose fail open: a rate limiter exists to protect against overload, and turning a limiter outage into a full outage is a cure worse than the disease. But a security-critical limiter (login throttling) might deliberately fail closed. It's a conscious tradeoff, not a default.

Honest clients deserve honest signals

A good limiter doesn't just slam the door — it tells the caller what happened and when to come back. Return 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header (seconds until there's capacity) and the X-RateLimit-Limit / X-RateLimit-Remaining / X-RateLimit-Reset headers so well-behaved clients can pace themselves before they get blocked. The difference between a limiter that returns these and one that doesn't is the difference between clients that back off gracefully and clients that retry-storm you into a worse outage.

The through-line: count → compare → allow/deny is trivial; doing it accurately, cheaply, globally, and gracefully is the craft. Pick the algorithm that matches the downstream, keep the state tiny, make the update atomic, share it across the fleet, choose your key deliberately, decide fail-open vs fail-closed on purpose, and always return honest 429 signals. Get those right and a two-line counter becomes the bouncer in front of every great API.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

The misconceptionThe reality
"A rate limiter is just a counter — any algorithm will do."The algorithm is the design. A naïve fixed window leaks ~2× at the boundary; token bucket forgives bursts; leaky bucket smooths output. Pick to match what you're protecting.
"Just read the count, check it, then write count + 1."That read-modify-write races under concurrency — two requests both read 999 and both pass. Use an atomic op: INCR, or a Lua script for token bucket.
"Keep the counter in memory on each server."With N servers each caller gets N× the limit. For a global limit you need a shared store (Redis) — or local counters with periodic sync, chosen knowingly.
"Rate-limit by IP address."One IP can be a whole office behind NAT (you throttle innocents) while attackers rotate thousands of IPs. Prefer per-API-key / per-user; use IP only as a coarse fallback.
"If the limiter's store is down, block everything to be safe."That turns a cache blip into a full API outage. Usually fail open (allow) — the limiter protects against overload, it shouldn't cause one. Security limiters may fail closed on purpose.
"Just return a 429 and move on."Add Retry-After and X-RateLimit-* headers. Without them, blocked clients retry-storm you; with them, they back off gracefully.

Active Recall

❓ You're putting a rate limiter in front of a public API. (1) Which algorithm would you reach for by default, and what do its two numbers mean? (2) A customer complains they got throttled despite staying under their per-minute limit — what bug did they likely hit, and how do you fix it? (3) You scale the limiter to 5 nodes and callers start getting 5× their limit — what went wrong and what are your options? (click to reveal)
(1) A token bucket. Its two knobs are the refill rate (tokens added per second = your sustained limit) and the bucket capacity (max tokens = your burst allowance). Each request spends a token; an empty bucket returns 429. It's the default because it forgives honest short bursts while capping the long-run rate, with just two numbers of state.

(2) The fixed-window boundary seam. A counter that resets on the clock lets a caller send a full window at :59 and another at :01 — two different windows, but up to 2× the limit inside a 2-second span. (3) Each node counted independently in local memory, so N nodes = N× the limit. Real-World Engineering

💳 Stripe

Stripe's API is a textbook public example: it uses a token-bucket style limiter and returns 429 with clear docs on backing off. It even runs load shedders alongside rate limiters — rejecting low-priority traffic first under stress — and publishes its design as guidance for API builders.

☁️ Cloudflare

Operating at the edge across hundreds of data centers, Cloudflare can't round-trip to a central store on every request. Its rate limiting enforces locally and syncs counts between nodes (Option C) — the gossip-style "local decision, eventual global agreement" trade — to approximate a global limit at edge latency.

🐙 GitHub API

A crisp example of the response contract: GitHub returns X-RateLimit-Limit, X-RateLimit-Remaining, and X-RateLimit-Reset on every response, so clients know exactly how much budget they have left and when it refills — the headers this lesson keeps insisting on, in the wild.

🔧 Nginx & Envoy

You rarely build the limiter yourself. Nginx's limit_req implements a leaky bucket in the web server; Envoy (the proxy behind many service meshes) offers a global rate-limit service backed by Redis. Reaching for these at the gateway is the default move — the algorithm is a config, not code.

Flashcards (Spaced Repetition)

Q: In one sentence, what is a rate limiter?
A: A component that caps how many requests each caller may make per time window — counting per caller against a limit and returning 429 Too Many Requests when exceeded — placed at the edge and backed by a shared, atomic store.
Q: How does the token bucket algorithm work, and what do its two numbers control?
A: A bucket refills with tokens at a steady rate (= sustained limit) up to a capacity (= burst allowance); each request spends a token, and an empty bucket returns 429. Big capacity forgives spikes; the rate caps the long-run throughput. State is just tokens + last_refill_time, refilled lazily on read.
Q: What's the fixed-window boundary problem, and how do you fix it?
A: A counter that resets on the clock lets a caller send a full window just before the reset and another just after — up to 2× the limit in a tiny span. Fix with a sliding window: a log (exact, pricey) or a weighted counter (prev × overlap + current, cheap and near-exact) that always counts the trailing window from now.
Q: Why does a distributed rate limiter need an atomic operation, not just a shared counter?
A: "Read count → check → write count+1" as three steps races: two concurrent requests both read 999 and both pass. The whole check-and-update must be one indivisible step — Redis INCR for simple counters, a Lua script for token bucket — so only one request can be the one that crosses the limit.
Q: Should a rate limiter fail open or fail closed if its store is unavailable?
A: Usually fail open (allow requests): the limiter exists to prevent overload, so letting its outage cause a full API outage is worse than being briefly unprotected. Exception: security-critical limits (login throttling) may deliberately fail closed. It's a conscious tradeoff.

Memory Test — Answer Without Looking Up

  • Explain what a rate limiter does and the one question it answers, in a single sentence.
  • Describe the token bucket with the bouncer image, and say what the refill rate and bucket size each control.
  • Walk through the fixed-window boundary burst: why does it let ~2× the limit through, and how does a sliding window stop it?
  • Name the five algorithms and give the one-line tradeoff (burst behavior / cost) for each.
  • Why must the count update be atomic across a fleet, and what two Redis tools give you that for a simple counter vs a token bucket?
  • Give the three ways to keep a global limit across many nodes (shared store, local-only, local+sync) and their tradeoffs.
  • Where should the limiter sit and why, and what should a 429 response include so good clients behave?
If you only remember one thing: a rate limiter is a per-caller counter with an algorithm, placed at the edge, backed by a shared atomic store. Default to a token bucket — refill rate sets the sustained limit, bucket size forgives honest bursts; avoid the naïve fixed window, whose boundary seam leaks ~2× the limit, in favor of a sliding window when accuracy matters. Run the check in the API gateway so rejected traffic dies cheaply before touching your database, keep the per-caller state tiny and refill it lazily, and make every check-and-update a single atomic step (INCR or a Lua script in Redis) so concurrency can't leak extra traffic. Choose your key deliberately (API key > IP), decide fail-open vs fail-closed on purpose, and return an honest 429 with Retry-After so clients back off instead of storming you.
